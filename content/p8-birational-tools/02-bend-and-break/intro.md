# Bend-and-break

A morphism from a curve that moves while one of its points stays fixed cannot move freely: the family has to degenerate, and the limit contains a rational curve. This chapter proves that principle, bend-and-break, and uses it with the dimension estimate of Chapter 1 to prove Mori's theorem that a smooth projective variety whose canonical class is not nef contains a rational curve on which the canonical class is negative and of degree at most $n + 1$.

Section 2.1 proves the rigidity lemma, which says that a family of morphisms from a proper connected scheme that contracts one member contracts every member, and deduces the two bend-and-break lemmas. In the first, a curve $f \colon C \to X$ that deforms with a point fixed is numerically equivalent to another image of $C$ plus a nonzero sum of rational curves through the fixed point. In the second, a rational curve that deforms in a family of dimension at least $2$ with two points fixed is numerically equivalent to a sum of at least two rational curves. Both proofs resolve the indeterminacy of a rational map from a surface, by the elimination theorem of P5, and read off the limit cycle from the fibres of the blown-up surface. Conics in the plane degenerating to pairs of lines are the model case.

Section 2.2 proves Mori's theorem. In characteristic $p$, composing a curve with the Frobenius morphism of its source multiplies the degree of $f^* T_X$ without changing the genus, so the dimension estimate eventually forces the curve to deform with many points fixed, and bend-and-break produces rational curves of bounded degree. Spreading out to finitely generated rings transfers the bound to characteristic $0$, because the morphisms of bounded degree form a scheme of finite type. A last application of bend-and-break with two fixed points brings $-K_X \cdot C$ down to at most $n + 1$, and lines in $\mathbb{P}^n$ show that the bound is sharp.

The chapter follows Kollár and Mori, Section 1.2 (KM98), Debarre, Chapter 3 (Deb01), and Kollár, Chapter II (Kol96), and it reproduces the original argument of Mori (Mor79, Mor82).
