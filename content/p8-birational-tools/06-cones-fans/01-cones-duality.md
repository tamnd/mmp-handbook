## Rational polyhedral cones, duality and Gordan's lemma

### Statement

Throughout Part C, $N$ is a lattice, that is, a free abelian group of rank $n$, and $M = \operatorname{Hom}_\mathbb{Z}(N, \mathbb{Z})$ is the dual lattice, with the pairing $\langle \, , \rangle \colon M \times N \to \mathbb{Z}$. Write $N_\mathbb{R} = N \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{R}$ and $M_\mathbb{R} = M \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{R}$, real vector spaces of dimension $n$ dual to each other, with the Euclidean topology. For $m \in M_\mathbb{R}$, write $m^\perp = \{ u \in N_\mathbb{R} : \langle m, u \rangle = 0 \}$, and similarly $u^\perp \subseteq M_\mathbb{R}$ for $u \in N_\mathbb{R}$, and $A^\perp$ for the set of elements that pair to $0$ with every element of a set $A$.

**Definition P8.6.1 (cones).** Let $S \subseteq N_\mathbb{R}$ be a finite set.

1. The *cone generated by $S$* is $\operatorname{Cone}(S) = \{ \sum_{u \in S} \lambda_u u : \lambda_u \geq 0 \}$, with $\operatorname{Cone}(\emptyset) = \{0\}$. A *convex polyhedral cone* is a subset $\sigma \subseteq N_\mathbb{R}$ of this form. It is *rational* if $S$ can be chosen in $N$. Its *dimension* $\dim \sigma$ is the dimension of the linear span of $\sigma$.
2. The *dual cone* of $\sigma$ is $\sigma^\vee = \{ m \in M_\mathbb{R} : \langle m, u \rangle \geq 0 \text{ for all } u \in \sigma \}$.
3. A *face* of $\sigma$ is a subset $\tau = \sigma \cap m^\perp$ with $m \in \sigma^\vee$, written $\tau \preceq \sigma$. Taking $m = 0$ shows that $\sigma$ is a face of itself; the other faces are *proper*. A *facet* is a face of dimension $\dim \sigma - 1$.
4. The cone $\sigma$ is *strongly convex* if $\sigma \cap (-\sigma) = \{0\}$, and *full-dimensional* if $\dim \sigma = n$.
5. A *ray* is a strongly convex rational polyhedral cone $\rho$ of dimension $1$. The group $N \cap \mathbb{R}\rho$ is infinite cyclic, so $\rho \cap N = \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} u_\rho$ for a unique primitive vector $u_\rho \in N$, the *minimal generator* of $\rho$.
6. A *ray of* a cone $\sigma$ is a face of $\sigma$ that is a ray. A strongly convex rational polyhedral cone is *simplicial* if the minimal generators of its rays are linearly independent over $\mathbb{R}$, and *smooth* if they are part of a $\mathbb{Z}$-basis of $N$.
7. For a rational polyhedral cone $\sigma$, its *semigroup* is $S_\sigma = \sigma^\vee \cap M$, a submonoid of $M$.

The same definitions apply to cones in $M_\mathbb{R}$, with the roles of $N$ and $M$ exchanged, and Lemma P8.6.2 is stated for an arbitrary finite-dimensional real vector space. The proofs use a Euclidean inner product on $N_\mathbb{R}$, which identifies $M_\mathbb{R}$ with $N_\mathbb{R}$; none of the statements depend on it.

**Lemma P8.6.2 (cones cut out by half-spaces).** Let $V$ be a real vector space of finite dimension, let $\ell_1, \dots, \ell_r$ be linear forms on $V$, and let $C = \{ v \in V : \ell_i(v) \geq 0 \text{ for all } i \}$.

1. $C \cap (-C) = L$ is the subspace $\{ \ell_1 = \dots = \ell_r = 0 \}$, and $C = L + \operatorname{Cone}(v_1, \dots, v_s)$ for finitely many vectors $v_j$. In particular $C$ is a convex polyhedral cone.
2. If $L = 0$, the $v_j$ can be chosen so that each half-line $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} v_j$ is a face of $C$, of the form $C \cap \{ \sum_{i \in I} \ell_i = 0 \}$ for a set $I$ of indices.
3. If $V = W \otimes_\mathbb{Z} \mathbb{R}$ for a lattice $W$ and the $\ell_i$ take integer values on $W$, then $L$ has a basis in $W$ and the $v_j$ can be chosen in $W$.

**Theorem P8.6.3 (duality and the theorem of Weyl and Minkowski).** Let $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(S) \subseteq N_\mathbb{R}$ be a convex polyhedral cone.

1. (Carathéodory) Every element of $\sigma$ lies in $\operatorname{Cone}(T)$ for a linearly independent subset $T \subseteq S$. The cone $\sigma$ is closed.
2. $\sigma^\vee$ is a convex polyhedral cone in $M_\mathbb{R}$, rational if $\sigma$ is.
3. $(\sigma^\vee)^\vee = \sigma$. If $\sigma$ is rational and $u \in N_\mathbb{R} \setminus \sigma$, there is $m \in \sigma^\vee \cap M$ with $\langle m, u \rangle < 0$.
4. A subset of $N_\mathbb{R}$ is a convex polyhedral cone if and only if it is an intersection of finitely many closed half-spaces $\{ \langle m_j, - \rangle \geq 0 \}$, and it is a rational polyhedral cone if and only if the $m_j$ can be chosen in $M$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Proposition P8.6.4 (faces and duality).** Let $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(S) \subseteq N_\mathbb{R}$ be a convex polyhedral cone.

1. A face $\tau = \sigma \cap m^\perp$ equals $\operatorname{Cone}(S \cap m^\perp)$. So $\sigma$ has finitely many faces, and each is a convex polyhedral cone, rational if $\sigma$ is.
2. The intersection of two faces of $\sigma$ is a face of $\sigma$, and a face of a face of $\sigma$ is a face of $\sigma$.
3. The span of $\sigma^\vee$ is $(\sigma \cap (-\sigma))^\perp$. In particular $\sigma$ is strongly convex if and only if $\sigma^\vee$ is full-dimensional, and $\sigma$ is full-dimensional if and only if $\sigma^\vee$ is strongly convex.
4. For a face $\tau \preceq \sigma$, the set $\tau^* = \sigma^\vee \cap \tau^\perp$ is a face of $\sigma^\vee$, and $\tau \mapsto \tau^*$ is an inclusion-reversing bijection from the faces of $\sigma$ to the faces of $\sigma^\vee$, with inverse $\eta \mapsto \sigma \cap \eta^\perp$. Also $\dim \tau + \dim \tau^* = n$.
5. If $\sigma$ is strongly convex, then $\{0\}$ is a face of $\sigma$, every face is strongly convex, and $\sigma$ is generated by any choice of one nonzero vector on each of its faces of dimension $1$. These faces of dimension $1$ are among the half-lines $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} u$ with $u \in S$. If $\sigma$ is also rational, its faces of dimension $1$ are rays, $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(u_\rho : \rho \preceq \sigma \text{ a ray})$, and every face of a simplicial or smooth cone is simplicial or smooth.

**Theorem P8.6.5 (Gordan's lemma).** Let $\sigma \subseteq N_\mathbb{R}$ be a rational polyhedral cone. Then $S_\sigma = \sigma^\vee \cap M$ is a finitely generated monoid. If $\sigma$ is strongly convex, then $S_\sigma$ generates $M$ as a group, that is, $M = S_\sigma - S_\sigma$.

**Proposition P8.6.6 (the Hilbert basis).** Let $\sigma \subseteq N_\mathbb{R}$ be a full-dimensional rational polyhedral cone. Call $m \in S_\sigma \setminus \{0\}$ *irreducible* if it is not a sum of two nonzero elements of $S_\sigma$. The set $\mathcal{H}$ of irreducible elements is finite, it generates $S_\sigma$, and it is contained in every generating set of $S_\sigma$. It is the *Hilbert basis* of $S_\sigma$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Lemma P8.6.7 (the separation lemma).** Let $\sigma, \sigma' \subseteq N_\mathbb{R}$ be rational polyhedral cones such that $\tau = \sigma \cap \sigma'$ is a face of both. Then there is $m \in \sigma^\vee \cap (-\sigma')^\vee \cap M$ with
$$\tau = \sigma \cap m^\perp = \sigma' \cap m^\perp.$$

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

### Proof in words

Carathéodory's argument removes generators one at a time until they are linearly independent, so a polyhedral cone is a finite union of images of closed orthants and is closed. A closed convex cone is separated from a point outside it by the nearest-point argument, which gives $(\sigma^\vee)^\vee = \sigma$. The finiteness of $\sigma^\vee$ is the harder direction of Weyl and Minkowski: a cone cut out by finitely many inequalities is generated by finitely many vectors. After splitting off the subspace where all inequalities are equalities, the cone contains no line, and a point that is not on an extremal half-line can be moved along a line inside the cone in both directions until one more inequality becomes an equality. Repeating this writes every point as a combination of points where the equalities cut out a line, and there are only finitely many such lines. Faces are dual to faces, because a face of $\sigma$ is the set where a functional of $\sigma^\vee$ vanishes, and the dimension count comes from the fact that $\tau^*$ is the dual of the cone $\sigma - \tau$, whose largest subspace is the span of $\tau$. Gordan's lemma is the observation that the lattice points of $\sigma^\vee$ are sums of the generators and of the finitely many lattice points of a half-open parallelepiped. The separation lemma applies the face construction to the cone $\sigma - \sigma'$.

### Examples

**Example P8.6.8 (smooth cones).** Let $e_1, \dots, e_n$ be a basis of $N$ with dual basis $e_1^*, \dots, e_n^*$ of $M$, and let $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_1, \dots, e_r)$. Then $m = \sum a_i e_i^*$ lies in $\sigma^\vee$ exactly when $a_1, \dots, a_r \geq 0$, so
$$\sigma^\vee = \operatorname{Cone}(e_1^*, \dots, e_r^*, \pm e_{r+1}^*, \dots, \pm e_n^*), \qquad S_\sigma = \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^r \times \mathbb{Z}^{n - r}.$$
The faces of $\sigma$ are the $2^r$ cones $\operatorname{Cone}(e_i : i \in I)$ for $I \subseteq \{1, \dots, r\}$, since $\sigma \cap m^\perp$ for $m = \sum_{i \notin I, i \leq r} e_i^*$ is this cone, and $\tau^* = \sigma^\vee \cap \tau^\perp$ is generated by $e_i^*$ for $i \leq r$, $i \notin I$, and $\pm e_j^*$ for $j > r$, of dimension $n - |I|$. Every smooth cone has this form after a change of basis. For $r = n$, $\sigma$ is the positive orthant and $S_\sigma = \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^n$, with Hilbert basis $e_1^*, \dots, e_n^*$.

**Example P8.6.9 (the cones of the cyclic quotient singularities).** Let $N = \mathbb{Z}^2$ with basis $e_1, e_2$, let $n \geq 1$ and $0 \leq q < n$ be coprime integers, and let $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, n e_1 - q e_2)$. The vector $(n, -q)$ is primitive because $\gcd(n, q) = 1$, and $\det(e_2, n e_1 - q e_2) = -n$, so $\sigma$ is simplicial, and smooth only for $n = 1$. A form $m = (a, b)$ lies in $\sigma^\vee$ if and only if $b \geq 0$ and $na - qb \geq 0$, so
$$\sigma^\vee = \operatorname{Cone}(e_1^*, \, q e_1^* + n e_2^*).$$
For $q = 1$, write $\sigma_n = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, n e_1 - e_2)$. Then $S_{\sigma_n} = \{ (a, b) : 0 \leq b \leq na \}$, and its Hilbert basis is
$$\mathcal{H} = \{ (1, j) : 0 \leq j \leq n \}.$$
Indeed, each $(1, j)$ is irreducible, since a sum of two nonzero elements has first coordinate at least $2$ (an element with $a = 0$ has $b = 0$). Conversely, if $(a, b) \in S_{\sigma_n}$ with $a \geq 2$, let $j = \min(b, n)$; then $(a, b) = (1, j) + (a - 1, b - j)$, and $(a - 1, b - j) \in S_{\sigma_n}$, because $b - j = 0$ if $b \leq n$ and $b - n \leq n(a - 1)$ if $b > n$. By induction on $a$, the $(1, j)$ generate. So the Hilbert basis has $n + 1$ elements, while a smooth cone of dimension $2$ has a Hilbert basis with $2$ elements.

**Example P8.6.10 (the cone over a square).** Let $N = \mathbb{Z}^3$ and $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2, u_3, u_4)$ with
$$u_1 = (0, 0, 1), \quad u_2 = (1, 0, 1), \quad u_3 = (1, 1, 1), \quad u_4 = (0, 1, 1),$$
the cone over the unit square at height $1$. It is strongly convex, since $e_3^*$ is positive on each $u_i$, and it is not simplicial. The forms vanishing on two cyclically adjacent $u_i$ and positive on the others are $e_2^*$ (for $u_1, u_2$), $e_3^* - e_1^*$ (for $u_2, u_3$), $e_3^* - e_2^*$ (for $u_3, u_4$) and $e_1^*$ (for $u_4, u_1$). The four $u_i$ and the four facets $\operatorname{Cone}(u_i, u_{i+1})$, together with $\{0\}$ and $\sigma$, are the faces, and by Theorem P8.6.3 and Proposition P8.6.4,
$$\sigma^\vee = \operatorname{Cone}(e_1^*, \, e_2^*, \, e_3^* - e_1^*, \, e_3^* - e_2^*).$$
The four generators satisfy one relation, $e_1^* + (e_3^* - e_1^*) = e_3^* = e_2^* + (e_3^* - e_2^*)$. If $m = a e_1^* + b e_2^* + c (e_3^* - e_1^*) + d (e_3^* - e_2^*)$ with nonnegative coefficients, replacing $\min(b, d)$ copies of $e_2^* + (e_3^* - e_2^*)$ by $e_1^* + (e_3^* - e_1^*)$ puts $m$ into one of the cones $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1^*, e_2^*, e_3^* - e_1^*)$ or $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1^*, e_3^* - e_1^*, e_3^* - e_2^*)$. Both are generated by a basis of $M$, as their determinants are $1$, so a lattice point in either is a nonnegative integer combination of its generators. Hence the four generators of $\sigma^\vee$ form the Hilbert basis of $S_\sigma$.

**Example P8.6.11 (a cone that is not strongly convex).** Let $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_1, -e_1, e_2) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, the upper half-plane. Then $\sigma \cap (-\sigma) = \mathbb{R} e_1$, $\sigma^\vee = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2^*)$ is a half-line, and $S_\sigma = \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} e_2^*$ generates only $\mathbb{Z} e_2^* \neq M$, in accordance with Theorem P8.6.5 and part (3) of Proposition P8.6.4. The faces of $\sigma$ are $\sigma$ and the line $\mathbb{R} e_1$, and $\{0\}$ is not a face.

### Proof

*Lemma P8.6.2.* (1) Clearly $L \subseteq C \cap (-C)$, and if $v$ and $-v$ lie in $C$ then $\ell_i(v) \geq 0$ and $-\ell_i(v) \geq 0$, so $v \in L$. Choose a complementary subspace $V'$ with $V = L \oplus V'$, and let $C' = C \cap V'$. If $v = l + v'$ with $l \in L$ and $v' \in V'$, then $\ell_i(v') = \ell_i(v)$, so $v \in C$ if and only if $v' \in C'$, and $C = L + C'$. Also $C' \cap (-C') = L \cap V' = 0$. A basis of $L$ and its negative generate $L$ as a cone, so it suffices to show that $C'$ is generated by finitely many vectors.

For $v \in C'$, let $I(v) = \{ i : \ell_i(v) = 0 \}$ and $E(v) = \{ w \in V' : \ell_i(w) = 0 \text{ for } i \in I(v) \}$, a subspace containing $v$. Call $v \neq 0$ *extremal* if $\dim E(v) = 1$, so $E(v) = \mathbb{R} v$. The subspace $E(v)$ depends only on the set $I(v)$, so there are finitely many lines $E(v)$ with $v$ extremal, and on each such line $C'$ meets in one half-line, since $C'$ contains no line. Choose a nonzero vector on each of these half-lines; these are the $v_j$. It remains to show that every $v \in C' \setminus \{0\}$ is a nonnegative combination of extremal vectors, and this is proved by induction on $\dim E(v) \geq 1$.

If $\dim E(v) = 1$, $v$ is extremal. Let $\dim E(v) \geq 2$, and let $K = C' \cap E(v) = \{ w \in E(v) : \ell_i(w) \geq 0 \text{ for } i \notin I(v) \}$. Since $\ell_i(v) > 0$ for $i \notin I(v)$, $K$ contains a neighbourhood of $v$ in $E(v)$. The sets $K \setminus \{0\}$ and $(-K) \setminus \{0\}$ are closed in $E(v) \setminus \{0\}$ and disjoint, since $K \cap (-K) = 0$, and $E(v) \setminus \{0\}$ is connected because $\dim E(v) \geq 2$. So their union is not all of $E(v) \setminus \{0\}$, and there is $w \in E(v)$ with $w \notin K$ and $-w \notin K$. Let $t_+ = \sup \{ t \geq 0 : v + tw \in K \}$ and $t_- = \sup \{ t \geq 0 : v - tw \in K \}$. Both are positive because $K$ contains a neighbourhood of $v$, and both are finite: if $v + tw \in K$ for all $t \geq 0$, then $w = \lim_{t \to \infty} (v + tw)/t$ lies in the closed cone $K$. The points $v_\pm = v \pm t_\pm w$ lie in $K$, and $v$ is a positive combination of them: $v = \frac{t_-}{t_+ + t_-} v_+ + \frac{t_+}{t_+ + t_-} v_-$. Neither $v_+$ nor $v_-$ is $0$, since $v_+ = 0$ would give $-w = v/t_+ \in K$, and similarly for $v_-$. At $t = t_+$ some inequality $\ell_j \geq 0$ with $j \notin I(v)$ becomes an equality, so $I(v_+) \supsetneq I(v)$. As $\ell_j(v) > 0$ and $v \in E(v)$, the form $\ell_j$ does not vanish on $E(v)$, so $E(v_+) \subseteq E(v) \cap \ker \ell_j$ has smaller dimension. The same holds for $v_-$, and induction applies to $v_+$ and $v_-$.

(2) If $L = 0$ then $V' = V$ and $C' = C$. For an extremal $v_j$, let $\ell = \sum_{i \in I(v_j)} \ell_i$. Then $\ell \geq 0$ on $C$, and $C \cap \{\ell = 0\} = \{ w \in C : \ell_i(w) = 0, \, i \in I(v_j) \} = C \cap E(v_j) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} v_j$.

(3) If the $\ell_i$ are integral on $W$, then $L$ and each $E(v)$ are cut out by linear equations with rational coefficients in a basis of $W$, so they have bases in $W \otimes \mathbb{Q}$, and after clearing denominators in $W$. Choose $V'$ spanned by elements of $W$; the extremal lines $E(v)$ are then spanned by elements of $W$, and the $v_j$ can be taken in $W$.

*Theorem P8.6.3.* (1) Let $u = \sum_{s \in T} \lambda_s s$ with $\lambda_s > 0$ for $s$ in a subset $T \subseteq S$. If $T$ is linearly dependent, there is a relation $\sum_{s \in T} \mu_s s = 0$ with some $\mu_s > 0$. Let $t = \min \{ \lambda_s / \mu_s : \mu_s > 0 \}$. Then $u = \sum_{s \in T} (\lambda_s - t \mu_s) s$ has nonnegative coefficients, and at least one of them is $0$. Repeating this ends with a linearly independent $T$. So $\sigma$ is the union of the cones $\operatorname{Cone}(T)$ over the linearly independent subsets $T \subseteq S$. Each such cone is the image of the closed orthant $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^T$ under an injective linear map, hence closed, and a finite union of closed sets is closed.

(2) We have $\sigma^\vee = \{ m \in M_\mathbb{R} : \langle m, s \rangle \geq 0 \text{ for } s \in S \}$, because a form nonnegative on the generators is nonnegative on their nonnegative combinations. This is a set of the form of Lemma P8.6.2 in $V = M_\mathbb{R}$, with $\ell_s = \langle -, s \rangle$. So $\sigma^\vee$ is a convex polyhedral cone, and if $S \subseteq N$ the forms are integral on $M$ and $\sigma^\vee$ is rational by part (3) of the lemma.

(3) By definition $\sigma \subseteq (\sigma^\vee)^\vee$. Let $u \notin \sigma$. The cone $\sigma$ is closed and nonempty, so there is a point $p \in \sigma$ nearest to $u$ for the chosen inner product. For $z \in \sigma$ and $t \geq 0$ the points $p + tz$ and $tp$ lie in $\sigma$, and the derivative at $t = 0$ of the squared distance to $u$ gives $(p - u) \cdot z \geq 0$ and $(p - u) \cdot p = 0$. Let $m = p - u$, viewed as an element of $M_\mathbb{R}$. Then $m \in \sigma^\vee$ and $\langle m, u \rangle = (p - u) \cdot u = -|p - u|^2 < 0$, so $u \notin (\sigma^\vee)^\vee$. If $\sigma$ is rational, $\sigma^\vee = \operatorname{Cone}(m_1, \dots, m_s)$ with $m_j \in M$ by (2), and since $\sigma = \{ u : \langle m_j, u \rangle \geq 0 \text{ for all } j \}$, a point $u \notin \sigma$ has $\langle m_j, u \rangle < 0$ for some $j$.

(4) An intersection $\bigcap_j \{ \langle m_j, - \rangle \geq 0 \}$ is a convex polyhedral cone by Lemma P8.6.2 with $V = N_\mathbb{R}$, rational if the $m_j$ lie in $M$. Conversely, for a convex polyhedral cone $\sigma$, write $\sigma^\vee = \operatorname{Cone}(m_1, \dots, m_s)$ by (2); then $\sigma = (\sigma^\vee)^\vee = \bigcap_j \{ \langle m_j, - \rangle \geq 0 \}$ by (3), with $m_j \in M$ if $\sigma$ is rational.

*Proposition P8.6.4.* (1) Let $m \in \sigma^\vee$ and $u = \sum_{s \in S} \lambda_s s \in \sigma$ with $\lambda_s \geq 0$. Then $\langle m, u \rangle = \sum \lambda_s \langle m, s \rangle$ is a sum of nonnegative terms, and it is $0$ exactly when $\lambda_s = 0$ for every $s$ with $\langle m, s \rangle > 0$. So $\sigma \cap m^\perp = \operatorname{Cone}(S \cap m^\perp)$, and there are at most $2^{|S|}$ faces.

(2) If $\tau = \sigma \cap m^\perp$ and $\tau' = \sigma \cap m'^\perp$ with $m, m' \in \sigma^\vee$, then $m + m' \in \sigma^\vee$, and since both terms are nonnegative on $\sigma$, $\sigma \cap (m + m')^\perp = \tau \cap \tau'$. Let now $\tau = \sigma \cap m^\perp$ and $\eta = \tau \cap m'^\perp$ with $m' \in \tau^\vee$. Choose $c > 0$ so large that $\langle m' + cm, s \rangle > 0$ for every $s \in S$ with $\langle m, s \rangle > 0$, which is possible since $S$ is finite. For $s \in S \cap m^\perp$ we have $s \in \tau$ and $\langle m' + cm, s \rangle = \langle m', s \rangle \geq 0$. So $m' + cm \in \sigma^\vee$, and by (1), $\sigma \cap (m' + cm)^\perp$ is generated by the $s \in S$ with $\langle m, s \rangle = 0$ and $\langle m', s \rangle = 0$, which by (1) applied to $\tau = \operatorname{Cone}(S \cap m^\perp)$ is $\eta$.

(3) Let $\Lambda = \sigma \cap (-\sigma)$. Every $m \in \sigma^\vee$ is nonnegative on $\Lambda$ and on $-\Lambda$, so $\sigma^\vee \subseteq \Lambda^\perp$. Conversely, let $u$ pair to $0$ with all of $\sigma^\vee$. Then $u$ and $-u$ lie in $(\sigma^\vee)^\vee = \sigma$ (Theorem P8.6.3), so $u \in \Lambda$. Hence $(\operatorname{span} \sigma^\vee)^\perp \subseteq \Lambda$, and taking orthogonals, $\Lambda^\perp \subseteq \operatorname{span} \sigma^\vee$. For the last two claims apply this to $\sigma$, and to $\sigma^\vee$ in place of $\sigma$, using $(\sigma^\vee)^\vee = \sigma$.

(4) Let $\tau \preceq \sigma$, and let $u_\tau = \sum_{s \in S \cap \tau} s \in \tau$. By (1), $\tau = \operatorname{Cone}(S \cap \tau)$, so an $m \in \sigma^\vee$ vanishes on $\tau$ exactly when it vanishes on each $s \in S \cap \tau$, that is, when $\langle m, u_\tau \rangle = 0$. Hence $\tau^* = \sigma^\vee \cap u_\tau^\perp$, and $u_\tau \in \sigma = (\sigma^\vee)^\vee$, so $\tau^*$ is a face of $\sigma^\vee$. The map reverses inclusions. The same construction applied to the cone $\sigma^\vee$, whose dual is $\sigma$, sends a face $\eta \preceq \sigma^\vee$ to the face $\sigma \cap \eta^\perp$ of $\sigma$. If $\tau = \sigma \cap m^\perp$ with $m \in \sigma^\vee$, then $\tau \subseteq \sigma \cap (\tau^*)^\perp$, and $m \in \tau^*$, so $\sigma \cap (\tau^*)^\perp \subseteq \sigma \cap m^\perp = \tau$. Thus $\sigma \cap (\tau^*)^\perp = \tau$, and symmetrically $\sigma^\vee \cap (\sigma \cap \eta^\perp)^\perp = \eta$, which proves the bijection.

For the dimension, let $W = \operatorname{span} \tau$ and $\gamma = \sigma + (-\tau)$, a convex polyhedral cone. A form is nonnegative on $\gamma$ exactly when it is nonnegative on $\sigma$ and vanishes on $\tau$, so $\gamma^\vee = \tau^*$. By (3), $\dim \tau^* = n - \dim (\gamma \cap (-\gamma))$. We claim $\gamma \cap (-\gamma) = W$. The subspace $W = \tau - \tau$ is contained in $\gamma$, hence in $\gamma \cap (-\gamma)$. Conversely, write $\tau = \sigma \cap m^\perp$ with $m \in \sigma^\vee$. Then $m \geq 0$ on $\gamma$, so $m$ vanishes on $\gamma \cap (-\gamma)$. If $x = s - t$ lies there, with $s \in \sigma$ and $t \in \tau$, then $\langle m, s \rangle = \langle m, x \rangle = 0$, so $s \in \tau$ and $x \in W$. Hence $\dim \tau^* = n - \dim \tau$.

(5) Assume $\sigma$ strongly convex, and let $m_1, \dots, m_s$ generate $\sigma^\vee$ and $m = \sum m_j$. As in the proof of (3), $\sigma \cap m^\perp$ consists of the $u \in \sigma$ on which every element of $\sigma^\vee$ vanishes, so $\sigma \cap m^\perp \subseteq \sigma \cap (-\sigma) = \{0\}$, and $\{0\}$ is a face. A face is contained in $\sigma$, so it is strongly convex. By Theorem P8.6.3, $\sigma = \{ u : \langle m_j, u \rangle \geq 0 \}$, which is a cone of the form of Lemma P8.6.2 with $L = 0$. Part (2) of the lemma writes $\sigma$ as generated by vectors $v_j$ with $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} v_j$ a face of $\sigma$. A face of dimension $1$ is $\operatorname{Cone}(S \cap m'^\perp)$ by (1), and it is a half-line in a strongly convex cone, so it is $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} s$ for each nonzero $s \in S \cap m'^\perp$. Every face of dimension $1$ is among the $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} v_j$ up to repetition, since by (1) applied to $S = \{ v_j \}$ a face of dimension $1$ is generated by some $v_j$; so one nonzero vector on each face of dimension $1$ generates $\sigma$. If $\sigma$ is rational, a face of dimension $1$ is rational by (1) and strongly convex, so it is a ray, and its minimal generator lies on it. Finally, a face $\tau = \sigma \cap m'^\perp$ of a strongly convex rational cone is generated by the $u_\rho$ with $\langle m', u_\rho \rangle = 0$, by (1) applied to $S = \{ u_\rho \}$; its rays are among these by the previous sentences, so the minimal generators of the rays of $\tau$ are a subset of those of $\sigma$, and a subset of a linearly independent set or of part of a basis is again such a set.

*Theorem P8.6.5.* Write $\sigma^\vee = \operatorname{Cone}(m_1, \dots, m_s)$ with $m_j \in M$, by Theorem P8.6.3. The set $K = \{ \sum t_j m_j : 0 \leq t_j < 1 \}$ is bounded, so $K \cap M$ is finite. Let $m \in S_\sigma$, and write $m = \sum \lambda_j m_j$ with $\lambda_j \geq 0$. Then
$$m = \sum_j \lfloor \lambda_j \rfloor m_j + \sum_j (\lambda_j - \lfloor \lambda_j \rfloor) m_j,$$
and the second sum is $m - \sum \lfloor \lambda_j \rfloor m_j \in M$, and lies in $K$. So $S_\sigma$ is generated by the finite set $\{ m_1, \dots, m_s \} \cup (K \cap M)$, which lies in $S_\sigma$. If $\sigma$ is strongly convex, $\sigma^\vee$ is full-dimensional by Proposition P8.6.4, so it contains a ball around some point, and therefore a lattice point $m_0$ in its interior, after scaling by a positive integer. For $m \in M$, the point $m + c m_0 = c (m_0 + m/c)$ lies in $\sigma^\vee$ for $c$ a large positive integer, because $m_0 + m/c$ tends to the interior point $m_0$. So $m = (m + c m_0) - c m_0 \in S_\sigma - S_\sigma$.

*Proposition P8.6.6.* As $\sigma$ is full-dimensional, it contains a lattice point $u_0$ in its interior, as in the proof of Theorem P8.6.5. For $m \in \sigma^\vee \setminus \{0\}$, $\langle m, u_0 \rangle > 0$: otherwise $m$ is nonnegative on a neighbourhood of $u_0$ and vanishes at $u_0$, so it vanishes on that neighbourhood and $m = 0$. So $h(m) = \langle m, u_0 \rangle$ is a positive integer on $S_\sigma \setminus \{0\}$ and is additive. By induction on $h(m)$, every nonzero $m \in S_\sigma$ is a sum of irreducible elements: if $m$ is not irreducible, $m = m' + m''$ with $h(m'), h(m'') < h(m)$. So $\mathcal{H}$ generates. If $G$ generates $S_\sigma$ and $m \in \mathcal{H}$, then $m$ is a sum of elements of $G \setminus \{0\}$, and irreducibility forces this sum to have one term, so $m \in G$. Taking $G$ finite by Theorem P8.6.5 shows that $\mathcal{H}$ is finite.

*Lemma P8.6.7.* Let $\gamma = \sigma + (-\sigma')$, a rational polyhedral cone, so that $\gamma^\vee = \sigma^\vee \cap (-\sigma')^\vee$. Write $\gamma^\vee = \operatorname{Cone}(m_1, \dots, m_s)$ with $m_j \in M$, and let $m = \sum m_j \in \gamma^\vee \cap M$. As in the proof of Proposition P8.6.4 (5), $\gamma \cap m^\perp = \gamma \cap (-\gamma)$. We show $\sigma \cap m^\perp = \tau$. If $x \in \tau$, then $x \in \sigma \subseteq \gamma$ and $-x \in -\sigma' \subseteq \gamma$, so $\langle m, x \rangle = 0$. Conversely, let $x \in \sigma$ with $\langle m, x \rangle = 0$. Then $x \in \gamma \cap (-\gamma)$, so $-x = s - s'$ with $s \in \sigma$ and $s' \in \sigma'$. Then $s' = x + s$ lies in $\sigma$ and in $\sigma'$, so $s' \in \tau$. Write $\tau = \sigma \cap m_1'^\perp$ with $m_1' \in \sigma^\vee$. Then $\langle m_1', x \rangle + \langle m_1', s \rangle = \langle m_1', s' \rangle = 0$, with both terms nonnegative, so $\langle m_1', x \rangle = 0$ and $x \in \tau$. The equality $\sigma' \cap m^\perp = \tau$ follows by the same argument with $\sigma$ and $\sigma'$ exchanged and $m$ replaced by $-m$, which lies in $\sigma'^\vee \cap (-\sigma)^\vee$ and has the same orthogonal.

### Exercises

1. (A) For $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, n e_1 - q e_2)$ as in Example P8.6.9, list the faces of $\sigma$ and of $\sigma^\vee$ and check the bijection and the dimension formula of Proposition P8.6.4 directly.
2. (A) Show that $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_1 + 2 e_2)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is simplicial and not smooth, and compute the Hilbert basis of its semigroup.
3. (B) Show that a strongly convex rational cone $\sigma$ of dimension $n$ is smooth if and only if $\sigma^\vee$ is smooth, and that in this case the Hilbert basis of $S_\sigma$ is the set of minimal generators of the rays of $\sigma^\vee$.
4. (B) For $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, n e_1 - q e_2)$ with $q = n - 1$, show that the Hilbert basis of $S_\sigma$ is $\{ (1, 0), (1, 1), (n - 1, n) \}$ when $n \geq 2$, so that $S_\sigma$ has three generators.
5. (B) Show that the separation lemma fails for convex polyhedral cones that do not meet in a common face: give two cones $\sigma, \sigma'$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ whose intersection is a ray that is a face of $\sigma$ but not of $\sigma'$, and show that no $m$ as in Lemma P8.6.7 exists.
6. (C) Let $\sigma$ be a strongly convex rational cone of dimension $n$ with $r$ rays. Show that $\sigma^\vee$ has exactly $r$ facets, and that the Hilbert basis of $S_\sigma$ contains the minimal generators of the rays of $\sigma^\vee$. Give an example where it contains other elements.

### Notes and history

The finiteness of a cone cut out by finitely many half-spaces goes back to Minkowski (1896) and Weyl (1935), and the duality theorem is Farkas's lemma (1902). Gordan's lemma is named after Paul Gordan's finiteness theorem for invariants (1873), whose combinatorial core is the finite generation of the lattice points of a rational cone. The proof given here of Lemma P8.6.2 is the elementary argument by moving along lines in the cone; Fulton, Section 1.2, deduces the same results from the separation of convex sets, and Cox, Little and Schenck, Section 1.2, follow the same route with more detail. The literature state of all results is theorem. The separation lemma is the combinatorial reason that the toric varieties of Section 6.2 are separated.
