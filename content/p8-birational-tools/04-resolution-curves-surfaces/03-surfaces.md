## Resolution of surfaces

### Statement

A *resolution* of a variety is a proper birational morphism from a smooth variety, as in Definition P8.4.1; for a reduced excellent scheme the word smooth is replaced by regular. The theorem below is cited in full. The proof in this section covers part 1 for normal projective surfaces, by a method different from Walker's and Zariski's, and the rest of the section uses only that case and the minimal resolution of Volume 2.

**Theorem P8.4.15 (resolution of surfaces).**

1. (*Walker 1935, Zariski 1939.*) Every surface over an algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$ has a resolution.
2. (*Abhyankar 1956.*) Every surface over an algebraically closed field of characteristic $p > 0$ has a resolution.
3. (*Lipman 1978.*) Let $X$ be an excellent, reduced, Noetherian scheme of dimension $2$. Then there is a proper birational morphism $f \colon Y \to X$ with $Y$ regular that is an isomorphism over the regular locus of $X$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

The proof of part 1 given here is Jung's method. It reduces the problem to the cyclic quotient singularities of Chapter 9, and resolves those by the toric resolution of Theorem P8.8.3. To bring the toric resolution back from the completed local ring to the surface, it has to be written as an operation on ideals, and the next lemma does that. Let $d \geq 2$, $0 < k < d$ with $\gcd(d, k) = 1$, and let $\sigma$, $b_1, \dots, b_r$, $v_0, \dots, v_{r+1}$, $\Sigma'$ and $E_1, \dots, E_r$ be as in Theorem P8.8.3, with $\gamma = \gamma_\sigma$ the torus fixed point of $U_\sigma$.

**Lemma P8.4.16 (the toric resolution as a normalized blow-up).** Let $c_0, \dots, c_{r+1}$ be integers with $c_0 = c_{r+1} = 0$, $c_i > 0$ for $1 \leq i \leq r$, and
$$c_{i-1} + c_{i+1} < b_i c_i \qquad (1 \leq i \leq r),$$
for instance $c_i = i(r + 1 - i)$. For $1 \leq i \leq r + 1$ let $m_i \in M$ be the element with $\langle m_i, v_{i-1} \rangle = c_{i-1}$ and $\langle m_i, v_i \rangle = c_i$, and let $J \subseteq k[\sigma^\vee \cap M]$ be the ideal generated by $\chi^{m_1}, \dots, \chi^{m_{r+1}}$.

1. Each $m_i$ lies in $\sigma^\vee$, and the zero set of $J$ in $U_\sigma$ is $\{ \gamma \}$.
2. $J \cdot \mathcal{O}_{X_{\Sigma'}} = \mathcal{O}_{X_{\Sigma'}}(-F)$ with $F = \sum_{j=1}^r c_j E_j$, and $-F \cdot E_i = b_i c_i - c_{i-1} - c_{i+1} > 0$.
3. The toric resolution $\pi \colon X_{\Sigma'} \to U_\sigma$ is isomorphic over $U_\sigma$ to the normalization of the blow-up of $U_\sigma$ along $J$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P8.4.17 (Jung's method).** Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$ and $X$ a normal projective surface over $k$. Then $X$ has a resolution $f \colon Y \to X$ with $Y$ projective.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Corollary P8.4.18 (the minimal resolution is an isomorphism over the smooth locus).** Let $X$ be a normal projective surface over an algebraically closed field of any characteristic, and $f_0 \colon Y_0 \to X$ its minimal resolution, which exists by Theorem P8.4.15 and Theorem 2.3.8 of Volume 2. Then $f_0$ is an isomorphism over the smooth locus $X_{\mathrm{sm}}$, and $\operatorname{Exc}(f_0) = f_0^{-1}(\operatorname{Sing} X)$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Corollary P8.4.19 (log resolutions of curves on normal surfaces).** Let $X$ be as in Corollary P8.4.18 and $C \neq 0$ a curve on $X$. Then there is a resolution $f \colon Y \to X$, with $Y$ projective, such that $f^{-1}(C) \cup \operatorname{Exc}(f)$ is a curve with simple normal crossings, and $f$ is an isomorphism over the open set of smooth points of $X$ at which $C_{\mathrm{red}}$ has simple normal crossings.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

### Proof in words

Project the surface finitely onto the plane. The projection is étale away from a branch curve, and after the branch curve is replaced by a curve with simple normal crossings, by the embedded resolution of Section 4.2, the normalization of the blown-up plane in the function field of the surface is étale away from a simple normal crossings divisor. Abhyankar's lemma says that such a cover looks, formally, like a quotient of a Kummer cover $x^n = u$, $y^n = v$ by an abelian group. Splitting off the reflections leaves a cyclic group acting with type $\frac{1}{d}(1, q)$, a toric singularity, which the toric method resolves. The toric resolution is the normalized blow-up of an ideal of finite colength, which comes from the surface itself, and regularity of the result can be tested after completion. For the minimal resolution, a regular $2$-form near a smooth point pulls back to a form vanishing on every exceptional curve, so the relative canonical divisor would be effective, nonzero and nef, against the negative definiteness of the exceptional curves.

### Examples

**Example P8.4.20 (Jung's method on $A_2$).** Let $X = V(z^2 - y^2 + x^3) \subseteq \mathbb{A}^3$, which is the $A_2$ singularity, since $z^2 - y^2 + x^3 = (z - y)(z + y) + x^3$. Projection to $(x, y)$ has degree $2$ and is branched along the cusp $B = V(y^2 - x^3)$, so $K(X) = K(\mathbb{A}^2)(\sqrt{y^2 - x^3})$. Take $S' = S_3$, the minimal log resolution of the cusp from Example P8.4.11, where $\sigma^*(y^2 - x^3)$ has divisor $C_3 + 2F_1 + 3F_2 + 6F_3$. The normalization $Z$ of $S'$ in $K(X)$ is the double cover branched along the components with odd coefficient, $C_3$ and $F_2$, which are disjoint smooth curves, so $Z$ is smooth and Jung's method already ends at $Z$. The exceptional curves of $Z \to X$ lie over $F_1$, $F_2$, $F_3$. The curve $F_1$ meets only $F_3$, away from the branch locus, so its preimage is two disjoint curves $F_1', F_1''$ with $q^* F_1 = F_1' + F_1''$ and $(F_1')^2 = (F_1'')^2 = 2F_1^2 / 2 = -3$. The curve $F_3$ meets the branch locus in two points, so its preimage is one curve $F_3'$ with $q^* F_3 = F_3'$ and $(F_3')^2 = 2 F_3^2 = -2$. The curve $F_2$ is in the branch locus, so $q^* F_2 = 2F_2'$ and $4 (F_2')^2 = 2F_2^2 = -4$, that is, $F_2'$ is a $(-1)$-curve. The curves $F_1'$, $F_1''$ and $F_2'$ each meet $F_3'$ once. Contracting $F_2'$ makes $F_3'$ a $(-1)$-curve, and contracting it makes $F_1'$ and $F_1''$ two $(-2)$-curves meeting once. This is the $A_2$ chain of Example P8.10.7, and by Theorem 2.3.8 of Volume 2 it is the minimal resolution.

**Example P8.4.21 (the quadric cone).** Let $X = V(z^2 - xy) \subseteq \mathbb{A}^3$. Projection to $(x, y)$ is branched along $B = V(xy)$, which already has simple normal crossings, so $S' = \mathbb{A}^2$ and $Z = X$. At the origin, $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{X, 0} \cong k[[s, t]]^H$ with $x = s^2$, $y = t^2$, $z = st$ and $H = \{ \pm (1, 1) \} \subseteq \mu_2 \times \mu_2$, which contains no reflections. So the singularity is $\frac{1}{2}(1, 1)$, with $r = 1$, $b_1 = 2$, $v_0 = e_2$, $v_1 = e_1$, $v_2 = 2e_1 - e_2$. Lemma P8.4.16 with $c = (0, 1, 0)$ gives $m_1 = (1, 0)$ and $m_2 = (1, 2)$. The semigroup $\sigma^\vee \cap M$ is generated by $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, with $u = \chi^{(1, 0)}$, $w = \chi^{(1, 1)}$, $v = \chi^{(1, 2)}$ and $w^2 = uv$, so $J = (u, v) = (x, y)$. The blow-up of $X$ along $(x, y)$ is not normal: in the chart $y = xy'$ it is $z^2 = x^2 y'$, and $z/x$ is integral over it. Adjoining $z/x$ gives the smooth chart with coordinates $x$ and $z/x$, and the exceptional curve is one $(-2)$-curve, by part 2 of Lemma P8.4.16 and Theorem P8.8.3.

**Example P8.4.22 (the ideal for $\frac{1}{5}(1, 2)$).** Here $5/2 = [3, 2]$, so $r = 2$, $v_0 = e_2$, $v_1 = e_1$, $v_2 = 3e_1 - e_2$, $v_3 = 5e_1 - 2e_2$. The choice $c = (0, 1, 1, 0)$ satisfies $0 + 1 < 3$ and $1 + 0 < 2$, and gives $m_1 = (1, 0)$, $m_2 = (1, 2)$, $m_3 = (2, 5)$, all in $\sigma^\vee = \{ m : m_2 \geq 0, \ 5m_1 - 2m_2 \geq 0 \}$. The degrees of $-F$ on $E_1$ and $E_2$ are $3 - 0 - 1 = 2$ and $2 - 1 - 0 = 1$. The choice $c_i = i(r + 1 - i)$ gives $c = (0, 2, 2, 0)$ and the ideal generated by $\chi^{(2, 0)}$, $\chi^{(2, 4)}$, $\chi^{(4, 10)}$, whose normalized blow-up is the same.

### Proof

*Lemma P8.4.16.* The cones $\operatorname{Cone}(v_{i-1}, v_i)$ are smooth by part 1 of Theorem P8.8.3, so $v_{i-1}, v_i$ is a basis of $N$ and $m_i$ exists and is unique. Let $\varphi$ be the function on $\sigma$ that is linear on each $\operatorname{Cone}(v_{i-1}, v_i)$ and equal to $m_i$ there; it is well defined, since $m_i$ and $m_{i+1}$ agree on $v_i$. For $1 \leq t \leq r$ put $g_t = m_{t+1} - m_t$. Then $\langle g_t, v_t \rangle = 0$, and since $v_{t-1} = b_t v_t - v_{t+1}$,
$$\langle g_t, v_{t-1} \rangle = b_t c_t - c_{t+1} - c_{t-1} > 0 .$$
The rays $v_0, \dots, v_{r+1}$ are in order of angle in the strongly convex cone $\sigma$, so $v_j$ is on the side of the line $\mathbb{R} v_t$ containing $v_{t-1}$ exactly when $j < t$. So $\langle g_t, v_j \rangle > 0$ for $j < t$ and $< 0$ for $j > t$. For $t < i$, $m_t - m_i = -(g_t + \dots + g_{i-1})$ is $\geq 0$ on $v_{i-1}$ and $v_i$, and for $t > i$, $m_t - m_i = g_i + \dots + g_{t-1}$ is $\geq 0$ on $v_{i-1}$ and $v_i$. So
$$m_t - m_i \in \operatorname{Cone}(v_{i-1}, v_i)^\vee \quad \text{for all } t, \qquad (\ast)$$
and $\langle m_t, v_j \rangle > c_j$ for $j \notin \{ t - 1, t \}$. In particular $\langle m_i, v_0 \rangle \geq c_0 = 0$ and $\langle m_i, v_{r+1} \rangle \geq 0$, so $m_i \in \sigma^\vee$. By part 1 of Theorem P8.6.25, $\chi^{m_1}$ does not vanish on $O(0) \cup O(\operatorname{Cone}(v_0))$, because $m_1 \in v_0^\perp$, and $\chi^{m_{r+1}}$ does not vanish on $O(\operatorname{Cone}(v_{r+1}))$, while every $\chi^{m_i}$ vanishes at $\gamma$ because $m_i \neq 0$. By part 2 of Theorem P8.6.25 these orbits cover $U_\sigma$, which proves part 1.

By $(\ast)$, on the affine chart $U_{\operatorname{Cone}(v_{i-1}, v_i)} \cong \mathbb{A}^2$ every generator of $J$ is $\chi^{m_i}$ times a regular function, so $J$ is generated there by $\chi^{m_i}$, whose divisor is $c_{i-1} D_{v_{i-1}} + c_i D_{v_i}$. Since $c_0 = c_{r+1} = 0$, these local divisors glue to $F = \sum_{j=1}^r c_j E_j$, and $J \cdot \mathcal{O}_{X_{\Sigma'}} = \mathcal{O}(-F)$. By part 3 of Theorem P8.8.3, $E_i^2 = -b_i$, $E_i \cdot E_{i \pm 1} = 1$ and $E_i \cdot E_j = 0$ for $|i - j| \geq 2$, so $-F \cdot E_i = b_i c_i - c_{i-1} - c_{i+1}$, which is positive by hypothesis. This proves part 2.

For part 3, let $\beta \colon B \to U_\sigma$ be the blow-up along $J$ and $\nu \colon \tilde B \to B$ its normalization. By part 2 and the universal property of the blow-up, Theorem P3.11.9, $\pi$ factors through $\beta$, and since $X_{\Sigma'}$ is normal and $\pi$ is birational, through $\nu$: $\pi = \beta \circ \nu \circ g$. The sheaf $\mathcal{O}_B(1) = J \cdot \mathcal{O}_B$ is ample relative to $U_\sigma$, because $B$ is the Proj of the Rees algebra of Definition P3.11.1, which is generated in degree $1$, and $\nu^* \mathcal{O}_B(1)$ is relatively ample because $\nu$ is finite; its pullback by $g$ is $J \cdot \mathcal{O}_{X_{\Sigma'}} = \mathcal{O}(-F)$. A curve contracted by $g$ lies over $\gamma$, because $\pi$ is an isomorphism elsewhere, so it is some $E_i$, and then $\deg \mathcal{O}(-F)|_{E_i} = 0$, against part 2. So $g$ is proper, birational, with finite fibres, hence finite by Zariski's main theorem, and a finite birational morphism onto the normal variety $\tilde B$ is an isomorphism.

*Theorem P8.4.17.* The construction has seven steps.

1. *A finite projection.* Embed $X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^N$ and choose a linear subspace $L$ of dimension $N - 3$ disjoint from $X$, which exists by a dimension count. Projection from $L$ is a morphism $p \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^2$. Its fibre over a point is $X \cap \Lambda$ for a linear space $\Lambda \supseteq L$ of dimension $N - 2$; a curve in $X \cap \Lambda$ would meet the hyperplane $L$ of $\Lambda$, so the fibres are finite and $p$ is finite by Corollary P4.6.17. It is surjective since $\dim X = 2$.
2. *The branch curve.* Since $\operatorname{char} k = 0$, the extension $K(X) / K(\mathbb{P}^2)$ is separable, so the set $R \subseteq X$ where $p$ is not étale is a proper closed subset, and $p(R)$ is a proper closed subset of $\mathbb{P}^2$. Let $B$ be a reduced curve containing $p(R)$. Then $p$ is étale over $\mathbb{P}^2 \setminus B$.
3. *Making the branch curve snc.* Let $\sigma \colon S' \to \mathbb{P}^2$ be a log resolution of $(\mathbb{P}^2, B)$, which exists by Theorem P8.4.10, and $D = \sigma^{-1}(B)$, a curve with simple normal crossings. The centres of $\sigma$ lie over $B$, so $\sigma$ is an isomorphism over $\mathbb{P}^2 \setminus B$.
4. *Normalizing.* Let $Z$ be the normalization of $S'$ in $K(X) \supseteq K(S') = K(\mathbb{P}^2)$, which is finite over $S'$ by Theorem P1.4.32 and projective. Since $X$ is normal and finite over $\mathbb{P}^2$, it is the normalization of $\mathbb{P}^2$ in $K(X)$, and the universal property of normalization gives a morphism $q \colon Z \to X$ over $\mathbb{P}^2$, which is proper and birational. Over $S' \setminus D \cong \mathbb{P}^2 \setminus B$, $Z$ is $X \times_{\mathbb{P}^2} (\mathbb{P}^2 \setminus B)$, so $Z \to S'$ is étale there.
5. *Abhyankar's lemma.* Let $z \in Z$ lie over $s \in D$, with local coordinates $u, v$ at $s$ such that $D = V(u)$ or $D = V(uv)$ near $s$. The ring $\mathcal{O}_Z \otimes \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{S', s}$ is the product of the $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z'}$ for $z'$ over $s$, and each factor is a normal domain, because $\mathcal{O}_{Z, z}$ is excellent (Matsumura, Section 32) and normal. So $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z}$ is the normalization of $k[[u, v]]$ in a finite extension of its fraction field that is étale over $uv \neq 0$. Since $\operatorname{char} k = 0$ the ramification is tame, and Abhyankar's lemma (SGA 1, Exposé XIII, Section 5, and Grothendieck and Murre, GM71) gives an integer $n \geq 1$, a subgroup $H \subseteq \mu_n \times \mu_n$ acting on $k[[x, y]]$ by $(\zeta, \eta) \cdot (x, y) = (\zeta x, \eta y)$, and an isomorphism $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z} \cong k[[x, y]]^H$ with $u = x^n$ and $v = y^n$. This is cited, not proved.
6. *Splitting off reflections.* Let $H_1 = H \cap (\mu_n \times 1)$ and $H_2 = H \cap (1 \times \mu_n)$, of orders $a$ and $b$, so that $k[[x, y]]^{H_1 \times H_2} = k[[x^a, y^b]]$, and $\bar H = H / (H_1 \times H_2)$ acts on $x' = x^a$, $y' = y^b$ diagonally with $k[[x, y]]^H = k[[x', y']]^{\bar H}$. If $h = (\zeta, \eta) \in H$ acts trivially on $y'$, then $\eta^b = 1$, so $(1, \eta) \in H_2$, since $H_2$ is the subgroup of order $b$ of $1 \times \mu_n$, and $h \in H_1 \times H_2$; likewise for $x'$. So $\bar H$ maps injectively to each factor of $k^\times \times k^\times$, hence is cyclic of some order $d$, generated by an element acting as $(\varepsilon, \varepsilon^q)$ with $\varepsilon$ a primitive $d$-th root of unity and $\gcd(q, d) = 1$. If $d = 1$, $z$ is a smooth point. Otherwise, by Proposition P8.9.5 for $\mu_d$ acting on $\mathbb{A}^2$ and Proposition P8.9.21, $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z} \cong \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{U_\sigma, \gamma}$ for the cone $\sigma$ of $\frac{1}{d}(1, q)$, and Theorem P8.8.3 applies with $k = q$.
7. *Descent.* $Z$ is normal, so it has finitely many singular points $z_1, \dots, z_s$. Fix $z = z_i$ and an isomorphism $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z} \cong \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{U_\sigma, \gamma}$ as in step 6. The ideal $J$ of Lemma P8.4.16 contains a power $\mathfrak{m}_\gamma^e$ by part 1, so $J \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{U_\sigma, \gamma}$ is an ideal of $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z}$ containing $\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}_z^e$. By Corollary P1.6.15 it is $J_z \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z}$ for a unique ideal $J_z \supseteq \mathfrak{m}_z^e$ of $\mathcal{O}_{Z, z}$, which is the stalk of a unique coherent ideal sheaf $\mathcal{J}_i$ with cosupport $\{ z_i \}$. Let $Y$ be the normalization of the blow-up of $Z$ along $\mathcal{J}_1 \cdots \mathcal{J}_s$; it is projective over $Z$ and an isomorphism over $Z \setminus \{ z_1, \dots, z_s \}$. Blowing up commutes with the flat base change $\operatorname{Spec} \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z} \to Z$, and normalization commutes with it as well, because the fibres of $\mathcal{O}_{Z, z} \to \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z}$ are geometrically regular (excellence again), so a base change of a normal scheme of finite type stays normal (Matsumura, Sections 23 and 32) and the base change of the normalization is finite and birational onto a normal scheme. The same holds on the toric side, so $Y \times_Z \operatorname{Spec} \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z} \cong X_{\Sigma'} \times_{U_\sigma} \operatorname{Spec} \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{U_\sigma, \gamma}$ by Lemma P8.4.16, and this scheme is regular, by Theorem 23.7 of Matsumura applied to the flat morphism with geometrically regular fibres from the smooth surface $X_{\Sigma'}$. For $y \in Y$ over $z$, the local ring of $Y \times_Z \operatorname{Spec} \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Z, z}$ at a point over $y$ is faithfully flat over $\mathcal{O}_{Y, y}$, so $\mathcal{O}_{Y, y}$ is regular by Theorem 23.7 of Matsumura. So $Y$ is a smooth projective surface, and $f = q \circ (Y \to Z) \colon Y \to X$ is a resolution.

*Corollary P8.4.18.* Let $x \in X_{\mathrm{sm}}$, and suppose $f_0^{-1}(x)$ is not a point. By Theorem P5.8.3 there are an affine neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ in $X_{\mathrm{sm}}$ over which $f_0$ is an isomorphism away from $x$, and integral curves $C_1, \dots, C_r$, $r \geq 1$, with $f_0^{-1}(x) = C_1 \cup \dots \cup C_r$ and negative definite intersection matrix. Shrinking $U$, let $s, t$ be local coordinates at $x$ with $ds \wedge dt$ a generator of $\omega_U$. The form $f_0^*(ds \wedge dt)$ is a regular $2$-form on $f_0^{-1}(U)$, nonzero where $f_0$ is an isomorphism, so its divisor $A$ is an effective divisor supported on the $C_j$, and $\mathcal{O}(K_{Y_0}) \cong \mathcal{O}(A)$ on $f_0^{-1}(U)$. At a general point of $C_j$ with local equation $w$, $f_0^* s = w \alpha$ and $f_0^* t = w \beta$, because $s$ and $t$ vanish at $x$, so $f_0^*(ds \wedge dt) = d(w\alpha) \wedge d(w\beta)$ is divisible by $w$. Hence $A = \sum a_j C_j$ with all $a_j \geq 1$. By part 4 of Theorem 2.3.8 of Volume 2, $A \cdot C_j = K_{Y_0} \cdot C_j \geq 0$ for every $j$, so $A^2 = \sum a_j (A \cdot C_j) \geq 0$, against negative definiteness, since $A \neq 0$. So $f_0^{-1}(x)$ is a point for every $x \in X_{\mathrm{sm}}$, and $f_0$ is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{sm}}$ by part 1 of Theorem P5.8.3. Conversely, if $x \in \operatorname{Sing} X$, then $f_0^{-1}(x)$ is not a point, since otherwise $f_0$ would be an isomorphism near $x$ by the same part 1, so $\operatorname{Exc}(f_0) = f_0^{-1}(\operatorname{Sing} X)$.

*Corollary P8.4.19.* Let $f_0 \colon Y_0 \to X$ be the minimal resolution and $C'$ the reduced curve $f_0^{-1}(C) \cup \operatorname{Exc}(f_0)$ on the smooth projective surface $Y_0$. Let $\pi \colon Y \to Y_0$ be the minimal log resolution of $(Y_0, C')$ from Theorem P8.4.10, and $f = f_0 \circ \pi$. The curves contracted by $f$ are those contracted by $\pi$ and the strict transforms of those contracted by $f_0$, so $f^{-1}(C) \cup \operatorname{Exc}(f) \subseteq \pi^{-1}(C') \cup \operatorname{Exc}(\pi)$, which has simple normal crossings. A union of components of a curve with simple normal crossings has simple normal crossings. The map $f_0$ is an isomorphism over $X_{\mathrm{sm}}$ by Corollary P8.4.18, and $\pi$ is an isomorphism over the points of $Y_0$ where $C'$ has simple normal crossings, by part 1 of Theorem P8.4.10, which proves the last claim.

### Exercises

1. (A) Run Jung's method on $z^n = xy$, $n \geq 2$: show that $H = \{ (\zeta, \zeta^{-1}) : \zeta \in \mu_n \}$, that the singularity is $\frac{1}{n}(1, n - 1)$, and that its resolution is a chain of $n - 1$ curves with self-intersection $-2$.
2. (A) For $\frac{1}{d}(1, 1)$ show that Lemma P8.4.16 gives $J = (\chi^{(1, 0)}, \chi^{(1, d)})$ and that the exceptional curve has self-intersection $-d$.
3. (B) Show that the strict inequality in Lemma P8.4.16 is needed: if $c_{i-1} + c_{i+1} = b_i c_i$ for some $i$, the normalized blow-up of $J$ contracts $E_i$.
4. (B) Let $H \subseteq \mu_4 \times \mu_4$ be generated by $(\sqrt{-1}, -1)$. Compute $H_1$, $H_2$ and $\bar H$ in step 6 of the proof of Theorem P8.4.17, and identify $k[[x, y]]^H$ with $\frac{1}{2}(1, 1)$.
5. (B) Let $f \colon Y \to X$ be a resolution of a normal projective surface with an exceptional curve over a smooth point of $X$. Show, with the argument of Corollary P8.4.18, that some exceptional curve over that point is a $(-1)$-curve.
6. (C) Run Jung's method on $z^2 = y^2 - x^5$, using Exercise 1 of Section 4.2, and show that the resulting resolution contracts, through two $(-1)$-curves, to a chain of four $(-2)$-curves, the $A_4$ configuration.

### Lean

Resolutions of surfaces, Abhyankar's lemma and the descent of the toric resolution are not in Lean. Section 5.2 explains why the formal side does not need them.

### Notes and history

Jung introduced the method of this section in 1908 for complex surfaces, with the branch curve resolved by Puiseux expansions and the remaining singularities identified through their local fundamental groups. Hirzebruch in 1953 resolved the cyclic quotient singularities by continued fractions, which completed Jung's method; the toric description of Chapter 8 is a later reformulation. Walker gave the first complete proof for complex surfaces in 1935, Zariski the first algebraic proof in characteristic $0$ in 1939, and Abhyankar the proof in positive characteristic in 1956. Lipman's theorem of 1978 covers excellent two-dimensional schemes, and his method, alternately normalizing and blowing up the singular points, is the one in Kollár's Chapter 2 and in Cutkosky's book. The proof of Theorem P8.4.17 uses three facts that are cited and not proved in this handbook: Abhyankar's lemma on tame covers, the excellence of local rings of varieties, and the descent of regularity along completion. It also relies on the toric results of Chapters 8 and 9, which come later in the volume but do not depend on this chapter.
