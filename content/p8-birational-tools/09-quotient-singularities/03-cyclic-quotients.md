## Cyclic quotient singularities

### Statement

Let $r \geq 2$ be invertible in $k$, and fix a primitive $r$-th root of unity $\varepsilon \in k$, so that the group $\mu_r$ of $r$-th roots of unity is cyclic, generated by $\varepsilon$.

**Definition P8.9.20 (cyclic quotient singularities).** For integers $a_1, \dots, a_n$ let $\mu_r$ act on $\mathbb{A}^n$ by letting $\varepsilon$ act on the coordinate functions by $x_i \mapsto \varepsilon^{a_i} x_i$. The quotient $\mathbb{A}^n / \mu_r$, together with the image $0$ of the origin, is the *cyclic quotient singularity* of type $\frac{1}{r}(a_1, \dots, a_n)$. The action is faithful if and only if $\gcd(r, a_1, \dots, a_n) = 1$, which is assumed from now on. The type depends only on the $a_i$ modulo $r$, is unchanged by permuting the $a_i$, and $\frac{1}{r}(a_1, \dots, a_n) = \frac{1}{r}(j a_1, \dots, j a_n)$ for $\gcd(j, r) = 1$, because $\varepsilon^j$ generates the same group.

**Proposition P8.9.21 (cyclic quotients are toric).** Let $N = \mathbb{Z}^n + \mathbb{Z} \cdot \frac{1}{r}(a_1, \dots, a_n) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_1, \dots, e_n)$. The dual lattice is
$$M = \{ m \in \mathbb{Z}^n : a_1 m_1 + \dots + a_n m_n \equiv 0 \pmod r \},$$
and $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]^{\mu_r} = k[\sigma^\vee \cap M]$, so $\frac{1}{r}(a_1, \dots, a_n) \cong U_{\sigma, N}$. For $n = 2$ and $a = (1, q)$ with $0 < q < r$ and $\gcd(r, q) = 1$, the vectors $w = \frac{1}{r}(1, q)$ and $e_2$ form a basis of $N$, and in this basis $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, r e_1 - q e_2)$, the cone of Example P8.6.9.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Proposition P8.9.22 (small cyclic groups).** The group $\mu_r$ acting with type $\frac{1}{r}(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ is small if and only if, for each $i$, $\gcd(r, a_1, \dots, \widehat{a_i}, \dots, a_n) = 1$, where the hat means that $a_i$ is omitted. For $n = 2$ the group is small if and only if $\gcd(r, a_1) = \gcd(r, a_2) = 1$, and then $\frac{1}{r}(a_1, a_2) = \frac{1}{r}(1, q)$ with $0 < q < r$ and $q \equiv a_2 a_1^{-1} \pmod r$. Also $\frac{1}{r}(1, q) \cong \frac{1}{r}(1, q')$ when $q q' \equiv 1 \pmod r$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P8.9.23 (cyclic quotient surface singularities).** Let $X = \frac{1}{r}(1, q)$ with $0 < q < r$ and $\gcd(r, q) = 1$.

1. $X$ is a normal affine surface, smooth away from $0$ and singular at $0$.
2. $\operatorname{Cl}(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}/r$, so $r D$ is Cartier for every Weil divisor $D$.
3. $r K_X$ is Cartier, and the index of $K_X$, the least $m \geq 1$ with $m K_X$ Cartier, is $r / \gcd(r, q + 1)$. In particular $K_X$ is Cartier if and only if $q = r - 1$.
4. Let $\frac{r}{q} = [b_1, \dots, b_s]$ be the Hirzebruch-Jung continued fraction. There is a resolution $f \colon Y \to X$ whose exceptional locus is a chain of smooth rational curves $E_1, \dots, E_s$ with $E_i^2 = -b_i \leq -2$, consecutive curves meeting transversally in one point and the others disjoint. No $E_i$ is a $(-1)$-curve.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

### Proof in words

A diagonal group acts on each monomial by a character, so the invariant ring is spanned by the invariant monomials, which are the lattice points of the positive orthant in the lattice dual to $\mathbb{Z}^n + \mathbb{Z} \cdot \frac{1}{r}(a)$. This makes every cyclic quotient singularity toric, and in dimension two the toric results of Part C apply directly: the class group, the Cartier index of the canonical divisor and the continued fraction resolution. An element of the cyclic group is a pseudo-reflection exactly when all but one of its eigenvalues are $1$, which is a divisibility condition on the weights.

### Examples

**Example P8.9.24 (the singularity $\frac{1}{2}(1, 1)$).** Here $N = \mathbb{Z}^2 + \mathbb{Z} (\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})$ and $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, 2 e_1 - e_2)$ in the basis of Proposition P8.9.21. The continued fraction is $\frac{2}{1} = [2]$, so the resolution has one exceptional curve $E$ with $E^2 = -2$. This is the quadric cone of Example P8.9.8, $K_X$ is Cartier because $q = 1 = r - 1$, and $\operatorname{Cl}(X) = \mathbb{Z}/2$.

**Example P8.9.25 (the singularity $\frac{1}{n}(1, 1)$).** A monomial $x^a y^b$ is invariant if and only if $n \mid a + b$, so the invariant ring is generated by the $n + 1$ monomials of degree $n$, matching the Hilbert basis $\{ (1, j) : 0 \leq j \leq n \}$ of Example P8.6.9. Their ratios define the rational normal curve of degree $n$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$, so $X$ is the affine cone over it, which Chapter 12 studies as $C(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(n))$. The continued fraction is $[n]$, so the resolution has one curve with $E^2 = -n$. By part 3 of Theorem P8.9.23 the index of $K_X$ is $n / \gcd(n, 2)$: it is $1$ for $n = 2$, $n/2$ for $n$ even and $n$ for $n$ odd.

**Example P8.9.26 (the singularity $\frac{1}{5}(1, 2)$).** A monomial $x^a y^b$ is invariant if and only if $a + 2b \equiv 0 \pmod 5$. For $b = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ the smallest admissible $a$ is $3$, $1$, $4$, $2$, $0$, and for $b = 0$ the smallest positive one is $a = 5$, giving $x^5$, $x^3 y$, $x y^2$, $x^4 y^3$, $x^2 y^4$, $y^5$. Since $x^4 y^3 = (x^3 y)(x y^2)$ and $x^2 y^4 = (x y^2)^2$, and every invariant monomial is a product of one of these with a power of $x^5$ or $y^5$, the invariant ring is minimally generated by
$$x^5, \quad x^3 y, \quad x y^2, \quad y^5,$$
none of which is a product of the others. So $X$ has embedding dimension $4$; Section 11.2 shows its multiplicity is $3$. The continued fraction is $\frac{5}{2} = [3, 2]$ (Example P8.8.5), so the resolution has curves with self-intersections $-3$ and $-2$. Since $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$, $\frac{1}{5}(1, 2) \cong \frac{1}{5}(1, 3)$ by Proposition P8.9.22, and $\frac{5}{3} = [2, 3]$ is the same chain reversed. The index of $K_X$ is $5 / \gcd(5, 3) = 5$.

**Example P8.9.27 (the singularities $A_{r-1}$).** The type $\frac{1}{r}(1, r - 1) = \frac{1}{r}(1, -1)$ is the action of Example P8.9.19, so $X = \{ uv = w^r \}$. The continued fraction $\frac{r}{r - 1} = [2, \dots, 2]$ has $r - 1$ entries (Example P8.8.6), so the resolution is a chain of $r - 1$ curves of self-intersection $-2$, and $K_X$ is Cartier.

**Example P8.9.28 (a non-small cyclic group).** The type $\frac{1}{4}(1, 2)$ is the group of Example P8.9.18. By Proposition P8.9.22 it is not small, since $\gcd(4, 2) = 2$: the element $\varepsilon^2$ acts by $x \mapsto -x$, $y \mapsto y$. Torically, $N = \mathbb{Z}^2 + \mathbb{Z} (\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2})$ contains $2 \cdot (\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}) - e_2 = (\frac{1}{2}, 0)$, so $e_1$ is not primitive in $N$. The vectors $u_1 = (\frac{1}{2}, 0)$ and $w = (\frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2})$ have determinant $\frac{1}{4}$, the covolume of $N$, so they form a basis of $N$, and $e_2 = 2 w - u_1$. In the basis $w, u_1$ the cone is $\operatorname{Cone}(e_2, 2 e_1 - e_2)$, so $\frac{1}{4}(1, 2) \cong \frac{1}{2}(1, 1)$, as Example P8.9.18 found algebraically.

### Proof

*Proposition P8.9.21.* The generator $\varepsilon$ multiplies the monomial $x^m = x_1^{m_1} \cdots x_n^{m_n}$ by $\varepsilon^{\langle a, m \rangle}$ with $\langle a, m \rangle = \sum_i a_i m_i$. Each monomial spans a stable line, so a polynomial is invariant if and only if each of its monomials is, and $k[x]^{\mu_r}$ is spanned by the monomials $x^m$ with $m \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^n$ and $r \mid \langle a, m \rangle$. An element $m \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ pairs integrally with $\frac{1}{r} a$ exactly when $r \mid \langle a, m \rangle$, and it pairs integrally with $\mathbb{Z}^n$ always; an element of $\mathbb{R}^n$ pairing integrally with $N \supseteq \mathbb{Z}^n$ lies in $\mathbb{Z}^n$. So $M$ is as stated. Since $\sigma^\vee$ is the positive orthant, the invariant monomials are the $x^m$ with $m \in \sigma^\vee \cap M$, and the invariant ring is the semigroup algebra $k[\sigma^\vee \cap M]$ that defines $U_{\sigma, N}$ in Section 6.1.

For $n = 2$ and $a = (1, q)$, $N$ is generated by $e_1$, $e_2$ and $w$, and $e_1 = r w - q e_2$, so $w$ and $e_2$ generate $N$. They are linearly independent, so they form a basis. In the coordinates of this basis, $e_2 = (0, 1)$ and $e_1 = (r, -q)$, so $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_2)$ becomes $\operatorname{Cone}(e_2, r e_1 - q e_2)$.

*Proposition P8.9.22.* The element $\varepsilon^j$ acts by $\operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon^{j a_1}, \dots, \varepsilon^{j a_n})$. It is a pseudo-reflection with the $i$-th eigenvalue different from $1$ if and only if $r \mid j a_l$ for all $l \neq i$ and $r \nmid j a_i$. Put $d_i = \gcd(r, a_l : l \neq i)$. If $d_i > 1$, let $j = r/d_i$. Then $r \mid j a_l$ for $l \neq i$, and $r \mid j a_i$ would mean $d_i \mid a_i$, so $d_i \mid \gcd(r, a_1, \dots, a_n) = 1$, which is false; so $\varepsilon^j$ is a pseudo-reflection. Conversely, if $\varepsilon^j$ is a pseudo-reflection with $i$-th eigenvalue different from $1$, let $c = r / \gcd(r, j)$. Then $r \mid j a_l$ means $c \mid a_l$ for $l \neq i$, and $c > 1$ because $\varepsilon^j \neq 1$, so $d_i \geq c > 1$. For $n = 2$ the condition reads $\gcd(r, a_2) = 1$ and $\gcd(r, a_1) = 1$. Then choose $j$ with $j a_1 \equiv 1 \pmod r$; by Definition P8.9.20 the type is $\frac{1}{r}(1, j a_2)$, and $j a_2 \equiv a_2 a_1^{-1}$ is prime to $r$, so it reduces to some $q$ with $0 < q < r$. Finally, exchanging the coordinates turns $\frac{1}{r}(1, q)$ into $\frac{1}{r}(q, 1)$, which is $\frac{1}{r}(1, q')$ by the previous step with $j = q'$.

*Theorem P8.9.23.* (1) The ring of invariants of the normal domain $k[x, y]$ is normal by Theorem P8.9.3. A point $(x, y) \neq (0, 0)$ is fixed by $\varepsilon^j$ only if $\varepsilon^j x = x$ and $\varepsilon^{j q} y = y$, so $r \mid j$ if $x \neq 0$, and $r \mid j q$, hence $r \mid j$, if $y \neq 0$. So the action is free away from the origin, and $\pi$ is étale there by Proposition P8.9.5; an étale image of a smooth point is smooth, so $X$ is smooth away from $0$. At $0$, by Proposition P8.9.21, $X = U_\sigma$ with $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, r e_1 - q e_2)$, whose ray generators have determinant $-r$, so they are not part of a basis of $N$ and $U_\sigma$ is singular by Theorem P8.6.27.

(2) This is Example P8.7.7 with $d = r$ and $k = q$. Since $\operatorname{Cl}(X)$ is killed by $r$, every $r D$ is principal, in particular Cartier.

(3) This is Example P8.7.13: for $K_X = -D_1 - D_2$ (Theorem P8.7.17), $m K_X$ is Cartier if and only if $r \mid m(q + 1)$, that is, if and only if $r / \gcd(r, q + 1)$ divides $m$. For $0 < q < r$, $r \mid q + 1$ if and only if $q = r - 1$.

(4) This is Theorem P8.8.3 with $d = r$ and $k = q$. Every $b_i \geq 2$ in the Hirzebruch-Jung continued fraction, so no $E_i$ has self-intersection $-1$.

### Exercises

1. (A) Compute the Hilbert basis of $\sigma^\vee \cap M$ for $\frac{1}{7}(1, 3)$, and compare its size with $3 + \sum_i (b_i - 2)$ for $\frac{7}{3} = [b_1, \dots, b_s]$.
2. (A) Show that $\frac{1}{3}(1, 1, 1)$ is the affine cone over the Veronese embedding $v_3(\mathbb{P}^2) \subseteq \mathbb{P}^9$.
3. (A) Show that $\frac{1}{6}(1, 3)$ is not small, and identify it with a quotient by a small group as in Example P8.9.28.
4. (B) Show that the singularities $\frac{1}{r}(1, q)$ with $K_X$ of index $1$ are exactly the $A_{r-1}$, and those with index $2$ have $r$ even and $q = \frac{r}{2} - 1$.
5. (B) For $\frac{1}{r}(1, q)$, show that the Hilbert basis of $\sigma^\vee \cap M$ has $t + 2$ elements, where $\frac{r}{r - q} = [b_1', \dots, b_t']$ is the dual continued fraction, and check this on Examples P8.9.25 to P8.9.27.
6. (C) Show that $\frac{1}{r}(1, 1, \dots, 1)$ in dimension $n$ is the affine cone over the $r$-th Veronese embedding of $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$, and that its canonical divisor has index $r / \gcd(r, n)$.

### Notes and history

Cyclic quotient surface singularities were resolved by Hirzebruch in 1953 and studied by Jung before him; the continued fraction appears in both. The toric description of a cyclic quotient singularity by the lattice $\mathbb{Z}^n + \mathbb{Z} \cdot \frac{1}{r}(a)$ is in Fulton (Ful93), Section 2.2, and in Cox, Little and Schenck (CLS11), Chapter 10. The notation $\frac{1}{r}(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ is Reid's, from the Young Person's Guide (Rei87), where the terminal cyclic quotient singularities of dimension $3$ are also classified. Riemenschneider computed the equations of the cyclic quotient surface singularities; their embedding dimension is governed by the dual continued fraction of Exercise 5.
