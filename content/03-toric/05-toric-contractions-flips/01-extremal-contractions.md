## Extremal rays and their contractions

### Statement

Throughout the chapter $\Sigma$ is a complete simplicial fan in $N_{\mathbb{R}} \cong \mathbb{R}^n$, and $X = X_\Sigma$ is projective. By Theorem 3.4.21 the cone of curves $NE(X)$ is the closed rational polyhedral cone spanned by the classes of the wall curves $V(\tau)$, and it contains no line. By Theorem 3.4.20 the map $C \mapsto (D_\rho \cdot C)_\rho$ identifies $N_1(X)$ with the space $R(\Sigma)$ of linear relations among the minimal generators $u_\rho$, and the class of $V(\tau)$ with the wall relation of $\tau$. This section attaches to each extremal ray of $NE(X)$ a toric morphism that contracts exactly the curves of the ray.

**Definition 3.5.1 (extremal rays, their walls and their signs).** An *extremal ray* of $NE(X)$ is a face $R$ of $NE(X)$ of dimension one. A wall $\tau$ of $\Sigma$ is an *$R$-wall* if $[V(\tau)] \in R$. For $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$, the sign of $D_\rho \cdot R$ is the sign of $D_\rho \cdot c$ for a nonzero class $c \in R$; it does not depend on $c$. The *sign sets* of $R$ are

$$J_+ = \{\rho : D_\rho \cdot R > 0\}, \qquad J_0 = \{\rho : D_\rho \cdot R = 0\}, \qquad J_- = \{\rho : D_\rho \cdot R < 0\}.$$

When $J_- \neq \emptyset$ we put $\sigma_- = \operatorname{Cone}(u_\rho : \rho \in J_-)$.

**Lemma 3.5.2 (the relation of an extremal ray).** Let $R$ be an extremal ray of $NE(X)$.

1. $R$ has at least one $R$-wall, and $[V(\tau)] \neq 0$ for every wall $\tau$.
2. There is a nonzero relation $b^R \in R(\Sigma)$, unique up to a positive factor, such that the wall relation of every $R$-wall $\tau$ is $c_\tau b^R$ with $c_\tau > 0$. So $J_\pm = \{\rho : \pm b^R_\rho > 0\}$, and $J_+ \cup J_-$ is the support of $b^R$.
3. Let $\tau$ be an $R$-wall with the standard labels of Definition 3.4.1, between $\sigma = \tau + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} u_n$ and $\sigma' = \tau + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} u_{n+1}$. Then the rays of $u_n$ and $u_{n+1}$ are in $J_+$, and every ray of $J_+ \cup J_-$ is a ray of $\sigma$ or of $\sigma'$. Every ray of $J_-$ is a ray of $\tau$.
4. $|J_+| \ge 2$. If $J_- \neq \emptyset$, then $\sigma_-$ is a face of every $R$-wall, so $\sigma_- \in \Sigma$ and $\dim \sigma_- = |J_-|$.

**Lemma 3.5.3 (the curves of a ray lie in the negative divisors).** Let $R$ be an extremal ray and $C \subset X$ an irreducible curve with $[C] \in R$. Then $C \subset D_\rho$ for every $\rho \in J_-$. If $J_- \neq \emptyset$, then $C \subset V(\sigma_-)$, a closed torus-invariant subvariety of dimension $n - |J_-|$.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

**Lemma 3.5.4 (supporting divisors).** Let $R$ be an extremal ray. There is a nef torus-invariant $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor $P$ with $P^\perp \cap NE(X) = R$. For such a $P$ and a wall $\tau$, $P \cdot V(\tau) = 0$ if and only if $\tau$ is an $R$-wall. We call $P$ a *supporting divisor* of $R$.

**Proposition 3.5.5 (the domains of a nef divisor).** Let $P$ be a nef torus-invariant $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor with support function $\varphi = \varphi_P$ and data $m_\sigma$ on the maximal cones (Definition 3.4.11). For each form $m$ in the finite set $\{m_\sigma\}$, put $C_m = \{x \in N_{\mathbb{R}} : \varphi(x) = \langle m, x \rangle\}$, the *domain* of $m$.

1. $C_m = \{x : \langle m, x \rangle \le \langle m', x \rangle \text{ for all } m' \in \{m_\sigma\}\}$. It is a rational polyhedral cone of dimension $n$, and it is the union of the maximal cones $\sigma$ with $m_\sigma = m$.
2. For a wall pair $(\sigma, \sigma')$ with wall $\tau$, $m_\sigma = m_{\sigma'}$ if and only if $P \cdot V(\tau) = 0$.
3. The set $L = \{x : \varphi(x) + \varphi(-x) = 0\}$ is the rational subspace $\bigcap_{m, m'} (m - m')^\perp$. It is the largest subspace contained in each $C_m$, and $\varphi(x + y) = \varphi(x) + \langle m, y \rangle$ for $x \in N_{\mathbb{R}}$, $y \in L$ and any $m$.
4. The domains and all their faces form a set of cones $\Sigma_P$, which we call the *domain fan* of $P$. Two of its cones meet in a face of each, and its cones cover $N_{\mathbb{R}}$. Its cones are strongly convex if and only if $L = 0$. Each cone of $\Sigma$ lies in a cone of $\Sigma_P$, and each cone of $\Sigma_P$ is a union of cones of $\Sigma$.
5. Two maximal cones $\sigma$, $\sigma''$ of $\Sigma$ lie in the same domain if and only if there is a chain $\sigma = \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_r = \sigma''$ of maximal cones such that $\sigma_i$ and $\sigma_{i+1}$ share a wall $\tau_i$ with $P \cdot V(\tau_i) = 0$.

**Theorem 3.5.6 (the contraction of an extremal ray).** Let $R$ be an extremal ray with supporting divisor $P$, and let $\Sigma_R = \Sigma_P$ and $L$ be as in Proposition 3.5.5. Put $N_Z = N / (N \cap L)$ and let $\Sigma_Z$ be the set of images in $(N_Z)_{\mathbb{R}} = N_{\mathbb{R}} / L$ of the cones of $\Sigma_R$.

1. $\Sigma_Z$ is a complete fan, and the projection $N \to N_Z$ is compatible with $\Sigma$ and $\Sigma_Z$. It gives a proper surjective toric morphism $\pi_R \colon X \to Z = X_{\Sigma_Z}$.
2. There is an ample torus-invariant $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor $A$ on $Z$ with $\pi_R^* A \sim_{\mathbb{Q}} P$. In particular $Z$ is projective.
3. An irreducible curve $C \subset X$ is contracted to a point by $\pi_R$ if and only if $[C] \in R$. A wall curve $V(\tau)$ is contracted if and only if $\tau$ is an $R$-wall.
4. $\dim Z = n - \dim L$, and $\pi_R$ is birational if and only if $L = 0$. Then $N_Z = N$ and $\Sigma_Z = \Sigma_R$.
5. $L \neq 0$ if and only if $J_- = \emptyset$. In that case $u_\rho \in L$ for every $\rho \in J_+$.
6. The fan $\Sigma_R$, and so $\pi_R$, does not depend on the choice of $P$: by part 5 of Proposition 3.5.5, its maximal cones are the unions of the classes of maximal cones of $\Sigma$ joined by chains of $R$-walls.

We call $\pi_R$ the *contraction* of $R$. It is of *fibre type* if $\dim Z < n$, *divisorial* if it is birational and its exceptional locus is a divisor, and *small* if it is birational and its exceptional locus has codimension at least $2$. Section 2 shows that every birational contraction is divisorial or small, according to the size of $J_-$.

The Lean library states the contraction through its fan: `MinimalModel.Toric.Fan.Refines`, `MinimalModel.Toric.Fan.HasRelPicardOne` and `MinimalModel.Toric.Fan.IsAmpleOver` in `MinimalModel/Toric/Program.lean`, and a fibre type contraction is `MinimalModel.Toric.IsToricMoriFibreSpace`. These are definitions; the theorem is not proved there.

### Proof in words

An extremal ray is spanned by one wall curve, so all its curves have the same relation among the $u_\rho$, up to a positive factor. The wall relation has positive coefficients at the two rays off the wall and zero coefficients at the rays far from the wall. So a negative coefficient can only sit on a ray of the wall itself, and this is why the rays of $J_-$ span a face of every $R$-wall.

A supporting divisor $P$ is a nef divisor that vanishes on $R$ and is positive on the rest of the cone of curves. Its support function is a tent made of flat pieces, and $P$ is zero on $V(\tau)$ exactly when the tent does not bend at the crease $\tau$. So the flat pieces of the tent are the unions of maximal cones joined across $R$-walls. These pieces, the domains, are convex because the tent is the minimum of finitely many linear forms. They form the fan of the target of the contraction. When the tent is flat along a whole subspace $L$, we divide by $L$ and the target has smaller dimension.

The tent of $P$ is linear on each domain, so it descends to a strictly convex tent on the target, which is an ample divisor $A$ with $\pi^* A = P$. Then the projection formula says that a curve is contracted if and only if $P$ is zero on it, which is the same as $[C] \in R$. Finally, if $J_-$ is empty, the relation of $R$ has only positive coefficients, so the $u_\rho$ of $J_+$ add up to zero with positive weights, and the tent is forced to be linear on the lines through them.

### Examples

**Example 3.5.7 (the Hirzebruch surface).** Let $\mathbb{F}_n$ with $n \ge 1$ have the rays $u_1 = (1, 0)$, $u_2 = (0, 1)$, $u_3 = (-1, n)$, $u_4 = (0, -1)$ of Example 3.4.7. The walls are the rays, and the wall relations are $u_2 + u_4 = 0$ at $u_1$ and at $u_3$, $u_1 + u_3 - n u_2 = 0$ at $u_2$, and $u_1 + u_3 + n u_4 = 0$ at $u_4$. In $R(\Sigma)$, with coordinates $(b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4)$, these are $f = (0, 1, 0, 1)$ for the fibres $D_1$ and $D_3$, $s = (1, -n, 1, 0)$ for the negative section $D_2$, and $(1, 0, 1, n) = s + n f$ for $D_4$. So $NE(\mathbb{F}_n)$ has the two extremal rays $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} f$ and $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} s$.

For $R = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} f$ the sign sets are $J_+ = \{u_2, u_4\}$, $J_0 = \{u_1, u_3\}$ and $J_- = \emptyset$. A supporting divisor is $P = D_1$, with $D_1 \cdot f = 0$ and $D_1 \cdot s = 1$. Its support function is $-x$ on the right half-plane and $0$ on the left half-plane, so the two domains are the half-planes $\{x \ge 0\}$ and $\{x \le 0\}$, and $L$ is the line $x = 0$, which contains $u_2$ and $u_4$ as part 5 of Theorem 3.5.6 says. The quotient $N / (N \cap L) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ carries the fan of $\mathbb{P}^1$, and $\pi_R$ is the ruling $\mathbb{F}_n \to \mathbb{P}^1$, of fibre type.

For $R = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} s$ the sign sets are $J_+ = \{u_1, u_3\}$, $J_0 = \{u_4\}$ and $J_- = \{u_2\}$. A supporting divisor is $P = D_4$, with $D_4 \cdot s = 0$ and $D_4 \cdot f = 1$. Its data, with $\langle m_\sigma, u_\rho \rangle = -a_\rho$, are $m = (0, 0)$ on the two cones $\operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2)$ and $\operatorname{Cone}(u_2, u_3)$, $(n, 1)$ on $\operatorname{Cone}(u_3, u_4)$ and $(0, 1)$ on $\operatorname{Cone}(u_4, u_1)$. So the domains are $\operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_3)$, the merge of the two cones at $u_2$, and the two cones at $u_4$. This is the fan of $\mathbb{P}(1,1,n)$ of Example 3.1.26, and $\pi_R \colon \mathbb{F}_n \to \mathbb{P}(1,1,n)$ contracts $D_2$ to the singular point. Here $L = 0$, the curve $D_2$ is the whole exceptional locus, and $V(\sigma_-) = V(\rho_2) = D_2$, as Lemma 3.5.3 predicts.

**Example 3.5.8 (projective space and products).** On $\mathbb{P}^n$ every wall relation is $u_0 + \dots + u_n = 0$ (Example 3.4.6), so $NE(\mathbb{P}^n)$ is one ray with $J_+ = \Sigma(1)$ and $J_- = \emptyset$. A supporting divisor is $P = 0$, whose support function is $0$, so there is one domain $N_{\mathbb{R}}$, $L = N_{\mathbb{R}}$, and $\pi_R$ maps $\mathbb{P}^n$ to a point. On $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ the two rays of $NE$ are the classes of the two rulings, both with $J_- = \emptyset$, and the two contractions are the two projections.

**Example 3.5.9 (the blow-up of a point of $\mathbb{P}^3$).** Let $X$ be the star subdivision of the fan of $\mathbb{P}^3$ at $v = e_1 + e_2 + e_3$, as in Problem 3 of Chapter 4. The walls $\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_i)$ have the relation $e_1 + e_2 + e_3 - v = 0$, the class $\ell$ of a line in $E = D_v \cong \mathbb{P}^2$, and the walls $\operatorname{Cone}(e_i, e_j)$ with $1 \le i < j \le 3$ have the relation $e_0 + v = 0$, the class $\ell'$ of the strict transform of a line through the point. The rest of the walls, $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, e_i)$, have the relation $e_0 + e_1 + e_2 + e_3 = 0$ of a general line, whose class is $\ell + \ell'$. So $NE(X)$ has the two extremal rays $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \ell$ and $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \ell'$. For $\ell$, $J_- = \{v\}$, and $P = D_0$ (a plane not through the point) is a supporting divisor; its domains are the four cones of $\mathbb{P}^3$, and $\pi$ is the blow-down $X \to \mathbb{P}^3$. For $\ell'$, $J_- = \emptyset$ and $J_+ = \{e_0, v\}$, so $L \supset \mathbb{R} v$; a supporting divisor is $D_1 \sim D_0 - D_v$, the strict transform of a plane through the point, of class $H - E$, and $\pi$ is the projection $X \to \mathbb{P}^2$ from the point, with $N / \mathbb{Z} v$ carrying the fan of $\mathbb{P}^2$. This is the $\mathbb{P}^1$-bundle $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(1))$ over $\mathbb{P}^2$.

### Proof

*Proof of Lemma 3.5.2.* Since $NE(X)$ is spanned by the finitely many classes $[V(\tau)]$, a face $R$ of dimension one is spanned by the generators that lie in it, and there is at least one. An ample divisor $H$ exists since $X$ is projective, and $H \cdot V(\tau) > 0$, so $[V(\tau)] \neq 0$. This is part 1. Fix a nonzero $c \in R$ and let $b^R \in R(\Sigma)$ be its image under Theorem 3.4.20, part 3. For an $R$-wall $\tau$, $[V(\tau)]$ is a nonzero element of the ray $R$, so $[V(\tau)] = c_\tau c$ with $c_\tau > 0$, and the wall relation of $\tau$, which is the image of $[V(\tau)]$, is $c_\tau b^R$. The coordinate $b^R_\rho$ is $D_\rho \cdot c$, which gives the description of $J_\pm$. This is part 2.

For part 3, Theorem 3.4.5 says that the wall relation of $\tau$ has positive coefficients at $u_n$ and $u_{n+1}$ and coefficient zero at every ray that is not a ray of $\sigma$ or $\sigma'$. By part 2 the signs of the coefficients of the wall relation are the signs of $D_\rho \cdot R$. So $u_n$ and $u_{n+1}$ lie on rays of $J_+$, and the support $J_+ \cup J_-$ lies in $\sigma(1) \cup \sigma'(1) = \tau(1) \cup \{\rho_n, \rho_{n+1}\}$. A ray of $J_-$ is neither $\rho_n$ nor $\rho_{n+1}$, so it is a ray of $\tau$.

For part 4, the rays $\rho_n \neq \rho_{n+1}$ are in $J_+$. If $J_- \neq \emptyset$, the rays of $J_-$ are some of the rays of the simplicial cone $\tau$, so they span a face of $\tau$, which is a cone of $\Sigma$ of dimension $|J_-|$. $\square$

*Proof of Lemma 3.5.3.* Let $\rho \in J_-$, so $D_\rho \cdot C < 0$, and choose $r \ge 1$ with $r D_\rho$ Cartier, which is possible by Theorem 3.3.20. If $C \not\subset D_\rho$, then the restriction of the effective Cartier divisor $r D_\rho$ to $C$ is an effective Cartier divisor on $C$, so $r D_\rho \cdot C = \deg (\mathcal{O}(r D_\rho)|_C) \ge 0$, a contradiction. So $C \subset \bigcap_{\rho \in J_-} D_\rho$. By part 4 of Theorem 3.2.29, $D_\rho = V(\rho)$ is the union of the orbits $O(\delta)$ with $\rho \subset \delta$, so the intersection is the union of the orbits $O(\delta)$ for the cones $\delta$ that contain every ray of $J_-$. A cone of the simplicial fan $\Sigma$ that contains these rays contains $\sigma_-$, which is a cone of $\Sigma$ by Lemma 3.5.2. So the intersection is the union of the $O(\delta)$ with $\sigma_- \subset \delta$, which is $V(\sigma_-)$, of dimension $n - \dim \sigma_- = n - |J_-|$. $\square$

*Proof of Lemma 3.5.4.* The cone $NE(X)$ is rational polyhedral, by Theorem 3.4.21. A face is cut out by a form of the dual cone, so there is a linear form $\ell_0$ on $N_1(X)$ with $\ell_0 \ge 0$ on $NE(X)$ and $\ell_0^\perp \cap NE(X) = R$. The set $R^* = NE(X)^\vee \cap R^\perp$ is a face of the dual cone, which is rational by Theorem 3.1.3, so $R^*$ is a rational polyhedral cone by Proposition 3.1.4, and its relative interior contains a rational form $\ell$. Since $\ell_0 \in R^*$ and $\ell$ is in the relative interior, $\ell - \varepsilon \ell_0 \in R^*$ for small $\varepsilon > 0$. If $x \in NE(X)$ and $\ell(x) = 0$, then $0 = (\ell - \varepsilon \ell_0)(x) + \varepsilon \ell_0(x) \ge \varepsilon \ell_0(x) \ge 0$, so $\ell_0(x) = 0$ and $x \in R$. So $\ell$ is a rational form with $\ell \ge 0$ on $NE(X)$ and $\ell^\perp \cap NE(X) = R$. By Theorem 3.4.20, part 2, $N^1(X) = \operatorname{Cl}(X) \otimes \mathbb{R}$ is dual to $N_1(X)$, so $\ell$ is the class of a torus-invariant $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor $P$. It is nef because it is nonnegative on $NE(X)$. For a wall $\tau$, $P \cdot V(\tau) = 0$ if and only if $[V(\tau)] \in P^\perp \cap NE(X) = R$. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition 3.5.5.* By Theorem 3.4.19, $\varphi$ is convex, so by Proposition 3.4.12 $\varphi(x) = \min_\sigma \langle m_\sigma, x \rangle$. Then $\varphi(x) = \langle m, x \rangle$ holds if and only if $m$ attains the minimum at $x$, which is the description of $C_m$ in part 1. So $C_m$ is an intersection of finitely many rational half-spaces through $0$, a rational polyhedral cone. If $m_\sigma = m$ then $\varphi = m$ on $\sigma$, so $\sigma \subset C_m$, and $C_m$ has dimension $n$. Conversely, the maximal cones cover $N_{\mathbb{R}}$, so the interior of $C_m$ is covered by the maximal cones that meet it. If $\sigma$ meets the interior of $C_m$, then it meets it in a set with nonempty interior, since $\sigma$ is the closure of its interior; on that set $\langle m_\sigma, x \rangle = \varphi(x) = \langle m, x \rangle$, so $m_\sigma = m$. A convex cone of dimension $n$ is the closure of its interior, and a finite union of closed cones is closed, so $C_m$ is the union of the $\sigma$ with $m_\sigma = m$. This is part 1.

Part 2 is Theorem 3.4.4: $P \cdot V(\tau) = \langle m_\sigma - m_{\sigma'}, w' \rangle$ with $m_\sigma - m_{\sigma'} \in \tau^\perp$, where $w'$ is the image of a vector that is not in $\operatorname{span}(\tau)$. A form in $\tau^\perp$ that vanishes at that vector vanishes on $\operatorname{span}(\tau)$ and one more direction, so on $N_{\mathbb{R}}$. So $P \cdot V(\tau) = 0$ if and only if $m_\sigma = m_{\sigma'}$.

For part 3, write $M_0 = \{m_\sigma\}$. For every $x$ and every $m \in M_0$, $\varphi(x) + \varphi(-x) \le \langle m, x \rangle + \langle m, -x \rangle = 0$. In fact $\varphi(x) + \varphi(-x) = \min_{m} \langle m, x \rangle - \max_{m} \langle m, x \rangle$, which is zero if and only if all $\langle m, x \rangle$ with $m \in M_0$ are equal, that is, $x \in \bigcap (m - m')^\perp$. So $L$ is this intersection, a subspace defined over $\mathbb{Q}$. For $y \in L$ the number $\langle m, y \rangle$ is the same for all $m \in M_0$, so $\varphi(x + y) = \min_m (\langle m, x \rangle + \langle m, y \rangle) = \varphi(x) + \langle m, y \rangle$. By part 1, $C_m$ is the intersection of the half-spaces $\langle m' - m, x \rangle \ge 0$, so its largest subspace is $\bigcap_{m'} (m' - m)^\perp$, which equals $L$ because $m' - m'' = (m' - m) - (m'' - m)$.

For part 4, let $m, m' \in M_0$. On $C_m$ the form $m' - m$ is nonnegative, and $x \in C_m$ lies in $C_{m'}$ if and only if $\langle m' - m, x \rangle = 0$, since then $\varphi(x) = \langle m, x \rangle = \langle m', x \rangle$. So $C_m \cap C_{m'} = C_m \cap (m' - m)^\perp$ is a face of $C_m$, and by symmetry of $C_{m'}$. Let $F$ be a face of $C_m$ and $G$ a face of $C_{m'}$. Then $F \cap C_{m'}$ is the intersection of the two faces $F$ and $C_m \cap C_{m'}$ of $C_m$, so it is a face of $C_m$; it lies in the face $C_m \cap C_{m'}$ of $C_{m'}$, so it is a face of $C_{m'}$, and then $F \cap G = (F \cap C_{m'}) \cap G$ is an intersection of two faces of $C_{m'}$ contained in $G$, so a face of $G$. By symmetry it is a face of $F$. The domains cover $N_{\mathbb{R}}$ since the maximal cones do. A cone is strongly convex if and only if its largest subspace is $0$, which by part 3 is $L$ for each domain, and every face of a domain contains $L$ as well. Each cone of $\Sigma$ is a face of a maximal cone, which lies in a domain by part 1. A face $F = C_m \cap H$ of a domain, cut out by a supporting hyperplane $H$, is the union of the sets $\sigma \cap H$ for the maximal cones $\sigma \subset C_m$, and $H$ supports each such $\sigma$, so $\sigma \cap H$ is a face of $\sigma$ and a cone of $\Sigma$.

For part 5, if $\sigma_i$ and $\sigma_{i+1}$ share a wall with $P \cdot V(\tau_i) = 0$, then $m_{\sigma_i} = m_{\sigma_{i+1}}$ by part 2, so a chain stays in one domain. Conversely, let $\sigma, \sigma'' \subset C_m$, and join an interior point of $\sigma$ to an interior point of $\sigma''$ by a generic segment, as in the proof of Theorem 3.4.14. The segment lies in $C_m$ by convexity, and it passes through a sequence of maximal cones $\sigma_1 = \sigma, \sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_r = \sigma''$, crossing from each to the next through the relative interior of a common wall. Each $\sigma_i$ contains a point of the segment that lies in no other maximal cone, so $\sigma_i$ meets the interior of $C_m$, and $m_{\sigma_i} = m$ by the proof of part 1. So consecutive cones have the same form, and by part 2 their common wall has $P \cdot V(\tau_i) = 0$. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 3.5.6.* Part 1. By part 3 of Proposition 3.5.5 every cone of $\Sigma_R$ contains $L$ and is the preimage of its image in $N_{\mathbb{R}} / L$. So the images are rational polyhedral cones whose largest subspace is $0$, they meet in faces because the cones of $\Sigma_R$ do, and they cover $N_{\mathbb{R}} / L$. The lattice $N \cap L$ is saturated in $N$, so $N_Z$ is a lattice, and $(N_Z)_{\mathbb{R}} = N_{\mathbb{R}} / L$ since $L$ is rational. So $\Sigma_Z$ is a complete fan. Each cone of $\Sigma$ lies in a cone of $\Sigma_R$, so its image lies in a cone of $\Sigma_Z$, and the projection is compatible. Theorem 3.2.37 gives the toric morphism $\pi_R$, and Theorem 3.2.38 says that it is proper, since the preimage of $|\Sigma_Z| = (N_Z)_{\mathbb{R}}$ is $N_{\mathbb{R}} = |\Sigma|$. The map of tori $T_N \to T_{N_Z}$ is surjective, because $N \to N_Z$ is, so the image of $\pi_R$ is closed and contains the dense torus of $Z$, and $\pi_R$ is surjective.

Part 2. Fix $m_0 \in M_{\mathbb{Q}}$ that agrees on $L$ with the common restriction of the forms $m_\sigma$; it exists since $L$ is rational. By part 3 of Proposition 3.5.5, $\psi = \varphi - m_0$ is invariant under translation by $L$, so it is a function on $N_{\mathbb{R}} / L$. On the image of each domain $C_m$ it is the linear form $m - m_0$, which vanishes on $L$ and so lies in $L^\perp = (M_Z)_{\mathbb{R}}$, the dual of $(N_Z)_{\mathbb{R}}$. The forms $m - m_0$ for different domains are different, and $\psi$ is the minimum of them, so $\psi$ is a strictly convex support function of $\Sigma_Z$ with rational data. By Cox-Little-Schenck, Theorem 6.1.14, the torus-invariant $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor $A$ on $Z$ with support function $\psi$ is ample. On the open set $U_{C}$ of a maximal cone $C$ of $\Sigma_Z$, $A$ is the divisor of the rational function $\chi^{-(m - m_0)}$ up to a factor. The morphism $\pi_R$ pulls back characters to characters by the dual map $M_Z \to M$, so on $\pi_R^{-1}(U_C)$, which contains $U_\sigma$ for each maximal cone $\sigma \subset C_m$, $\pi_R^* A$ is the divisor of $\chi^{-(m - m_0)}$. So $\pi_R^* A$ has the data $m_\sigma - m_0$ and the support function $\varphi - m_0$, and $\pi_R^* A = P + \operatorname{div}(\chi^{m_0})$ as $\mathbb{Q}$-divisors, by Proposition 3.3.3. Since $A$ is ample, $Z$ is projective.

Part 3. Let $C$ be an irreducible curve. By the projection formula, $P \cdot C = \pi_R^* A \cdot C = A \cdot \pi_{R*} C$. If $\pi_R(C)$ is a point, then $\pi_{R*} C = 0$, so $P \cdot C = 0$ and $[C] \in R$. If $\pi_R(C)$ is a curve, then $\pi_{R*} C$ is a positive multiple of it and $A \cdot \pi_{R*} C > 0$ since $A$ is ample, so $P \cdot C > 0$ and $[C] \notin R$. The statement for wall curves is the case $C = V(\tau)$ with Lemma 3.5.4.

Part 4. $\dim Z = \operatorname{rank} N_Z = n - \dim L$. If $L = 0$, the lattice map is the identity, which restricts to an isomorphism of tori, so $\pi_R$ is birational. If $L \neq 0$, then $\dim Z < n$ and $\pi_R$ is not birational.

Part 5. Assume $J_- = \emptyset$, and write $P = \sum_\rho a_\rho D_\rho$, so $a_\rho = -\varphi(u_\rho)$. The relation $b^R$ has support $J_+$ and positive coefficients there, and $P \cdot R = 0$ says $\sum_{\rho \in J_+} b^R_\rho a_\rho = 0$, that is, $\sum_{J_+} b^R_\rho \varphi(u_\rho) = 0$. A minimum of linear forms satisfies $\varphi(x + y) \ge \varphi(x) + \varphi(y)$ and $\varphi(s x) = s \varphi(x)$ for $s \ge 0$. Fix $\rho \in J_+$. Then $-u_\rho = \sum_{\rho' \in J_+ \setminus \{\rho\}} (b^R_{\rho'} / b^R_\rho) u_{\rho'}$, so

$$\varphi(-u_\rho) \ge \frac{1}{b^R_\rho} \sum_{\rho' \neq \rho} b^R_{\rho'} \varphi(u_{\rho'}) = \frac{1}{b^R_\rho} \big( 0 - b^R_\rho \varphi(u_\rho) \big) = -\varphi(u_\rho).$$

So $\varphi(u_\rho) + \varphi(-u_\rho) \ge 0$, and the reverse inequality always holds, so $u_\rho \in L$. Since $J_+ \neq \emptyset$, $L \neq 0$.

Conversely, assume $L \neq 0$ and $J_- \neq \emptyset$. By part 4, $\dim Z < n$, so every fibre of the surjective morphism $\pi_R$ has dimension at least $n - \dim Z \ge 1$ at each of its points (Hartshorne, Exercise II.3.22). So each point $x \in X$ lies on an irreducible curve contracted by $\pi_R$, whose class is in $R$ by part 3, and which lies in $V(\sigma_-)$ by Lemma 3.5.3. Then $X = V(\sigma_-)$, which is false since $\dim V(\sigma_-) = n - |J_-| < n$.

Part 6 follows from part 5 of Proposition 3.5.5 and Lemma 3.5.4, since the condition $P \cdot V(\tau) = 0$ means that $\tau$ is an $R$-wall for every supporting divisor $P$. $\square$

### Notes and history

The contraction of an extremal ray of a projective toric variety is in Reid's paper of 1983, which the map cites for the toric contractions and flips, and in Cox-Little-Schenck, Section 15.4. Fujino and Sato (2004, Theorem 4.5) prove the contraction theorem for an extremal face of $NE(X/Y)$ for every projective toric morphism $X \to Y$, with no simplicial hypothesis. Their proof takes a nef torus-invariant Cartier divisor that supports the face and applies their Proposition 4.6: such a divisor is relatively free, and the morphism $X \to \operatorname{Proj}_Y \bigoplus_m f_* \mathcal{O}_X(mD)$ has connected fibres and contracts exactly the curves on which $D$ is zero. The last part of their Theorem 4.5, that the target is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial with Picard number one less when the contraction is not small, they attribute to Kollár-Mori, Proposition 3.36. The fan construction of this section, the domains of linearity of the support function, is the toric form of the same morphism. The proof of part 5 of Theorem 3.5.6 by the superadditivity of $\varphi$ is written for this handbook.
