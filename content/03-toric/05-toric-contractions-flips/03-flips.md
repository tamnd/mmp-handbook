## Toric flips by wall crossing

### Statement

A small contraction cannot be a step of the minimal model program by itself, since its target is not $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial (Theorem 3.5.15). The program replaces the contracted locus by a new one on the other side. For toric varieties the new side is the other triangulation of each circuit cone, and this section proves that it has the properties of a flip.

**Definition 3.5.19 (flips).** Let $\pi \colon X \to Z$ be a small proper birational morphism of normal varieties, and $D$ a $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor on $X$ with $-D$ $\pi$-ample. A *$D$-flip* of $\pi$ is a small proper birational morphism $\pi^+ \colon X^+ \to Z$ from a normal variety $X^+$ such that the strict transform $D^+$ of $D$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier and $\pi^+$-ample. When $D = K_X$ it is a *flip*, when $K_X$ is $\pi$-trivial it is a *flop*, and when $-K_X$ is $\pi$-ample, the $D$-flip for $D = -K_X$ is an *antiflip*.

**Theorem 3.5.20 (toric flips).** Let $\Sigma$ be complete simplicial, $X = X_\Sigma$ projective, $R$ an extremal ray with $|J_-| \ge 2$, $b^R$ its relation, $\pi \colon X \to Z_R = X_{\Sigma_R}$ the small contraction of $R$ (Theorem 3.5.15), $\sigma_- = \operatorname{Cone}(u_\rho : \rho \in J_-)$ and $\sigma_+ = \operatorname{Cone}(u_\rho : \rho \in J_+)$. Let $\Sigma^+$ be the set of the cones of $\Sigma$ that lie in a maximal cone of $\Sigma_R$ of the first kind, together with the cones of $T_-(W_C)$, for the circuit data $(W_C, b^R)$, for all maximal cones $C$ of $\Sigma_R$ of the second kind (part 1 of Theorem 3.5.15).

1. $\Sigma^+$ is a complete simplicial fan with $\Sigma^+(1) = \Sigma(1)$, each of its cones lies in a cone of $\Sigma_R$, and the identity of $N$ gives a proper birational toric morphism $\pi^+ \colon X^+ = X_{\Sigma^+} \to Z_R$.
2. The cones of $\Sigma$ that are not in $\Sigma^+$ are those that contain $\sigma_-$, and the cones of $\Sigma^+$ that are not in $\Sigma$ are those that contain $\sigma_+$. So $X \setminus V(\sigma_-)$ and $X^+ \setminus V^+(\sigma_+)$ are the same open set $X_{\Sigma \cap \Sigma^+}$, over $Z_R$. The exceptional locus of $\pi^+$ is $V^+(\sigma_+)$, of dimension $n - |J_+| \le n - 2$, so $\pi^+$ is small. $X^+$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial with $\rho(X^+) = \rho(X)$.
3. Identify $N_1(X)$ and $N_1(X^+)$ with $R(\Sigma) = R(\Sigma^+)$ (Theorem 3.4.20; the two fans have the same rays). A wall curve $V^+(\tau)$ of $X^+$ is contracted by $\pi^+$ if and only if $\tau$ lies in the interior of a maximal cone of $\Sigma_R$, and then its class is a positive multiple of $-b^R$. The curves contracted by $\pi^+$ are the curves with class in $R^+ = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} (-b^R)$, and $R^+$ is an extremal ray of $NE(X^+)$.
4. Let $D = \sum_\rho a_\rho D_\rho$ be a torus-invariant $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor on $X$ with $D \cdot R < 0$, that is, $\sum_\rho a_\rho b^R_\rho < 0$, and let $D^+ = \sum_\rho a_\rho D^+_\rho$ be its strict transform. Then $-D$ is $\pi$-ample, $D^+$ has positive intersection with every wall curve of $X^+$ that $\pi^+$ contracts, $D^+$ is $\pi^+$-ample, and $X^+$ is projective. So $\pi^+$ is the $D$-flip of $\pi$.

The fan $\Sigma^+$ depends only on $R$, so the $D$-flip is the same for every $D$ with $D \cdot R < 0$. Since $-b^R$ is the relation of $R^+$, the circuit cones of $\Sigma^+$ for $R^+$ are the same cones $C$ with the roles of $J_+$ and $J_-$ exchanged, and the flip of $R^+$ in $X^+$ is $X$ again (Exercise 4).

**Proposition 3.5.21 (uniqueness of flips).** In Definition 3.5.19, assume that $Z$ is normal. Let $D_Z = \pi_* D$, and let $m > 0$ be an integer such that $mD$ and $mD^+$ are Cartier. Then

$$X^+ \cong \operatorname{Proj}_Z \bigoplus_{k \ge 0} \mathcal{O}_Z(k m D_Z)$$

over $Z$. In particular a $D$-flip, if it exists, is unique up to a unique isomorphism over $Z$, and the toric flip of Theorem 3.5.20 is the $D$-flip.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

**Corollary 3.5.22 (the local flip of a circuit).** Let $(W, q)$ be circuit data with $|J_+| \ge 2$ and $|J_-| \ge 2$, and $\pi_\pm \colon X_\pm = X_{T_\pm} \to U = U_{\operatorname{Cone}(W)}$ as in Theorem 3.5.14. Let $D = \sum_i a_i D_{w_i}$ be a torus-invariant $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor with $\sum_i a_i q_i < 0$. Then $-D$ is ample on $X_+$, $D$ is ample on $X_-$, and $\pi_-$ is the $D$-flip of $\pi_+$. If $J_0 = \emptyset$, the exceptional loci are $V_+(\sigma_-)$, of dimension $|J_+| - 1$, and $V_-(\sigma_+)$, of dimension $|J_-| - 1$, both over the fixed point of $U$.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

### Proof in words

The domain fan $\Sigma_R$ is $\Sigma$ with the circuit cones glued together. Each circuit cone has two triangulations, and $\Sigma$ uses $T_+$ on all of them; the flip uses $T_-$ on all of them. These choices agree on the common faces of two circuit cones, so they make one fan, with the same rays as before since each triangulation uses all the vectors when $|J_\pm| \ge 2$.

The new interior walls have the relation $-b^R$, so every curve that $\pi^+$ contracts has class on the opposite ray. A divisor $D$ that is negative on $R$ is then positive on $R^+$. Add a large multiple of the supporting divisor $P$, which is positive on the remaining walls and zero on the contracted ones: the sum is positive on every wall of $\Sigma^+$, so it is ample. Since $P$ comes from $Z_R$, $D^+$ is ample relative to $Z_R$.

For uniqueness, a small morphism does not change the sections of a divisorial sheaf, because those extend over subsets of codimension two. So $X^+$ is the relative Proj of a ring that only sees $Z$ and the pushforward of $D$.

### Examples

**Example 3.5.23 (the Atiyah flop).** Take the cone over a square of Example 3.5.16: $u_1 = (0,0,1)$, $u_2 = (1,0,1)$, $u_3 = (1,1,1)$, $u_4 = (0,1,1)$ and $q = (1, -1, 1, -1)$. The fan $T_+$ has the interior wall $\operatorname{Cone}(u_2, u_4)$ with the curve $C_+ = V_+(\operatorname{Cone}(u_2, u_4))$, and $T_-$ has the interior wall $\operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_3)$ with the curve $C_-$. All cones are smooth, so the wall relations are normalized with coefficient $1$ at the two vectors outside the wall (Theorem 3.4.5): they are $q$ for $C_+$ and $-q$ for $C_-$. So

$$(D_1, D_2, D_3, D_4) \cdot C_+ = (1, -1, 1, -1), \qquad (D_1, D_2, D_3, D_4) \cdot C_- = (-1, 1, -1, 1),$$

and $K \cdot C_\pm = -\sum_i (\pm q_i) = 0$. The curve $C_+ = D_2 \cap D_4$ is a $\mathbb{P}^1$ with normal bundle $\mathcal{O}(-1) \oplus \mathcal{O}(-1)$, the restriction of $\mathcal{O}(D_2) \oplus \mathcal{O}(D_4)$. By Corollary 3.5.22 with $D = D_2$, the variety $X_-$ is the $D_2$-flip of $X_+$. Since $K$ is trivial on both curves it is a flop: the Atiyah flop, the passage between the two small resolutions of the cone $xy = zw$ over a quadric surface. The two triangulations of the square are the two diagonals, and the flop is the move from one diagonal to the other.

**Example 3.5.24 (the Francia flip).** Let $q = (1, 1, -1, -2)$, $N = \mathbb{Z}^4 / \mathbb{Z} q$ and $W = \{u_1, u_2, u_3, u_4\}$ the images of the basis. In the basis $u_2, u_3, u_4$ of $N$ one has $u_1 = -u_2 + u_3 + 2 u_4$. Here $J_+ = \{1, 2\}$, $J_- = \{3, 4\}$ and $J_0 = \emptyset$. By Proposition 3.5.13:

- $T_+$ has the cones $\operatorname{Cone}(u_2, u_3, u_4)$ and $\operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_3, u_4)$, of multiplicities $|q_1| = |q_2| = 1$, so $X_+$ is smooth;
- $T_-$ has the cones $\operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2, u_4)$ and $\operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2, u_3)$, of multiplicities $|q_3| = 1$ and $|q_4| = 2$.

In the lattice generated by $u_1, u_2, u_3$, which has index $2$ in $N$, the vector $u_4 = \frac12 (u_1 + u_2 - u_3)$ generates $N$ modulo that lattice, and $\frac12(1, 1, -1) \equiv \frac12(1, 1, 1)$ modulo $\mathbb{Z}^3$. So $\operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2, u_3)$ is the cone of the quotient singularity $\frac12(1,1,1)$ of Example 3.3.24, and $X_-$ has one such point.

The curve $C_+ = V_+(\operatorname{Cone}(u_3, u_4))$ lies between two smooth cones, so its wall relation is $q$:

$$(D_1, D_2, D_3, D_4) \cdot C_+ = (1, 1, -1, -2), \qquad K \cdot C_+ = -(1 + 1 - 1 - 2) = 1.$$

It is a $\mathbb{P}^1$ with normal bundle $\mathcal{O}(-1) \oplus \mathcal{O}(-2)$. The curve $C_- = V_-(\operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2))$ lies between $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2, u_4)$, of multiplicity $1$, and $\sigma' = \operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2, u_3)$, of multiplicity $2$, and $\operatorname{mult} \operatorname{Cone}(u_1, u_2) = 1$. By Theorem 3.4.5 its wall relation is the multiple of $-q$ with coefficient $1$ at $u_4$ and $\frac12$ at $u_3$, which is $-\frac12 q$:

$$(D_1, D_2, D_3, D_4) \cdot C_- = \big(-\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 1\big), \qquad K \cdot C_- = -\tfrac12.$$

So $K$ is negative on $C_-$ and positive on $C_+$. By Corollary 3.5.22 with $-q$ in place of $q$ and $D = K = -\sum_i D_i$, for which $\sum_i (-1)(-q_i) = -1 < 0$, the variety $X_+$ is the $K$-flip of $X_-$: the flip goes from the singular side, with the $\frac12(1,1,1)$ point, to the smooth side. This is the Francia flip. A $K$-flip can thus make a variety less singular. On the smooth side the flipped curve has $K \cdot C_+ > 0$, as the flipped curve of every $K$-flip must.

**Example 3.5.25 (the flip in the weighted blow-up).** Let $X$ be the $(1, 2, 3)$ weighted blow-up of $\mathbb{P}^3$ of Example 3.5.18, with the small extremal ray $R = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}[C_{23}]$ and $b^R = (1, 1, -1, -2)$ on $(e_0, v, e_2, e_3)$. The circuit cone is $C = \operatorname{Cone}(e_0, v, e_2, e_3)$; the four vectors generate $\mathbb{Z}^3$, since $e_1 = -e_0 - e_2 - e_3$, and $b^R$ is primitive. This is the configuration of the Francia flip, with $(u_1, u_2, u_3, u_4) = (e_0, v, e_2, e_3)$. The fan $\Sigma^+$ of Theorem 3.5.20 replaces the two cones $\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_2, e_3)$ and $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, e_2, e_3)$ of $T_+(W_C)$ by the cones $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, v, e_3)$ and $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, v, e_2)$ of $T_-(W_C)$, of multiplicities $1$ and $2$, and keeps the other cones of $\Sigma$. Since $D_{e_2} \cdot C_{23} = -1$ and $D_{e_3} \cdot C_{23} = -2$, $X^+$ is the $D_{e_2}$-flip and the $D_{e_3}$-flip of the contraction of $C_{23}$. Since $K \cdot C_{23} = 1$, it is also the $(-K)$-flip, an antiflip. On $X^+$ the new curve $C^+ = V^+(\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, v))$ has $K \cdot C^+ = -\frac12$ as in Example 3.5.24, and $X^+$ has a point of type $\frac12(1,1,1)$, at the fixed point of $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, v, e_2)$. By the remark after Theorem 3.5.20, the $K$-flip of $C^+$ in $X^+$ is $X$. Chapter 6 runs the $K$-program on $X$, where this ray does not appear, since $K \cdot C_{23} > 0$.

### Proof

*Proof of Theorem 3.5.20.* Part 1. For each maximal cone $C$ of $\Sigma_R$, let $\Sigma^+_C$ be the set of cones of $\Sigma^+$ that lie in $C$: the cones of $T_-(W_C)$ if $C$ is of the second kind, and the faces of $C$ if $C$ is a cone of $\Sigma$. In both cases $\Sigma^+_C$ is a simplicial fan with support $C$, by Lemma 3.5.11. Let $G$ be a face of $C$. We claim that the cones of $\Sigma^+_C$ in $G$ are the cones of $\Sigma$ in $G$ if $G \not\supset Z$, and the cones $\operatorname{Cone}(A)$ with $A \subset W_C \cap G$ not containing all of $\{u_\rho : \rho \in J_-\}$ if $G \supset Z$. If $C$ is a cone of $\Sigma$, then $G$ is a cone of $\Sigma$, so it does not contain the dependent set $Z$, and the cones of $\Sigma^+_C$ in $G$ are its faces. If $C$ is of the second kind and $G \not\supset Z$, then $G$ is a face of the first kind by Proposition 3.5.12, its cones in $T_-$ are the cones of subsets of $W_C \cap G$, and these lie in $T_+ \cap T_-$, so they are the cones of $\Sigma$ in $G$. If $G \supset Z$, the cones of $T_-(W_C)$ in $G$ are the $\operatorname{Cone}(A)$ with $A \subset W_C$ not containing all of $\{u_\rho : \rho \in J_-\}$ and $\operatorname{Cone}(A) \subset G$, that is, $A \subset W_C \cap G$, since the form that cuts out $G$ is positive on the other vectors of $W_C$. In both cases the description depends only on $G$ and on the rays of $\Sigma$ in $G$, not on $C$.

Now let $\delta \in \Sigma^+_C$ and $\delta' \in \Sigma^+_{C'}$, and $G = C \cap C'$, a face of both since $\Sigma_R$ is a fan. Then $\delta \cap G$ is a face of $\delta$, since $G$ is a face of $C \supset \delta$, and it is a cone of $\Sigma^+_C$ in $G$; likewise for $\delta'$. By the claim both lie in the same set of cones, the cones of $\Sigma^+_C$ in $G$, which is a fan with support $G$ because the cones of the fan $\Sigma^+_C$ meet $G$ in faces. So $\delta \cap \delta' = (\delta \cap G) \cap (\delta' \cap G)$ is a face of $\delta \cap G$ and of $\delta' \cap G$, hence of $\delta$ and $\delta'$. The faces of a cone of $\Sigma^+$ are in $\Sigma^+$. So $\Sigma^+$ is a fan; it is simplicial, and its support is the union of the cones $C$, which is $N_{\mathbb{R}}$. Each vector of $W_C$ spans a cone of $T_-(W_C)$, since a single vector misses some $u_\rho$ with $\rho \in J_-$ when $|J_-| \ge 2$; so the rays of $\Sigma^+$ in $C$ are those of $\Sigma$, and $\Sigma^+(1) = \Sigma(1)$. Each cone of $\Sigma^+$ lies in a cone of $\Sigma_R$, and each cone of $\Sigma_R$ is a union of cones of $\Sigma^+$; so the identity of $N$ is compatible with the two fans and gives a proper toric morphism by Theorem 3.2.38, birational since the lattice map is the identity.

Part 2. A cone of $\Sigma$ in a maximal cone of the first kind is in $\Sigma^+$. A cone of $\Sigma$ in a circuit cone $C$ is $\operatorname{Cone}(A)$ with $A \subset W_C$ not containing all of $\{u_\rho : \rho \in J_+\}$, and it is in $\Sigma^+$ if and only if $A$ also does not contain all of $\{u_\rho : \rho \in J_-\}$. So a cone of $\Sigma$ that is not in $\Sigma^+$ contains $\sigma_-$. Conversely, let $\delta \in \Sigma$ contain $\sigma_-$ and let $\sigma \supset \delta$ be a maximal cone of $\Sigma$. If $\sigma$ were a maximal cone of $\Sigma_R$ of the first kind, then $\pi$ would be an isomorphism over the open set $U_\sigma$ of $Z_R$, by Corollary 3.2.39, since $\sigma$ is the only maximal cone of $\Sigma$ in it; but the fixed point of $\sigma$ lies in $V(\delta) \subset V(\sigma_-)$, the exceptional locus of $\pi$ (Theorem 3.5.15). So $\sigma \subset C$ for a circuit cone $C$, $\delta = \operatorname{Cone}(A)$ with $A \subset W_C$ containing $\sigma_-$, and $\delta \notin T_-(W_C)$. As $\delta \subset C$, and the cones of $\Sigma^+$ in $C$ are those of $T_-(W_C)$, $\delta \notin \Sigma^+$. No cone of $\Sigma$ contains $\sigma_+$. Indeed, let $\delta \in \Sigma$ contain $\sigma_+$, and let $C$ be a circuit cone; it contains $\sigma_+$. The smallest cone $F$ of $\Sigma_R$ that contains $\delta$ meets $C$ in a face of $C$ that contains $\sigma_+$, so it contains $Z$, by the proof of Proposition 3.5.12 with $J_+$ and $J_-$ exchanged. Then $F \supset Z$, so by part 2 of Theorem 3.5.15 the cones of $\Sigma$ in $F$ do not contain all of $\{u_\rho : \rho \in J_+\}$, a contradiction. Now a cone of $\Sigma^+$ in a maximal cone of the first kind is in $\Sigma$, and a cone $\operatorname{Cone}(A)$ of $T_-(W_C)$ is in $T_+(W_C)$ unless $A$ contains all of $\{u_\rho : \rho \in J_+\}$. So the cones of $\Sigma^+ \setminus \Sigma$ contain $\sigma_+$, and conversely the cones of $\Sigma^+$ that contain $\sigma_+$ are not in $\Sigma$.

The cones of $\Sigma_R$ that do not contain $Z$ are cones of $\Sigma$ (part 2 of Theorem 3.5.15) and of $\Sigma^+$ (by the claim in part 1). By Corollary 3.2.39 and Theorem 3.2.29, $\pi$ and $\pi^+$ are isomorphisms over the open set $Z_R \setminus V(\operatorname{Cone}(Z))$, which is the union of the $U_F$ for these cones. The orbits of $X$ over $V(\operatorname{Cone}(Z))$ are the $O(\delta)$ for which the smallest cone of $\Sigma_R$ containing $\delta$ contains $Z$ (Theorem 3.2.37, part 3); these are the cones $\operatorname{Cone}(A)$ of $T_+(W_C)$ that are not faces of the first kind, that is, the cones that contain $\sigma_-$. So $\pi^{-1}(V(\operatorname{Cone}(Z))) = V(\sigma_-)$, and in the same way $(\pi^+)^{-1}(V(\operatorname{Cone}(Z))) = V^+(\sigma_+)$. Both complements are the toric variety of $\Sigma \cap \Sigma^+$ and map isomorphically onto $Z_R \setminus V(\operatorname{Cone}(Z))$. As in the proof of Theorem 3.5.15, part 3, $\pi^+$ maps $V^+(\sigma_+)$, of dimension $n - |J_+|$, onto $V(\operatorname{Cone}(Z))$, of dimension $n - |Z| + 1$, with fibres of dimension at least $|J_-| - 1 \ge 1$. So the exceptional locus of $\pi^+$ is $V^+(\sigma_+)$. So $\pi^+$ is small, as $|J_+| \ge 2$. $X^+$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial by Theorem 3.3.20, and $\rho(X^+) = |\Sigma(1)| - n = \rho(X)$ by Theorem 3.4.20.

Part 3. Let $P$ be a supporting divisor of $R$ with support function $\varphi$, and $P^+$ the divisor on $X^+$ with the same coefficients. The function $\varphi$ is linear on each cone of $\Sigma_R$, so on each cone of $\Sigma^+$, and it takes the value $-p_\rho$ at $u_\rho$, where $P = \sum_\rho p_\rho D_\rho$; so it is the support function of $P^+$. It is convex, so $P^+$ is nef by Theorem 3.4.19. For a wall $\tau$ of $\Sigma^+$ between maximal cones $\delta \subset C$ and $\delta' \subset C'$, the data of $P^+$ on $\delta$ and $\delta'$ are the forms $m_C$ and $m_{C'}$ of the domains. By the argument for part 2 of Proposition 3.5.5, which uses only Theorem 3.4.4, $P^+ \cdot V^+(\tau) = 0$ if and only if $m_C = m_{C'}$, that is, $C = C'$. A maximal cone of the first kind contains one maximal cone of $\Sigma^+$, so $C = C'$ means that $C$ is a circuit cone and $\tau$ is an interior wall of $T_-(W_C)$, whose relation is a positive multiple of $-b^R$ by Lemma 3.5.11, part 4. As in the proof of part 2 of Theorem 3.5.6, with $L = 0$, $P^+ = (\pi^+)^* A$ for the ample divisor $A$ on $Z_R$ with support function $\varphi$, so by the projection formula an irreducible curve $C^+ \subset X^+$ is contracted by $\pi^+$ if and only if $P^+ \cdot C^+ = 0$. By Theorem 3.4.21 for $X^+$, the face $(P^+)^\perp \cap NE(X^+)$ is spanned by the classes of the wall curves on which $P^+$ vanishes, which all lie on $R^+$. So it is $R^+$, an extremal ray, and the contracted curves are those with class in $R^+$.

Part 4. On $X$, the $R$-walls are the walls on which $P$ vanishes (Lemma 3.5.4), and $-D \cdot V(\tau) = -c_\tau \sum_\rho a_\rho b^R_\rho > 0$ on them. Let $t_0$ be larger than $|D \cdot V(\tau)| / P \cdot V(\tau)$ for each of the finitely many walls $\tau$ with $P \cdot V(\tau) > 0$. Then $t_0 P - D$ is positive on every wall of $\Sigma$, so its support function is strictly convex by Theorem 3.4.13 and it is ample by Cox-Little-Schenck, Theorem 6.1.14. As $t_0 P = \pi^*(t_0 A)$, the divisor $-D$ differs from an ample divisor by a pullback from $Z_R$, so it is $\pi$-ample (Lazarsfeld, Section 1.7). On $X^+$, the walls with $P^+ \cdot V^+(\tau) = 0$ have relation $c(-b^R)$ with $c > 0$, so $D^+ \cdot V^+(\tau) = -c \sum_\rho a_\rho b^R_\rho > 0$. Choose $t$ as before for the walls of $\Sigma^+$ with $P^+ \cdot V^+(\tau) > 0$. Then $H^+ = t P^+ + D^+$ is positive on every wall of $\Sigma^+$, so it is ample by the same two theorems, and $X^+$ is projective. Since $t P^+ = (\pi^+)^*(t A)$, $D^+$ is $\pi^+$-ample. $D^+$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier since $\Sigma^+$ is simplicial. Together with parts 1 and 2, $\pi^+$ is a $D$-flip of $\pi$. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition 3.5.21.* We follow Kollár-Mori, Lemma 6.2, which Fujino and Sato quote in their Theorem 4.8 for the toric case. Since $\pi^+$ is small, there are closed subsets $E \subset X$ and $E^+ \subset X^+$ of codimension at least $2$, and $F \subset Z$ of codimension at least $2$, with $\pi^{-1}(F) = E$, $(\pi^+)^{-1}(F) = E^+$, and $\pi$, $\pi^+$ isomorphisms over $Z \setminus F$. For each $k$, $\mathcal{O}_{X^+}(kmD^+)$ is an invertible sheaf on the normal variety $X^+$, so a section of it over $(\pi^+)^{-1}(V) \setminus E^+$, for an open $V \subset Z$, extends over $E^+$. So $\pi^+_* \mathcal{O}_{X^+}(kmD^+)$ is the sheaf of sections of $\mathcal{O}_Z(kmD_Z)$ over $V \setminus F$, which is $\mathcal{O}_Z(kmD_Z)$ because the divisorial sheaf $\mathcal{O}_Z(kmD_Z)$ on the normal variety $Z$ has the same extension property. Since $mD^+$ is $\pi^+$-ample, $X^+ = \operatorname{Proj}_Z \bigoplus_k \pi^+_* \mathcal{O}_{X^+}(kmD^+)$, and this is the stated algebra. It depends only on $Z$ and $D_Z$, which gives uniqueness. For the toric flip, $Z = Z_R$ is normal and $\pi^+$ is a $D$-flip by Theorem 3.5.20. $\square$

*Proof of Corollary 3.5.22.* Write $\sigma_k = \operatorname{Cone}(W \setminus \{w_k\})$ for $k \in J_-$, and let $m_k \in M_{\mathbb{Q}}$ be the data of $D$ on $\sigma_k$, so $\langle m_k, w_i \rangle = -a_i$ for $i \neq k$. Let $k' \neq k$ in $J_-$. Pairing $m_{k'}$ with the relation gives $q_{k'} \langle m_{k'}, w_{k'} \rangle = \sum_{i \neq k'} q_i a_i$, so

$$\langle m_{k'} - m_k, w_{k'} \rangle = \frac{\sum_i a_i q_i}{q_{k'}} > 0,$$

since numerator and denominator are negative. The form $m_{k'} - m_k$ vanishes on $W \setminus \{w_k, w_{k'}\}$. A point $x$ of $\sigma_k$ is $x = \sum_{i \neq k} \lambda_i w_i$ with $\lambda \ge 0$, and $\langle m_{k'} - m_k, x \rangle = \lambda_{k'} \sum_i a_i q_i / q_{k'} \ge 0$, with equality only on the facet $\lambda_{k'} = 0$. So the support function $\varphi$ of $D$ on $T_-$ is $\min_k m_k$, convex, with different forms on different maximal cones: it is strictly convex. The support $\operatorname{Cone}(W)$ is convex of dimension $n$, so $D$ is ample on $X_-$ by Cox-Little-Schenck, Theorem 6.1.14. The same computation on $T_+$, with $j, j' \in J_+$ and $-D$ in place of $D$, gives $\langle m_{j'} - m_j, w_{j'} \rangle = -\sum_i a_i q_i / q_{j'} > 0$ for the data of $-D$, so $-D$ is ample on $X_+$. Over the affine variety $U$, ampleness and $\pi_\pm$-ampleness agree. The morphisms $\pi_\pm$ are small by Theorem 3.5.14, and $D$ is the strict transform of itself since $T_+$ and $T_-$ have the same rays. So $\pi_-$ is the $D$-flip of $\pi_+$. If $J_0 = \emptyset$, then $\operatorname{Cone}(Z) = \operatorname{Cone}(W)$, $V_U(\operatorname{Cone}(W))$ is the fixed point, and the dimensions are $n - |J_-| = |J_+| - 1$ and $n - |J_+| = |J_-| - 1$ by Theorem 3.5.14. $\square$

3. (B) Let $q = (1, 1, 1, -1, -1)$, $N = \mathbb{Z}^5 / \mathbb{Z} q$ and $W$ the images of the basis. Show that $X_\pm$ are smooth, that the exceptional locus of $\pi_+$ is a surface $\mathbb{P}^2$ and that of $\pi_-$ a curve $\mathbb{P}^1$, and decide which of $X_\pm$ is the $K$-flip of the other.
4. (B) In Theorem 3.5.20, show that $R^+$ has the relation $-b^R$, the same circuit cones $C$ and the sign sets $J_\mp$, and deduce that the flip of $R^+$ in $X^+$ is $X$.
5. (B) In Example 3.5.25, show that $\sum_\rho D_\rho \cdot C^+ = \frac12$, and check that $C^+$ is the only curve of $X^+$ that is not in $X$.
6. (C) Let $(W, q)$ be circuit data with $|J_\pm| \ge 2$ and $J_0 = \emptyset$, such that $W$ generates $N$ and $q$ is primitive. Show that $X_+$ and $X_-$ are both smooth if and only if all $q_i = \pm 1$, and that then $X_-$ is the $K$-flip of $X_+$ if and only if $|J_+| > |J_-|$.
7. (C) In Theorem 3.5.20, show that the ample cones of $X$ and $X^+$ in $N^1 = \operatorname{Cl}(X) \otimes \mathbb{R} = \operatorname{Cl}(X^+) \otimes \mathbb{R}$ lie on the two sides of the hyperplane of classes that vanish on $b^R$, and that the class of $P$ lies in the closure of both. This is the wall crossing of the title, and Chapter 7 makes it a statement about the secondary fan.

### Lean

- Status: not yet in Lean. The library has no circuits, no flip construction and no toric varieties.
- Fidelity: `MinimalModel.Toric.IsToricStep` in `MinimalModel/Toric/Program.lean` is the combinatorial form of Theorem 3.5.20. With base $G = \Sigma_R$: $\Sigma$ refines $G$ with relative Picard number one (`Fan.HasRelPicardOne`), $-D$ is ample over $G$ (`Fan.IsAmpleOver`, strict convexity of the support function inside each cone of $G$, which is the positivity of $-D$ on the $R$-walls); $\Sigma^+$ is simplicial, refines $G$ and has the rays of $G$; $D^+$ is ample over $G$, which is part 4; and $D^+$ has the coefficients of $D$. The definition records the conclusion. That such a step exists for every small $D$-negative extremal ray is Theorem 3.5.20, and it is not proved in Lean. The uniqueness of Proposition 3.5.21 is a statement about schemes and is outside the library.

### Notes and history

Reid's paper of 1983 constructs toric flips by this exchange of triangulations, and Fujino and Sato (2004, Section 4) give the account that the map follows, with the Proj description of the flip in their Theorem 4.8, which rests on Kollár-Mori, Lemma 6.2. Cox-Little-Schenck treat the same construction in Section 15.4. The proofs here were written for the handbook from the circuit lemma of Section 2: the global fan $\Sigma^+$ is glued from the local flips, and its projectivity is proved with the supporting divisor. Reid's paper was not read for this section. The Atiyah flop is named after Atiyah; the handbook has no record of his paper and uses the flop only as an example. The name Francia flip refers to Francia's example of a flip from a threefold with a $\frac12(1,1,1)$ point to a smooth threefold; the handbook has no record of Francia's paper and states the example only in its toric form. Chapter 8 obtains both examples from the action of $\mathbb{C}^*$ on $\mathbb{C}^4$ with weights $(1, -1, 1, -1)$ and $(1, 1, -1, -2)$.
