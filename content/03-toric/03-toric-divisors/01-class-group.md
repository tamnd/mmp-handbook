## Torus-invariant divisors and the class group

### Statement

**Definition 3.3.1 (the divisors $D_\rho$).** Let $\Sigma$ be a fan and $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$ a ray with minimal generator $u_\rho$. The *torus-invariant prime divisor* of $\rho$ is $D_\rho = V(\rho)$, the closure of the orbit $O(\rho)$ of dimension $n - 1$. A *torus-invariant divisor* is a Weil divisor $\sum_\rho a_\rho D_\rho$ with $a_\rho \in \mathbb{Z}$. They form the group $\operatorname{Div}_T(X_\Sigma) = \bigoplus_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} \mathbb{Z} D_\rho$.

**Proposition 3.3.2 (invariant prime divisors).** The prime divisors of $X_\Sigma$ that are invariant under $T_N$ are exactly the $D_\rho$, $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$, and their union is $X_\Sigma \setminus T_N$.

**Proposition 3.3.3 (the divisor of a character).** For $m \in M$ and $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$, $\operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}(\chi^m) = \langle m, u_\rho \rangle$. So

$$\operatorname{div}(\chi^m) = \sum_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)} \langle m, u_\rho \rangle D_\rho.$$

Lean: not yet in Lean.

**Theorem 3.3.4 (the class group).** The sequence

$$M \longrightarrow \operatorname{Div}_T(X_\Sigma) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma) \longrightarrow 0, \qquad m \mapsto \operatorname{div}(\chi^m), \quad D \mapsto [D],$$

is exact. The first map is injective if and only if the vectors $u_\rho$, $\rho \in \Sigma(1)$, span $N_{\mathbb{R}}$. In particular $\operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma)$ is a finitely generated abelian group, and when the $u_\rho$ span $N_{\mathbb{R}}$ its rank is $|\Sigma(1)| - n$.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

### Proof in words

Near the generic point of $D_\rho$, the variety is the chart $U_\rho \cong \mathbb{A}^1 \times (k^*)^{n-1}$ of the ray. In a basis of $M$ adapted to $u_\rho$, the first coordinate is the character $\chi^{m_1}$ with $\langle m_1, u_\rho \rangle = 1$, and $D_\rho$ is its zero set. A character $\chi^m$ is $\chi^{m_1}$ to the power $\langle m, u_\rho \rangle$ times a unit, so its order along $D_\rho$ is $\langle m, u_\rho \rangle$.

For the class group, remove all the $D_\rho$. What is left is the torus, whose coordinate ring is a Laurent polynomial ring, a unique factorization domain, so its class group is zero. Hence the $D_\rho$ generate $\operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma)$. A relation $\sum a_\rho D_\rho = \operatorname{div}(f)$ forces $f$ to have no zeros and poles on the torus, so $f$ is a unit of the Laurent polynomial ring, which is $c \chi^m$. So the relations are the divisors of characters.

### Examples

**Example 3.3.5 (projective space).** For $\mathbb{P}^n$ with rays $u_0 = -e_1 - \dots - e_n$ and $u_i = e_i$, $D_i = \{x_i = 0\}$ and $\operatorname{div}(\chi^{e_i^*}) = D_i - D_0$. So all $D_i$ are linearly equivalent to a hyperplane $H$, and $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{P}^n) = \mathbb{Z}^{n+1} / \mathbb{Z}^n \cong \mathbb{Z} H$.

**Example 3.3.6 (the Hirzebruch surface).** For $\mathbb{F}_n$ with $u_1 = e_1$, $u_2 = e_2$, $u_3 = -e_1 + n e_2$, $u_4 = -e_2$:

$$\operatorname{div}(\chi^{e_1^*}) = D_1 - D_3, \qquad \operatorname{div}(\chi^{e_2^*}) = D_2 + n D_3 - D_4.$$

So $D_1 \sim D_3$ and $D_4 \sim D_2 + n D_3$, and $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F}_n) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ with basis $[D_2], [D_3]$. The class $f = [D_1] = [D_3]$ is the class of a fibre of $\mathbb{F}_n \to \mathbb{P}^1$ (Example 3.2.42).

**Example 3.3.7 (the class group of $\frac1n(1,1)$).** For $\sigma_n = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, n e_1 - e_2)$ write $D_1$ and $D_2$ for the divisors of the rays $e_2$ and $n e_1 - e_2$. Then $\operatorname{div}(\chi^{(1,0)}) = n D_2$ and $\operatorname{div}(\chi^{(0,1)}) = D_1 - D_2$. So

$$\operatorname{Cl}(U_{\sigma_n}) = \mathbb{Z}^2 / \langle (0, n), (1, -1) \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/n,$$

generated by $[D_1] = [D_2]$. For the quadric cone $xz = y^2$ ($n = 2$), $D_1$ is the line $y = z = 0$ of Example 3.2.33, the class group is $\mathbb{Z}/2$, and $2 D_1 = \operatorname{div}(z)$ with $z = \chi^{(1,2)}$.

**Example 3.3.8 (the weighted projective plane).** For $\mathbb{P}(1,1,n)$, with the rays $u_1 = e_1$, $u_3 = -e_1 + n e_2$, $u_4 = -e_2$ of Example 3.1.26, $\operatorname{div}(\chi^{e_1^*}) = D_1 - D_3$ and $\operatorname{div}(\chi^{e_2^*}) = n D_3 - D_4$. So $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{P}(1,1,n)) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, generated by $D_0 = [D_1] = [D_3]$, with $[D_4] = n D_0$.

**Example 3.3.9 (the cone over a square).** With $x, y, z, w$ as in Example 3.2.16 and $D_i = V(u_i)$,

$$\operatorname{div}(x) = D_2 + D_3, \quad \operatorname{div}(y) = D_3 + D_4, \quad \operatorname{div}(z) = D_1 + D_4, \quad \operatorname{div}(w) = D_1 + D_2.$$

The relation $xz = yw$ appears as $\operatorname{div}(x) + \operatorname{div}(z) = \operatorname{div}(y) + \operatorname{div}(w)$. The three relations from $x$, $y$, $z$ give $D_3 \sim -D_2$, $D_4 \sim D_2$ and $D_1 \sim -D_2$. So $\operatorname{Cl}(U_\sigma) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, generated by $[D_2]$: the homomorphism $\operatorname{Div}_T \to \mathbb{Z}$ with $D_1, D_2, D_3, D_4 \mapsto -1, 1, -1, 1$ kills every $\operatorname{div}(\chi^m)$, because $-u_1 + u_2 - u_3 + u_4 = 0$, and it sends $D_2$ to $1$. The planes $D_1$ and $D_2$ through the vertex are not linearly equivalent; they are opposite generators of $\operatorname{Cl}$.

**Example 3.3.10 (the first map need not be injective).** Let $\Sigma$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ have the single ray $e_1$. Then $X_\Sigma = U_{\operatorname{Cone}(e_1)} = \mathbb{A}^1 \times k^*$, with one invariant divisor $D_1 = \{0\} \times k^*$. The character $\chi^{e_2^*}$ is a unit with $\operatorname{div}(\chi^{e_2^*}) = 0$, so $M \to \operatorname{Div}_T$ has the kernel $\mathbb{Z} e_2^*$. Also $D_1 = \operatorname{div}(\chi^{e_1^*})$, so $\operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma) = 0$.

### Proof

*Proof of Proposition 3.3.2.* A prime divisor that is invariant under $T_N$ is a closed invariant set, so it is a union of orbits, and by irreducibility it is the closure of one orbit of dimension $n - 1$. By Theorem 3.2.29 these orbits are the $O(\rho)$ for the rays $\rho$. The complement $X_\Sigma \setminus T_N$ is the union of the $O(\sigma)$ with $\sigma \neq \{0\}$, and each such $O(\sigma)$ lies in $D_\rho$ for any ray $\rho$ of $\sigma$, by Theorem 3.2.29, part 4. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition 3.3.3.* The open set $U_\rho$ meets $D_\rho$ in $O(\rho)$, which is dense in $D_\rho$, so $\operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}$ can be computed on $U_\rho$. Since $u_\rho$ is primitive, it extends to a basis $u_\rho = v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ of $N$. Let $m_1, \dots, m_n$ be the dual basis. Then $S_\rho = \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} m_1 + \mathbb{Z} m_2 + \dots + \mathbb{Z} m_n$, and $U_\rho = \operatorname{Spec} k[x_1, x_2^{\pm 1}, \dots, x_n^{\pm 1}]$ with $x_i = \chi^{m_i}$. The point $\gamma_\rho$ kills exactly the $\chi^m$ with $\langle m, u_\rho \rangle > 0$, so $D_\rho \cap U_\rho = \{x_1 = 0\}$, and $\operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}(x_1) = 1$. For $m \in M$, $m = \langle m, u_\rho \rangle m_1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} \langle m, v_i \rangle m_i$, so $\chi^m = x_1^{\langle m, u_\rho \rangle} \cdot \prod_{i \ge 2} x_i^{\langle m, v_i \rangle}$, and the second factor is a unit on $U_\rho$. So $\operatorname{ord}_{D_\rho}(\chi^m) = \langle m, u_\rho \rangle$. Since $\chi^m$ is a unit on $T_N$, its divisor is supported on $X_\Sigma \setminus T_N = \bigcup_\rho D_\rho$, and the formula for $\operatorname{div}(\chi^m)$ follows. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 3.3.4.* By Proposition 3.3.2, $X_\Sigma \setminus T_N$ is the union of the $D_\rho$. The excision sequence for class groups (Hartshorne, Chapter II, Section 6) gives an exact sequence

$$\operatorname{Div}_T(X_\Sigma) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Cl}(T_N) \longrightarrow 0.$$

The ring $k[M] \cong k[x_1^{\pm 1}, \dots, x_n^{\pm 1}]$ is a localization of a polynomial ring, so it is a unique factorization domain and $\operatorname{Cl}(T_N) = 0$. So $\operatorname{Div}_T(X_\Sigma) \to \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma)$ is onto. The composite $M \to \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma)$ is zero, since $\operatorname{div}(\chi^m)$ is principal. Let $D = \sum a_\rho D_\rho$ with $D = \operatorname{div}(f)$ for some $f \in k(X_\Sigma)^*$. Then $f$ has no zeros or poles on $T_N$. Since $T_N$ is normal, $f$ and $1/f$ are regular on $T_N$, so $f$ is a unit of $k[M]$ and $f = c \chi^m$ with $c \in k^*$ (Exercise 3 of Section 2.1). So $D = \operatorname{div}(\chi^m)$, and the sequence is exact in the middle. Finally $\operatorname{div}(\chi^m) = 0$ if and only if $\langle m, u_\rho \rangle = 0$ for all $\rho$, which happens for some $m \neq 0$ exactly when the $u_\rho$ lie in a hyperplane of $N_{\mathbb{R}}$. $\square$

### Exercises

1. (A) Compute $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1)$ from the fan, and identify the generators with the two rulings.
2. (A) Show that $\operatorname{Cl}(U_\sigma) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n$ for the cone $\sigma = \operatorname{Cone}(e_2, n e_1 - q e_2)$ of $\frac1n(1,q)$, for every $q$ prime to $n$.
3. (B) Let $\sigma \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be a simplicial cone of dimension $n$. Show that $\operatorname{Cl}(U_\sigma)$ is a finite group of order $\operatorname{mult}(\sigma)$, and that it is isomorphic to $N / (\mathbb{Z} u_1 + \dots + \mathbb{Z} u_n)$.
4. (B) Let $q_0, q_1, q_2$ be positive integers, pairwise coprime, and let $\Sigma$ be a complete fan in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with three rays whose minimal generators generate $N$ and satisfy $q_0 u_0 + q_1 u_1 + q_2 u_2 = 0$. Show that $\operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, and find the class of each $D_i$ in terms of a generator. This $X_\Sigma$ is the weighted projective plane $\mathbb{P}(q_0, q_1, q_2)$.

### Notes and history

The exact sequence of Theorem 3.3.4 is in Fulton's book, Chapter 3, and in Cox-Little-Schenck, Chapter 4. When $\Sigma$ has no torus factor, the dual of the sequence is the start of Gale duality: the map $\mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)} \to \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma)$ gives the grading of the Cox ring $k[x_\rho : \rho \in \Sigma(1)]$, and the later chapters of this volume use it for the secondary fan and for variation of GIT. Example 3.3.10 is the reason why most statements assume that the $u_\rho$ span $N_{\mathbb{R}}$, which holds when $X_\Sigma$ has no torus factor.
