## The cone of curves of a toric variety

### Statement

Let $X$ be a complete normal variety. A *curve* is an integral closed subscheme of dimension one. Two $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier divisors $D$, $D'$ are *numerically equivalent*, $D \equiv D'$, if $D \cdot C = D' \cdot C$ for every curve $C$, and $N^1(X)$ is the quotient of $\operatorname{Pic}(X) \otimes \mathbb{R}$ by numerical equivalence. Dually, $N_1(X)$ is the real vector space of formal sums $\sum r_i C_i$ of curves modulo the sums that have degree zero against every Cartier divisor. When $N^1(X)$ is finite dimensional, the intersection pairing $N^1(X) \times N_1(X) \to \mathbb{R}$ is perfect, by the definition of both sides. The *cone of curves* $NE(X) \subset N_1(X)$ is the set of classes $\sum r_i [C_i]$ with $r_i \ge 0$, and $\overline{NE}(X)$ is its closure. A divisor is *nef* if $D \cdot C \ge 0$ for every curve, so the nef cone $\operatorname{Nef}(X) \subset N^1(X)$ is the dual cone of $\overline{NE}(X)$. Fujino and Sato (2004, 2.7) and Kollár-Mori, Chapter 1, set up these notions in the same way.

**Theorem 3.4.19 (the nef criterion).** Let $\Sigma$ be a complete simplicial fan and $D$ a torus-invariant $\mathbb{R}$-divisor on $X_\Sigma$. The following are equivalent.

1. $D$ is nef.
2. $D \cdot V(\tau) \ge 0$ for every wall $\tau$ of $\Sigma$.
3. $\varphi_D$ is convex.

If $D$ is Cartier, they are also equivalent to: $\mathcal{O}(D)$ is generated by its global sections. If $X_\Sigma$ is projective, then $D$ is ample if and only if $D \cdot V(\tau) > 0$ for every wall, that is, if and only if $\varphi_D$ is strictly convex.

Lean: not yet in Lean. The Lean library takes part 3 as its definition of nef on the fan, `MinimalModel.Toric.Fan.IsNef`, and proves the equivalence of parts 2 and 3 (Theorem 3.4.13). Part 1 needs the scheme $X_\Sigma$.

**Theorem 3.4.20 (numerical classes on a toric variety).** Let $\Sigma$ be a complete simplicial fan.

1. A torus-invariant $\mathbb{R}$-divisor $D$ is numerically trivial if and only if $D = \sum_\rho \langle m, u_\rho \rangle D_\rho$ for some $m \in M_{\mathbb{R}}$.
2. $N^1(X_\Sigma) = \operatorname{Pic}(X_\Sigma) \otimes \mathbb{R} = \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma) \otimes \mathbb{R}$, and its dimension is $\rho(X_\Sigma) = |\Sigma(1)| - n$.
3. The map $C \mapsto (D_\rho \cdot C)_{\rho \in \Sigma(1)}$ is an isomorphism from $N_1(X_\Sigma)$ onto the space of linear relations
$$R(\Sigma) = \Big\{ b \in \mathbb{R}^{\Sigma(1)} : \sum_\rho b_\rho u_\rho = 0 \Big\}.$$
Under it, the class of a wall curve $V(\tau)$ is the wall relation of $\tau$ (Theorem 3.4.5).

Lean: not yet in Lean.

**Theorem 3.4.21 (the toric cone theorem).** Let $\Sigma$ be a complete simplicial fan. Then

$$NE(X_\Sigma) = \overline{NE}(X_\Sigma) = \sum_{\tau \text{ a wall}} \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} [V(\tau)].$$

So $NE(X_\Sigma)$ is a closed rational polyhedral cone, each of its extremal rays is spanned by the class of a wall curve, and $\operatorname{Nef}(X_\Sigma)$ is the rational polyhedral cone $\{D : D \cdot V(\tau) \ge 0 \text{ for all } \tau\}$. If $X_\Sigma$ is projective, $NE(X_\Sigma)$ contains no line, and the ample cone is the interior of $\operatorname{Nef}(X_\Sigma)$.

Lean: not yet in Lean. Its combinatorial core is Theorem 3.4.13, `MinimalModel.Toric.Fan.coneTheorem`. The step to curves on $X_\Sigma$ needs the scheme.

The theorem holds for every complete fan, simplicial or not, and also relative to a proper toric morphism (Cox-Little-Schenck, Theorem 6.3.20; Fujino-Sato, Theorem 4.1 and Remark 4.2). The proof below uses that $X_\Sigma$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, which is the case the toric MMP needs.

### Proof in words

A nef divisor is nonnegative on wall curves, by definition. Section 2 turns nonnegativity on wall curves into convexity of the support function. Convexity says that the data $m_\sigma$ all lie on the same side of $\varphi_D$, and Cox-Little-Schenck show that this makes $\chi^{m_\sigma}$ a section of $\mathcal{O}(D)$ that does not vanish at the fixed point $\gamma_\sigma$. So $\mathcal{O}(D)$ is globally generated, and a globally generated divisor has nonnegative degree on every curve. The circle of implications closes.

For the cone of curves, the finitely many classes $[V(\tau)]$ span a closed cone $K$ inside $NE$. A divisor is nonnegative on $K$ exactly when it is nonnegative on the wall curves, so by the nef criterion the dual cone of $K$ is the nef cone, which is the dual cone of $\overline{NE}$. Two closed convex cones with the same dual cone are equal, so $K = \overline{NE}$, and $NE$ is squeezed between them.

The space $N_1$ is computed through its dual. A divisor that is zero on every wall curve has a support function that is convex and also concave, so linear, so the divisor is the divisor of a character. So $N^1$ is the class group tensored with $\mathbb{R}$, which is $\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma(1)}$ modulo the image of $M_{\mathbb{R}}$. Its dual is the space of vectors $b$ that vanish on that image, and this is the space of relations $\sum b_\rho u_\rho = 0$.

### Examples

**Example 3.4.22 (projective space and weighted projective space).** For $\mathbb{P}^n$, $\rho = 1$ and $R(\Sigma)$ is spanned by $(1, \dots, 1)$. Every wall curve is a line with this class, so $NE(\mathbb{P}^n) = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} [L]$. For $\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,2)$, $R(\Sigma)$ is spanned by $(1, 1, 1, 2)$ on $(u_0, u_1, u_2, u_3)$. The wall curves of Example 3.4.10 have the classes $\frac12 (1,1,1,2)$ and $(1,1,1,2)$, both on the same ray. Every divisor with $H$-degree $\ge 0$ is nef, and $-K = 5H$ is ample, so $\mathbb{P}(1,1,1,2)$ is a Fano variety with $\rho = 1$.

**Example 3.4.23 (the Hirzebruch surface).** For $\mathbb{F}_n$, $R(\Sigma)$ has dimension $4 - 2 = 2$. With coordinates on $(u_1, u_2, u_3, u_4)$, Example 3.4.7 gives the classes

$$[D_1] = [D_3] = (0, 1, 0, 1), \qquad [D_2] = (1, -n, 1, 0), \qquad [D_4] = (1, 0, 1, n).$$

So $[D_4] = [D_2] + n [D_1]$, and $NE(\mathbb{F}_n) = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} [D_2] + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} [f]$ with $f = D_1$ a fibre. This is the cone that Problem 2 of Chapter 3 assumed. A divisor $D$ is nef if and only if $D \cdot D_2 \ge 0$ and $D \cdot f \ge 0$. For $-K = \sum D_i$, $-K \cdot f = 2$ and $-K \cdot D_2 = 2 - n$. So $-K$ is ample for $n \le 1$ and nef for $n \le 2$. For $n = 0$ both extremal rays are fibres, of the two projections of $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$.

**Example 3.4.24 (a weighted blow-up of $\mathbb{P}^3$).** Let $\Sigma$ be the fan of $\mathbb{P}^3$, with rays $e_1, e_2, e_3$ and $e_0 = -e_1 - e_2 - e_3$, and let $\Sigma'$ be its star subdivision at $v = e_1 + e_2 + 2 e_3$. So $X_{\Sigma'}$ is the weighted blow-up of $\mathbb{P}^3$ at the fixed point $\gamma_{\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_2, e_3)}$ with weights $(1, 1, 2)$, and $E = D_v$ is its exceptional divisor. The cone $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_2, e_3)$ is replaced by

$$\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_2, e_3), \qquad \operatorname{Cone}(e_1, v, e_3), \qquad \operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_2, v),$$

of multiplicities $1$, $1$ and $2$ (the coordinates of $v$). The third one contains $e_1 + e_2 + e_3 = \frac12(e_1 + e_2 + v)$, and it is the cone of $\frac12(1,1,1)$. The discrepancy of $E$ over $\mathbb{P}^3$ is $1 + 1 + 2 - 1 = 3$ (Proposition 3.3.35). The space $R(\Sigma')$ has dimension $5 - 3 = 2$, with the basis

$$r_1 = (1, 1, 1, 1, 0), \qquad r_2 = (0, -1, -1, -2, 1)$$

in the coordinates $(e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3, v)$, from $e_0 + e_1 + e_2 + e_3 = 0$ and $v - e_1 - e_2 - 2 e_3 = 0$. Theorem 3.4.5 gives the classes of the nine wall curves. A wall curve uses only the rays of its two cones, and the coefficients at the two rays off the wall are fixed by the multiplicities:

| wall | cones on its sides | multiplicities | class | $K \cdot C$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, e_i)$ | $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, e_i, e_j)$, $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, e_i, e_k)$ | $1, 1$ | $r_1$ | $-4$ |
| $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_3)$, $\operatorname{Cone}(e_2, e_3)$ | $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, \cdot, e_3)$, $\operatorname{Cone}(\cdot, v, e_3)$ | $1, 1$ | $r_1 + r_2$ | $-1$ |
| $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_2)$ | $\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, e_1, e_2)$, $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_2, v)$ | $1, 2$ | $r_1 + \frac12 r_2$ | $-\frac52$ |
| $\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_3)$ | $\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_2, e_3)$, $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, v, e_3)$ | $1, 1$ | $-r_2$ | $-3$ |
| $\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_1)$, $\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_2)$ | one smooth cone and $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_2, v)$ | $1, 2$ | $-\frac12 r_2$ | $-\frac32$ |

Here $K \cdot C$ is minus the sum of the coordinates (Theorem 3.4.5, part 3). For instance, at the wall $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_2)$ the rays off the wall are $e_0$ and $v$, so the class is the combination of $r_1$ and $r_2$ with no $e_3$ term, which is a multiple of $2 r_1 + r_2 = (2, 1, 1, 0, 1)$. The coefficient at $e_0$ must be $\operatorname{mult}(\tau) / \operatorname{mult}(\operatorname{Cone}(e_0, e_1, e_2)) = 1$, so the class is $(1, \frac12, \frac12, 0, \frac12) = r_1 + \frac12 r_2$.

In the basis $r_1, r_2$ the classes are $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$, $(1, \frac12)$, $(0, -1)$ and $(0, -\frac12)$. So

$$NE(X_{\Sigma'}) = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} [C_{13}] + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} [\ell], \qquad C_{13} = V(\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_3)), \quad \ell = V(\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_3)),$$

with $[C_{12}] = [C_{13}] + \frac12 [\ell]$ and $[L] = [C_{13}] + [\ell]$ for a general line. The curve $C_{13}$ is the strict transform of a coordinate line through the blown-up point, and $\ell$ is a curve in $E \cong \mathbb{P}(1,1,2)$. With $H = D_0$ and $E = D_v$, the divisor $aH + bE$ has degree $a + b$ on $C_{13}$ and $-b$ on $\ell$. So $\operatorname{Nef}(X_{\Sigma'}) = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} H + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} (H - E)$. The characters $e_1^*$ and $e_3^*$ give $D_1 \sim D_2 \sim H - E$ and $D_3 \sim H - 2E$, so $-K \sim 4H - 3E$, with degree $1$ on $C_{13}$ and $3$ on $\ell$. So $-K$ is ample, and both extremal rays are $K$-negative. Chapter 6 runs the MMP on this variety.

### Proof

*Proof of Theorem 3.4.19.* Part 1 implies part 2, since $V(\tau)$ is a curve. Part 2 implies part 3 by Theorem 3.4.13.

Assume part 3 with $D$ Cartier. By Cox-Little-Schenck, Theorem 6.1.7, a Cartier divisor on a complete toric variety is generated by its global sections if and only if its support function is convex. The idea is this: by Proposition 3.4.12, $\langle m_\sigma, u_\rho \rangle \ge \varphi_D(u_\rho) = -a_\rho$ for all $\rho$, so $\operatorname{div}(\chi^{m_\sigma}) + D \ge 0$ and $\chi^{m_\sigma}$ is a global section of $\mathcal{O}(D)$. On $U_\sigma$ it is the local generator of $\mathcal{O}(D)$ given by Theorem 3.3.12, so it does not vanish on $U_\sigma$. The $U_\sigma$ cover $X_\Sigma$. A globally generated divisor is nef: for a curve $C$, choose a point $x \in C$ and a global section $s$ with $s(x) \neq 0$; then $s|_C$ is a nonzero section of $\mathcal{O}(D)|_C$, and its divisor is effective of degree $D \cdot C \ge 0$.

For a $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor with convex $\varphi_D$, some multiple $rD$ is Cartier (Theorem 3.3.20) and $\varphi_{rD} = r \varphi_D$ is convex, so $rD$ is nef and so is $D$. For an $\mathbb{R}$-divisor, the set of $(a_\rho) \in \mathbb{R}^{\Sigma(1)}$ that satisfy part 2 is a polyhedral cone cut out by the finitely many inequalities $D \cdot V(\tau) \ge 0$. Their coefficients are rational, by Theorem 3.4.4. So the cone is the dual of a rational polyhedral cone, and it is rational by Theorem 3.1.3, part 1: it is generated by rational vectors. So $D$ is a nonnegative combination of nef $\mathbb{Q}$-divisors, and it is nef.

For ampleness, let $X_\Sigma$ be projective. By Kleiman's criterion (Kleiman 1966; Kollár-Mori, Chapter 1) the ample cone is the interior of $\operatorname{Nef}(X_\Sigma)$ in $N^1(X_\Sigma)$. By Theorem 3.4.21, proved below without this part, $\operatorname{Nef}$ is the dual cone of $K = \sum \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} [V(\tau)]$, and $K$ contains no line, because an ample divisor is positive on every nonzero element of $K$. For a closed cone $K$ that contains no line, the interior of the dual cone is the set of $D$ that are positive on $K \setminus \{0\}$. So $D$ is ample if and only if $D \cdot V(\tau) > 0$ for all walls, and that is strict convexity by Theorem 3.4.13. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 3.4.20.* For part 1, the divisor $\sum \langle m, u_\rho \rangle D_\rho$ is the divisor of the character $\chi^m$ for $m \in M$ (Proposition 3.3.3), so it has degree $0$ on every curve, and by linearity the same holds for $m \in M_{\mathbb{R}}$. Conversely, let $D$ be numerically trivial. Then $D \cdot V(\tau) = 0$ and $(-D) \cdot V(\tau) = 0$ for every wall, so $\varphi_D$ and $\varphi_{-D} = -\varphi_D$ are both convex by Theorem 3.4.13. For $u, v \in N_{\mathbb{R}}$ this gives $\varphi_D(u + v) \ge \varphi_D(u) + \varphi_D(v)$ and the reverse inequality, so $\varphi_D$ is additive. It is positively homogeneous, and $\varphi_D(-u) = -\varphi_D(u)$ by additivity, so it is linear: $\varphi_D = \langle m', \cdot \rangle$ with $m' \in M_{\mathbb{R}}$. Then $a_\rho = -\varphi_D(u_\rho) = \langle -m', u_\rho \rangle$, and $m = -m'$ works.

For part 2, Theorem 3.3.4 and its kernel give the exact sequence $0 \to M_{\mathbb{R}} \to \mathbb{R}^{\Sigma(1)} \to \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma) \otimes \mathbb{R} \to 0$. The map on the left is injective because the $u_\rho$ span $N_{\mathbb{R}}$, as $\Sigma$ is complete. Since $\Sigma$ is simplicial, every Weil divisor has a Cartier multiple (Theorem 3.3.20), so $\operatorname{Pic}(X_\Sigma) \subset \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma)$ (Theorem 3.3.13) has finite index, and the two agree after tensoring with $\mathbb{R}$. By part 1 the numerically trivial classes are exactly the image of $M_{\mathbb{R}}$, which is zero in $\operatorname{Cl} \otimes \mathbb{R}$. So $N^1(X_\Sigma) = \operatorname{Cl}(X_\Sigma) \otimes \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^{\Sigma(1)} / M_{\mathbb{R}}$, of dimension $|\Sigma(1)| - n$.

For part 3, the pairing is perfect, so $N_1(X_\Sigma)$ is the dual of $\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma(1)} / M_{\mathbb{R}}$. A linear form on $\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma(1)}$ is a vector $b$, acting by $a \mapsto \sum b_\rho a_\rho$, and it descends to the quotient if and only if $\sum_\rho b_\rho \langle m, u_\rho \rangle = 0$ for all $m$, that is, $\sum b_\rho u_\rho = 0$. A curve $C$ acts by $D_\rho \mapsto D_\rho \cdot C$, so its vector is $b_\rho = D_\rho \cdot C$. For $C = V(\tau)$ this is the wall relation by Theorem 3.4.5. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 3.4.21.* Let $K = \sum_\tau \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} [V(\tau)]$. It is a finitely generated cone, so it is closed, and it lies in $NE(X_\Sigma)$. By Theorem 3.4.20, part 2, every class in $N^1(X_\Sigma)$ is the class of a torus-invariant $\mathbb{R}$-divisor. So, by Theorem 3.4.19,

$$K^\vee = \{ D \in N^1 : D \cdot V(\tau) \ge 0 \ \forall \tau \} = \operatorname{Nef}(X_\Sigma) = \overline{NE}(X_\Sigma)^\vee.$$

Both $K$ and $\overline{NE}(X_\Sigma)$ are closed convex cones, and a closed convex cone $C$ satisfies $C^{\vee\vee} = C$, by the separation theorem for convex sets (Theorem 3.1.3, part 2, is the polyhedral case). So $K = K^{\vee\vee} = \overline{NE}(X_\Sigma)^{\vee\vee} = \overline{NE}(X_\Sigma)$. Then $K \subset NE(X_\Sigma) \subset \overline{NE}(X_\Sigma) = K$. The cone $K$ is generated by the finitely many classes $[V(\tau)]$, which are rational points of $R(\Sigma)$ by Theorem 3.4.5, and an extremal ray of a finitely generated cone is spanned by one of the generators. The nef cone is the dual of a rational polyhedral cone, so it is rational polyhedral. If $X_\Sigma$ is projective with an ample $A$, then $A \cdot V(\tau) > 0$ for every $\tau$, so $A > 0$ on $K \setminus \{0\}$ and $K$ contains no line. The statement on the ample cone is the last part of Theorem 3.4.19. $\square$

### Exercises

1. (A) Compute $NE(\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1)$ and its nef cone. Show that both are simplicial cones of dimension 3.
2. (A) Let $X$ be the blow-up of $\mathbb{P}^2$ at a torus-fixed point, the fan of Exercise 5 of Section 2. Show that $X \cong \mathbb{F}_1$ and that its cone of curves is spanned by the exceptional curve and the strict transform of a line through the point.
3. (B) In Example 3.4.24, check the classes of the walls $\operatorname{Cone}(e_1, e_3)$ and $\operatorname{Cone}(v, e_2)$, and the linear equivalences $D_1 \sim H - E$ and $D_3 \sim H - 2E$.
4. (B) Show that two walls $\tau$, $\tau'$ of a complete simplicial fan have the same class in $N_1(X_\Sigma)$ if and only if every torus-invariant divisor has the same degree on $V(\tau)$ and $V(\tau')$. Find two different walls with the same class in Example 3.4.24, and two in $\mathbb{F}_n$.
5. (B) Show that for a complete simplicial toric surface with $\rho \ge 2$ the extremal rays of $NE$ are spanned by curves $D_i$ with $D_i^2 \le 0$, and that every $D_i$ with $D_i^2 < 0$ spans an extremal ray.
6. (C) Let $\Sigma$ be a complete simplicial fan with $\rho(X_\Sigma) = 1$, so that $\Sigma$ has $n + 1$ rays. Show that the $u_\rho$ satisfy one relation $\sum q_\rho u_\rho = 0$ with all $q_\rho > 0$, that every wall curve has a class that is a positive multiple of $q$, and that every $D_\rho$ has a strictly convex support function.

### Notes and history

The map cites Reid (1983, Section 1) for the statement that the invariant curves span the cone of curves, and Cox-Little-Schenck give it as Theorem 6.3.20. Fujino and Sato (2004, Theorem 4.1) prove that $NE(X/Y)$ is polyhedral for every proper toric morphism, with no simplicial hypothesis. Their first proof reduces to a complete toric variety over each orbit of the base and cites Fulton's book for the complete case. Their second proof, in characteristic zero, applies the cone theorem for klt pairs to the pair $(X, \sum (1 - a_i) D_i)$ of Proposition 2.10 of their paper. Remark 4.2 of their paper adds that for complete $X$ the extremal rays are spanned by invariant curves, and Example 4.3 shows that this fails when $X$ is not complete. The nef cone of a toric variety is rational polyhedral, which fails for general varieties, and this is why the toric MMP can be run by hand.
