## Integral elements and integral closure

### Statement

**Definition P1.4.1 (integral elements).** Let $A \subseteq B$ be rings, or more generally let $B$ be an $A$-algebra. An element $b \in B$ is *integral* over $A$ if it is a root of a monic polynomial with coefficients in $A$:
$$
b^n + a_1 b^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0, \qquad a_i \in A .
$$
The algebra $B$ is *integral* over $A$ if every element is integral. It is *finite* over $A$ if it is finitely generated as an $A$-module.

Lean: Mathlib `IsIntegral` and `Algebra.IsIntegral` (definitions).

**Proposition P1.4.2 (forms of integrality).** For $b \in B$ the following are equivalent: (a) $b$ is integral over $A$; (b) the subring $A[b]$ is a finitely generated $A$-module; (c) $A[b]$ lies in a subring $C \subseteq B$ that is a finitely generated $A$-module; (d) there is a faithful $A[b]$-module $M$ that is finitely generated as an $A$-module.

Lean: Mathlib `IsIntegral.fg_adjoin_singleton`, `IsIntegral.of_mem_of_fg` and `IsIntegral.of_finite` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* (a) implies (b): the relation writes $b^{n+j}$ as an $A$-combination of lower powers, so $1, b, \dots, b^{n-1}$ generate $A[b]$. (b) implies (c): take $C = A[b]$. (c) implies (d): take $M = C$, which is faithful over $A[b]$ because $1 \in C$. (d) implies (a): apply the Cayley-Hamilton theorem of Section 1.2 to the map $\varphi = b \cdot$ on $M$ with $I = A$. It gives $(b^n + a_1 b^{n-1} + \dots + a_n) M = 0$, and since $M$ is faithful, $b^n + a_1 b^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$. $\square$

**Corollary P1.4.3 (integral closure).** (1) If $b_1, \dots, b_m \in B$ are integral over $A$, then $A[b_1, \dots, b_m]$ is a finitely generated $A$-module. (2) The integral elements of $B$ form a subring, the *integral closure* of $A$ in $B$. (3) If $B$ is finite over $A$, then $B$ is integral over $A$. (4) $B$ is finite over $A$ if and only if it is integral and finitely generated as an $A$-algebra.

Lean: Mathlib `IsIntegral.add`, `IsIntegral.mul`, `integralClosure`, `Algebra.IsIntegral.of_finite`, `Algebra.IsIntegral.finite` and `Algebra.finite_iff_isIntegral_and_finiteType` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* (1) By induction on $m$: $b_m$ is integral over $A[b_1, \dots, b_{m-1}]$, so $A[b_1, \dots, b_m]$ is a finite module over $A[b_1, \dots, b_{m-1}]$, which is finite over $A$ by induction. A finite module over a finite algebra is finite over $A$: products of the two sets of generators generate. (2) For integral $x, y$, the ring $C = A[x, y]$ is finite over $A$ by (1), and it contains $x \pm y$ and $xy$. By (c) of the proposition, these are integral. (3) Apply (c) with $C = B$. (4) If $B$ is finite, it is integral by (3), and module generators are algebra generators. If $B$ is integral and generated by $b_1, \dots, b_m$, apply (1). $\square$

**Proposition P1.4.4 (transitivity).** If $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$ with $B$ integral over $A$ and $C$ integral over $B$, then $C$ is integral over $A$. In particular the integral closure of $A$ in $B$ is integrally closed in $B$.

Lean: Mathlib `isIntegral_trans`, `Algebra.IsIntegral.trans` and `integralClosure_idem` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* Let $c \in C$ satisfy $c^n + b_1 c^{n-1} + \dots + b_n = 0$ with $b_i \in B$. Then $c$ is integral over $A' = A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$, so $A'[c]$ is finite over $A'$, and $A'$ is finite over $A$ by part (1) of the corollary. So $A'[c]$ is finite over $A$, and $c$ is integral over $A$. For the second claim, apply this to $A \subseteq \bar{A} \subseteq B$ and an element of $B$ integral over $\bar{A}$. $\square$

**Proposition P1.4.5 (quotients and localizations).** Let $B$ be integral over $A$. (1) For an ideal $J$ of $B$, $B/J$ is integral over $A/(J \cap A)$. (2) For a multiplicative set $S \subseteq A$, $S^{-1}B$ is integral over $S^{-1}A$. (3) If $\bar{A}$ is the integral closure of $A$ in $B$, then $S^{-1}\bar{A}$ is the integral closure of $S^{-1}A$ in $S^{-1}B$.

Lean: Mathlib proves these with `IsIntegral` and `IsLocalization`; the handbook does not name a single declaration.

*Proof.* (1) Reduce an integral equation for $b$ modulo $J$. (2) If $b^n + a_1 b^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$, then $(b/s)^n + (a_1/s)(b/s)^{n-1} + \dots + a_n/s^n = 0$. (3) By (2), $S^{-1}\bar{A}$ is integral over $S^{-1}A$. Conversely, let $b/s \in S^{-1}B$ be integral over $S^{-1}A$. Then so is $b/1 = s \cdot b/s$. With a common denominator $t \in S$, it satisfies $(b/1)^n + (c_1/t)(b/1)^{n-1} + \dots + c_n/t = 0$ with $c_i \in A$. Multiply by $t^n$: the element $tb/1$ satisfies a monic equation $(tb)^n + d_1 (tb)^{n-1} + \dots + d_n = 0$ in $S^{-1}B$ with $d_i \in A$. So $u((tb)^n + d_1 (tb)^{n-1} + \dots + d_n) = 0$ in $B$ for some $u \in S$. Multiply by $u^{n-1}$: then $(utb)^n + u d_1 (utb)^{n-1} + \dots + u^n d_n = 0$, so $utb \in \bar{A}$, and $b/s = utb/(uts) \in S^{-1}\bar{A}$. $\square$

### Proof in words

An element is integral when it satisfies a monic equation. This is the same as saying that the ring it generates is a finitely generated module, and the determinant trick turns any finite faithful module into a monic equation. Finite modules behave well under sums, products and towers, so integral elements form a ring and integrality is transitive.

For varieties, let $A \subseteq B$ be finitely generated $k$-algebras, $X = \operatorname{Spec} B$ and $Y = \operatorname{Spec} A$. Then $B$ is integral over $A$ exactly when $X \to Y$ is a finite morphism. A monic equation for a coordinate function $b$ of $X$ over $Y$ says that over each point of $Y$, $b$ takes at most $n$ values, and that $b$ stays bounded: the roots of a monic polynomial cannot go to infinity while the coefficients stay finite. The failure of integrality is a point running off to infinity, as in the hyperbola below.

### Examples

**Example P1.4.6 (the cusp).** Let $A = k[t^2, t^3] \subseteq B = k[t]$. The element $t$ is a root of $X^2 - t^2$, so it is integral over $A$, and $B = A + At$ is finite over $A$. Geometrically, the line $\operatorname{Spec} B$ maps to the cuspidal cubic $y^2 = x^3$ by $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$, a finite bijective morphism.

**Example P1.4.7 (the integers).** The integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{Z}$. If $p/q$ in lowest terms satisfies a monic equation of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, multiply by $q^n$: then $q$ divides $p^n$, so $q = \pm 1$. In $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ the element $(1 + \sqrt{5})/2$ is a root of $X^2 - X - 1$, so it is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ but not in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{5}]$.

**Example P1.4.8 (a hyperbola is not finite over a line).** Let $A = k[x] \subseteq B = k[x, 1/x] = k[x, y]/(xy - 1)$. The element $y = 1/x$ is not integral over $k[x]$. If $y^n + a_1(x) y^{n-1} + \dots + a_n(x) = 0$, multiply by $x^n$: then $1 + x(a_1 + a_2 x + \dots) = 0$ in $k[x]$, which is impossible. Geometrically, the projection of the hyperbola $xy = 1$ to the $x$-axis misses the origin, and the point $(x, 1/x)$ runs off to infinity as $x \to 0$.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ and find a monic equation for it.
2. (A) Show that $B = k[x, y]/(y^2 + xy - 1)$ is finite over $k[x]$ and not finite over $k[y]$. (Hint: $y(x + y) = 1$ in $B$.) Describe the projections of the curve to the two axes.
3. (B) Let $f \in k[x, y]$ have total degree $d$ and let $f_d$ be its homogeneous part of degree $d$. Show that $k[x, y]/(f)$ is integral over $k[x]$ if $f_d(0, 1) \neq 0$. Interpret this with the points at infinity of the curve $f = 0$.
4. (B) Let $A \subseteq B$ be domains with $B$ integral over $A$. Show that $\operatorname{Frac}(B)$ is algebraic over $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$, and that every $x \in \operatorname{Frac}(B)$ is $b/a$ with $b \in B$ and $a \in A$.
5. (B) Let a finite group $G$ act on a ring $B$ by ring automorphisms. Show that $B$ is integral over $B^G$ (Hint: $\prod_{g \in G} (X - g b)$.) If $B$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra and $G$ acts by $k$-automorphisms, deduce with the Artin-Tate lemma of Section 3.2 that $B^G$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra.

### Notes and history

Integral elements go back to Dedekind and Kronecker, who studied algebraic integers in number fields. Noether put integral dependence into abstract commutative algebra in 1926, in her axiomatic characterization of Dedekind domains. The proof by the determinant trick is the standard one. The literature state of every result here is theorem. The section follows Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapter 5.
