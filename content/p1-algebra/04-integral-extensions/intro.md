# Integral extensions and Noether normalization

This chapter studies integral extensions of rings, the algebra of finite morphisms. Section 4.1 defines integral elements and proves that they form a ring, using the determinant trick of Section 1.2. Section 4.2 proves the theorems of Cohen and Seidenberg on lying over, going up and incomparability, which compare the primes of a ring and of an integral extension. Section 4.3 is about integrally closed, or normal, domains, and the going down theorem. Section 4.4 proves the Noether normalization lemma and draws its consequences: Zariski's lemma, the Jacobson property of finitely generated algebras, the dimension of an affine domain, and the finiteness of the integral closure.

For varieties, an integral extension $A \subseteq B$ of finitely generated algebras is a finite surjective morphism $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$. Lying over says that it is surjective, going up that it is closed, and incomparability that its fibres have dimension zero, so they are finite sets of points. Noether normalization says that every affine variety of dimension $d$ is a finite cover of $\mathbb{A}^d$, and this gives the dimension of a variety as a transcendence degree. The integral closure of the coordinate ring is the normalization of the variety, a finite birational morphism that removes the singularities in codimension one, as Chapters 8 and 11 will show.

The minimal model program works with normal varieties. A $\mathbb{Q}$-Weil divisor, the canonical divisor $K_X$ and the discrepancies of Volume 4 are defined on normal varieties, and the first step with a singular variety is often to normalize it. Finite morphisms appear as covers: the index one cover of a klt singularity in Volume 4 and the cyclic covers used in vanishing theorems. For slc pairs in Volume 13, the normalization of a demi-normal scheme and the conductor are the main tools. Noether normalization and the Jacobson property make the Nullstellensatz of P0 Ch. 3 work, and so they let the MMP pass between closed points and scheme theoretic points.

The chapter follows Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapter 5, Matsumura, Chapters 2 and 3, and Eisenbud, Chapters 4 and 13. It uses Chapters 1 to 3.
