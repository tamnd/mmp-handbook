## Kähler differentials

### Statement

Throughout, $k$ is a ring, $B$ is a $k$-algebra and $M$ is a $B$-module. In the applications $k$ is a field.

**Definition P1.12.1 (derivation).** A *$k$-derivation* of $B$ into $M$ is a $k$-linear map $D : B \to M$ with
$$
D(bc) = b\,D(c) + c\,D(b) \qquad (b, c \in B) .
$$
The $k$-derivations form a $B$-module $\operatorname{Der}_k(B, M)$. The rule gives $D(1) = 0$, so $D(\lambda) = \lambda D(1) = 0$ for $\lambda \in k$.

Lean: Mathlib `Derivation` (definition).

**Definition P1.12.2 (Kähler differentials).** Let $F$ be the free $B$-module on symbols $db$, $b \in B$, and let $N \subseteq F$ be the submodule generated by the elements
$$
d(b + c) - db - dc, \qquad d(bc) - b\,dc - c\,db, \qquad d\lambda \qquad (b, c \in B, \ \lambda \in k) .
$$
The *module of Kähler differentials* is $\Omega_{B/k} = F/N$, and $d : B \to \Omega_{B/k}$ sends $b$ to the class of $db$.

Lean: Mathlib `KaehlerDifferential`, written `Ω[B⁄k]`, with the universal derivation `KaehlerDifferential.D` (definitions).

**Proposition P1.12.3 (universal property).** The map $d$ is a $k$-derivation, and for every $B$-module $M$ the map
$$
\operatorname{Hom}_B(\Omega_{B/k}, M) \to \operatorname{Der}_k(B, M), \qquad \varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ d ,
$$
is an isomorphism of $B$-modules. The module $\Omega_{B/k}$ is generated by the elements $db$.

Lean: Mathlib `KaehlerDifferential.linearMapEquivDerivation` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* The relations in $N$ say exactly that $d$ is additive, satisfies the product rule and kills $k$; with the product rule, $d(\lambda b) = \lambda\,db + b\,d\lambda = \lambda\,db$, so $d$ is $k$-linear. A $B$-linear map $\varphi : \Omega_{B/k} \to M$ is determined by the values $\varphi(db)$, since the $db$ generate. Conversely, a derivation $D$ defines a $B$-linear map $F \to M$, $db \mapsto D(b)$, which kills $N$ because $D$ is a derivation. $\square$

**Proposition P1.12.4 (polynomial rings).** Let $R = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. Then $\Omega_{R/k}$ is a free $R$-module with basis $dx_1, \dots, dx_n$, and
$$
df = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\, dx_i \qquad (f \in R) .
$$

Lean: Mathlib `KaehlerDifferential.mvPolynomialBasis` and, for one variable, `KaehlerDifferential.polynomialEquiv` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* The formal partial derivatives $\partial/\partial x_i$ are $k$-derivations of $R$ into $R$, so the universal property gives $R$-linear maps $\varphi_i : \Omega_{R/k} \to R$ with $\varphi_i(df) = \partial f/\partial x_i$, and $\varphi_i(dx_j) = \delta_{ij}$. So the $dx_i$ are linearly independent. A derivation kills $k$ and satisfies the product rule, so by induction on monomials $df = \sum_i (\partial f/\partial x_i)\,dx_i$ for every $f$. So the $dx_i$ generate. $\square$

**Theorem P1.12.5 (first fundamental sequence).** Let $k \to A \to B$ be ring maps. The sequence of $B$-modules
$$
B \otimes_A \Omega_{A/k} \xrightarrow{\ \alpha\ } \Omega_{B/k} \xrightarrow{\ \beta\ } \Omega_{B/A} \to 0 ,
$$
with $\alpha(b \otimes da) = b\,da$ and $\beta(db) = db$, is exact.

Lean: Mathlib `KaehlerDifferential.exact_mapBaseChange_map` and `KaehlerDifferential.map_surjective` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* A sequence $P' \to P \to P'' \to 0$ of $B$-modules is exact if and only if $0 \to \operatorname{Hom}_B(P'', M) \to \operatorname{Hom}_B(P, M) \to \operatorname{Hom}_B(P', M)$ is exact for every $B$-module $M$ (Section 1.3). By the universal property and $\operatorname{Hom}_B(B \otimes_A \Omega_{A/k}, M) = \operatorname{Hom}_A(\Omega_{A/k}, M)$, this dual sequence is
$$
0 \to \operatorname{Der}_A(B, M) \to \operatorname{Der}_k(B, M) \to \operatorname{Der}_k(A, M) ,
$$
where the second map is the inclusion and the third is the restriction along $A \to B$. A $k$-derivation of $B$ is an $A$-derivation if and only if it kills the image of $A$, which is exactness. $\square$

**Theorem P1.12.6 (conormal sequence).** Let $C = B/I$. The sequence of $C$-modules
$$
I/I^2 \xrightarrow{\ \delta\ } C \otimes_B \Omega_{B/k} \xrightarrow{\ \alpha\ } \Omega_{C/k} \to 0 ,
$$
with $\delta(f + I^2) = 1 \otimes df$, is exact.

Lean: Mathlib `KaehlerDifferential.kerCotangentToTensor` and `KaehlerDifferential.exact_kerCotangentToTensor_mapBaseChange` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* The map $\delta$ is well defined: for $f, g \in I$, $d(fg) = f\,dg + g\,df$ maps to $0$ in $C \otimes_B \Omega_{B/k}$, and $\delta$ is $C$-linear. Dualize as before with a $C$-module $M$. The dual sequence is
$$
0 \to \operatorname{Der}_k(C, M) \to \operatorname{Der}_k(B, M) \to \operatorname{Hom}_C(I/I^2, M) ,
$$
where the third map sends $D$ to its restriction to $I$. A $k$-derivation $D : B \to M$ with $D(I) = 0$ factors through $C$, since $D$ is additive, and it is then a derivation of $C$. So the dual sequence is exact. $\square$

**Corollary P1.12.7 (presentations).** Let $A = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]/(f_1, \dots, f_r)$. Then
$$
\Omega_{A/k} \cong \left( \bigoplus_{i=1}^n A\,dx_i \right) \Big/ \left( df_1, \dots, df_r \right), \qquad df_j = \sum_i \frac{\partial f_j}{\partial x_i}\,dx_i .
$$
So $\Omega_{A/k}$ is the cokernel of the map $A^r \to A^n$ given by the transpose of the Jacobian matrix $(\partial f_j/\partial x_i)$, and it is a finitely presented $A$-module.

Lean: not in Mathlib in this form; it follows from `KaehlerDifferential.exact_kerCotangentToTensor_mapBaseChange` and `KaehlerDifferential.mvPolynomialBasis`.

*Proof.* Apply the conormal sequence to $B = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and $I = (f_1, \dots, f_r)$. The module $I/I^2$ is generated by the classes of the $f_j$, and $A \otimes_B \Omega_{B/k}$ is free on the $dx_i$ by the proposition on polynomial rings. $\square$

**Proposition P1.12.8 (localization and base change).** Let $S \subseteq B$ be a multiplicative set. Then $S^{-1}\Omega_{B/k} \cong \Omega_{S^{-1}B/k}$, with $db/s \mapsto d(b)/s$. If $k \to k'$ is a ring map, then $\Omega_{(B \otimes_k k')/k'} \cong \Omega_{B/k} \otimes_k k'$.

Lean: Mathlib `KaehlerDifferential.isLocalizedModule_of_isLocalizedModule` and `KaehlerDifferential.isBaseChange` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* For localization, a $k$-derivation $D : B \to M$ into an $S^{-1}B$-module extends uniquely to $S^{-1}B$ by the quotient rule $D(b/s) = (s\,D(b) - b\,D(s))/s^2$; the uniqueness holds because $0 = D(s \cdot s^{-1}) = s\,D(s^{-1}) + s^{-1}D(s)$ forces $D(s^{-1}) = -D(s)/s^2$. The direct check that the quotient rule is well defined and is a derivation is Exercise 1. So $\operatorname{Der}_k(S^{-1}B, M) = \operatorname{Der}_k(B, M) = \operatorname{Hom}_B(\Omega_{B/k}, M) = \operatorname{Hom}_{S^{-1}B}(S^{-1}\Omega_{B/k}, M)$ for every $S^{-1}B$-module $M$, and the two modules represent the same functor. For base change, a $k'$-derivation of $B \otimes_k k'$ into a module $M$ is the same as a $k$-derivation of $B$ into $M$, since it is determined by its values on $B \otimes 1$ and kills $1 \otimes k'$. So both modules represent the same functor on $(B \otimes_k k')$-modules. $\square$

**Proposition P1.12.9 (cotangent space at a rational point).** Let $k$ be a field, $B$ a $k$-algebra and $\mathfrak{m}$ a maximal ideal with $B/\mathfrak{m} = k$. Then the map $\delta : \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 \to \Omega_{B/k} \otimes_B k$ of the conormal sequence is an isomorphism of $k$-vector spaces.

Lean: not in Mathlib in this form.

*Proof.* The conormal sequence for $B \to B/\mathfrak{m} = k$ ends with $\Omega_{k/k} = 0$, so $\delta$ is surjective. For each $b \in B$ let $\lambda(b) \in k$ be its residue, so that $b - \lambda(b) \in \mathfrak{m}$. The map $D : B \to \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$, $b \mapsto b - \lambda(b)$, is $k$-linear, and it is a derivation into the $B$-module $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$, on which $b$ acts by $\lambda(b)$:
$$
bc - \lambda(b)\lambda(c) = \lambda(b)(c - \lambda(c)) + \lambda(c)(b - \lambda(b)) + (b - \lambda(b))(c - \lambda(c)) ,
$$
and the last term lies in $\mathfrak{m}^2$. By the universal property it gives a $k$-linear map $\Omega_{B/k} \otimes_B k \to \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ that sends $\delta(f) = 1 \otimes df$ to $D(f) = f$ for $f \in \mathfrak{m}$. So $\delta$ has a left inverse and is injective. $\square$

For $B = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and the ideal $P$ of a rational point, $\delta$ is the isomorphism $D$ of Lemma P1.9.11. For a maximal ideal $P$ whose residue field $L$ is separable over $k$, Lemma P1.9.11 says that $\delta : P/P^2 \to L \otimes_B \Omega_{B/k}$ is again an isomorphism, which matches the conormal sequence, since $\Omega_{L/k} = 0$ by Section 12.2.

### Proof in words

A derivation is a map that obeys the product rule, and the module of differentials is the free target for such maps. Differentials of polynomials are given by partial derivatives, and every finitely generated algebra is a quotient of a polynomial ring, so its differentials are the free module on the $dx_i$ modulo the differentials of the equations. The two exact sequences come from the remark that a derivation that kills a subring or an ideal is a derivation relative to the subring or of the quotient. At a rational point, the differentials modulo the maximal ideal are the cotangent space, because "value minus residue" is a derivation into $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$.

### Examples

**Example P1.12.10 (the node).** Let $A = k[x, y]/(y^2 - x^2 - x^3)$, with $\operatorname{char} k \neq 2$. By the corollary, $\Omega_{A/k}$ is generated by $dx, dy$ with the one relation $2y\,dy = (2x + 3x^2)\,dx$. At the origin $\mathfrak{m} = (x, y)$, the relation vanishes, so $\Omega_{A/k} \otimes_A k$ has dimension $2$, which is the dimension of $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$, as Proposition P1.12.9 says. At a smooth point $(a, b)$ of the curve, one of $2b$ and $2a + 3a^2$ is nonzero, and $\Omega_{A/k} \otimes_A \kappa$ has dimension $1$.

**Example P1.12.11 (a torsion differential on the cusp).** Let $\operatorname{char} k \neq 2, 3$ and $A = k[x, y]/(y^2 - x^3) \cong k[t^2, t^3]$. Then $\Omega_{A/k}$ is generated by $dx, dy$ with the relation $2y\,dy = 3x^2\,dx$. Put $\omega = 2x\,dy - 3y\,dx$. Then
$$
y\,\omega = 2xy\,dy - 3y^2\,dx = x(2y\,dy) - 3x^3\,dx = x \cdot 3x^2\,dx - 3x^3\,dx = 0 ,
$$
using $y^2 = x^3$. So $\omega$ is killed by the nonzerodivisor $y$. Give $x, y, dx, dy$ the degrees $2, 3, 2, 3$. The relation is homogeneous of degree $6$, so $\Omega_{A/k}$ is graded, and its part of degree $5$ is spanned by $y\,dx$ and $x\,dy$ with no relation, since the submodule generated by the relation starts in degree $6$. So $\omega \neq 0$, and $\Omega_{A/k}$ has torsion. In terms of $t$, $dx = 2t\,dt$ and $dy = 3t^2\,dt$ in $\Omega_{k(t)/k}$, and $\omega$ maps to $6t^4\,dt - 6t^4\,dt = 0$.

**Example P1.12.12 (the plane and its cotangent spaces).** For $R = k[x, y]$ and the point $(a, b)$, the proposition on the cotangent space gives $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 \cong k\,dx \oplus k\,dy$, with $f \mapsto f_x(a, b)\,dx + f_y(a, b)\,dy$. This is the differential of $f$ at the point, the linear part of the Taylor expansion of $f$ at $(a, b)$.

### Exercises

1. (A) Let $D : B \to M$ be a $k$-derivation into an $S^{-1}B$-module. Show that $D(b/s) = (s\,D(b) - b\,D(s))/s^2$ is well defined on $S^{-1}B$ and is a derivation.
2. (A) Compute $\Omega_{A/k}$ for $A = k[x]/(x^n)$, and show that it is a cyclic $A$-module of length $n - 1$ if $n \neq 0$ in $k$, and of length $n$ if $n = 0$ in $k$.
3. (A) Let $A = k \times k$. Show that $\Omega_{A/k} = 0$. Let $A = k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$. Show that $\Omega_{A/k} \cong k\,d\varepsilon$.
4. (B) Let $B = k[x, y]$ and $I = (x, y)^2$. Show that the conormal map $I/I^2 \to (B/I) \otimes_B \Omega_{B/k}$ is not injective.
5. (B) Let $A$ be a $k$-algebra and $B = A[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. Show that $\Omega_{B/k} \cong (B \otimes_A \Omega_{A/k}) \oplus \bigoplus_i B\,dx_i$, so that the first fundamental sequence is split exact on the left in this case.
6. (B) Let $A = k[x, y, z]/(xy - z^2)$, the quadric cone, with $\operatorname{char} k \neq 2$. Show that $\Omega_{A/k}$ has rank $2$ and that $\Omega_{A/k} \otimes_A k$ has dimension $3$ at the vertex.
7. (C) Let $A = k[t^2, t^3]$ and $\bar{A} = k[t]$ with $\operatorname{char} k \neq 2, 3$. Show that the kernel of $\Omega_{A/k} \to \Omega_{\bar{A}/k}$ is the torsion submodule of $\Omega_{A/k}$, and that it has dimension $2$ over $k$, with basis $\omega$ and $x\omega$ for the form $\omega$ of the example on the cusp. (Hint: use the grading and compute the dimension of each graded piece of $\Omega_{A/k}$.)

### Notes and history

The differentials of a ring are named after Erich Kähler, who introduced them in the 1930s in his work on the algebraic theory of differential forms. Grothendieck put them at the centre of the theory of smooth and étale morphisms in EGA IV. The two exact sequences are the first steps of the cotangent complex of André and Quillen. The literature state of every result here is theorem. The section follows Matsumura, Chapter 9, and Eisenbud, Chapter 16.
