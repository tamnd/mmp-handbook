# Regular local rings

This chapter studies the local rings of smooth points. A Noetherian local ring $(A, \mathfrak{m}, k)$ always satisfies $\dim A \leq \dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ by Section 5.2, and it is *regular* when equality holds: the Zariski tangent space at the point has the same dimension as the variety. Section 9.1 proves that a regular local ring is a domain whose associated graded ring is a polynomial ring, that the quotients by part of a regular system of parameters are again regular, and that regularity passes to and from the completion. Section 9.2 proves the Jacobian criterion, which computes the tangent space of an affine variety from the partial derivatives of its equations, and deduces that the singular locus is closed. Section 9.3 states Serre's homological characterization of regular local rings, deduces that a localization of a regular local ring is regular, and proves the theorem of Auslander and Buchsbaum that a regular local ring is factorial.

For a variety $X$ over a perfect field and a closed point $x$, the local ring $\mathcal{O}_{X, x}$ is regular exactly when $x$ is a smooth point in the sense of the Jacobian matrix. The theorem of Auslander and Buchsbaum then says that a smooth variety is locally factorial, so by Section 8.3 every Weil divisor on it is Cartier and its class group is its Picard group. This is the promise of Chapter 8, and it is the reason that the canonical divisor of a smooth variety is a Cartier divisor with a line bundle $\omega_X$. A normal variety is regular in codimension one by Section 8.1, so its singular locus has codimension at least two.

In the minimal model program the singular points that remain after the program runs are measured against the smooth ones. Terminal surface singularities are smooth points, the Du Val singularities of Volume 4 are surface singularities $f = 0$ with $f$ of order two, and their local class groups are finite, and a $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial variety is one whose local class groups are torsion. Smooth points are the first case in each of these classifications: by Section 9.3 their local class groups vanish, and by Section 9.2 they are the points where the Jacobian matrix has the expected rank.

The chapter follows Matsumura, Chapters 5 and 7, Eisenbud, Chapters 10, 16 and 19, and Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapter 11. It uses Chapters 1 to 8, in particular the dimension theorem of Section 5.3, the completion of Section 6.2, the discrete valuation rings of Section 7.2 and the class groups of Chapter 8.
