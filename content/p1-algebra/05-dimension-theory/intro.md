# Dimension theory

This chapter measures the size of a ring by the length of its chains of prime ideals. Section 5.1 defines the Krull dimension and the height of a prime, and gives the first properties and the first warnings. Section 5.2 proves Krull's principal ideal theorem and the height theorem: in a Noetherian ring a prime that is minimal over an ideal with $r$ generators has height at most $r$. So a Noetherian local ring has finite dimension, and it has a system of parameters. Section 5.3 proves Hilbert's theorem on the Hilbert function of a graded module and the Artin-Rees lemma, and uses them for the dimension theorem: for a Noetherian local ring, the Krull dimension, the degree of the Hilbert-Samuel polynomial and the least number of generators of a primary ideal for the maximal ideal are the same number. Section 5.4 returns to finitely generated algebras over a field and proves that their maximal chains of primes all have the same length, so that $\operatorname{ht} \mathfrak{p} + \dim A/\mathfrak{p} = \dim A$ for a domain. It ends with the dimension formula for a flat local homomorphism and with $\dim A[x] = \dim A + 1$.

For varieties, the Krull dimension of the coordinate ring is the dimension of the variety, and the height of a prime is the codimension of the subvariety it defines. The principal ideal theorem says that a hypersurface section of a variety has codimension at most one at every component, and the height theorem says that $r$ equations cut out a subvariety of codimension at most $r$. The Hilbert-Samuel polynomial of the local ring at a point has degree the local dimension, and its leading coefficient is the multiplicity of the point: $1$ at a smooth point, $2$ at a node or a cusp. The flat dimension formula says that in a flat family the fibres have the expected dimension.

The minimal model program counts dimensions all the time. A prime divisor is a subvariety of codimension one, and the principal ideal theorem shows that the zero set of a function is a union of prime divisors, which is the start of the theory of Weil divisors in Chapter 8. The codimension of the exceptional locus decides whether a contraction is divisorial or small (Volume 7), and a flip exists only for a small contraction. The dimension formula for flat morphisms is the tool for families of varieties in Volume 13. Systems of parameters come back in Chapter 10, where a Cohen-Macaulay ring is one whose systems of parameters are regular sequences.

The chapter follows Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapters 10 and 11, Matsumura, Chapter 5, and Eisenbud, Chapters 8 to 13. It uses Chapters 1 to 4.
