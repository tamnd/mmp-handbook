## Chain conditions

### Statement

**Definition P1.3.1 (Noetherian and Artinian modules).** An $A$-module $M$ is *Noetherian* if every increasing chain $N_1 \subseteq N_2 \subseteq \dots$ of submodules is eventually constant (the ascending chain condition). It is *Artinian* if every decreasing chain $N_1 \supseteq N_2 \supseteq \dots$ is eventually constant (the descending chain condition). A ring $A$ is Noetherian, or Artinian, if it is so as an $A$-module, that is, if its ideals satisfy the condition.

Lean: Mathlib `IsNoetherian`, `IsNoetherianRing`, `IsArtinian` and `IsArtinianRing` (definitions).

**Example P1.3.2.** A field is Noetherian and Artinian. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian, since every ideal is principal and $(a_1) \subseteq (a_2) \subseteq \dots$ forces $a_{i+1} \mid a_i$, which can happen with $a_{i+1} \neq \pm a_i$ only finitely often. It is not Artinian: $(2) \supsetneq (4) \supsetneq (8) \supsetneq \dots$.

**Example P1.3.3.** The polynomial ring $k[x_1, x_2, \dots]$ in infinitely many variables is not Noetherian: $(x_1) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2) \subsetneq \dots$. The $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$ is Artinian but not Noetherian: its proper submodules are the finite cyclic groups $p^{-n}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}$, which form one increasing chain that does not stop.

**Proposition P1.3.4 (forms of the ascending chain condition).** For an $A$-module $M$ the following are equivalent: (a) $M$ is Noetherian; (b) every nonempty set of submodules of $M$ has a maximal element; (c) every submodule of $M$ is finitely generated. The same holds for (a') and (b') with the descending chain condition and minimal elements.

Lean: Mathlib `set_has_maximal_iff_noetherian` and `isNoetherian_iff_fg_wellFounded` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* (a) implies (b): if a nonempty set has no maximal element, choose $N_1$ in it and then, step by step, $N_{i+1}$ in it with $N_i \subsetneq N_{i+1}$; this chain does not stop. (b) implies (c): given $N$, the set of finitely generated submodules of $N$ is nonempty and has a maximal element $N_0$. For $x \in N$, $N_0 + Ax$ is finitely generated and contains $N_0$, so it equals $N_0$ and $x \in N_0$. So $N = N_0$. (c) implies (a): the union $N$ of an increasing chain is a submodule, generated by finitely many elements. They all lie in some $N_i$, and then $N_i = N_{i+1} = \dots = N$. The proof for (a') and (b') is the first step with the inclusions reversed. $\square$

**Proposition P1.3.5 (exact sequences).** Let $0 \to M' \xrightarrow{f} M \xrightarrow{g} M'' \to 0$ be exact. Then $M$ is Noetherian if and only if $M'$ and $M''$ are Noetherian, and the same holds for Artinian.

Lean: Mathlib `isNoetherian_iff_submodule_quotient` and the instances for submodules and quotients of `IsNoetherian` and `IsArtinian` modules (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* A chain in $M'$ maps by $f$ to a chain in $M$, and a chain in $M''$ pulls back by $g$ to a chain in $M$; both maps are injective on submodules. So the condition passes from $M$ to $M'$ and $M''$. For the converse, first a remark: if $N \subseteq N'$ are submodules of $M$ with $f^{-1}(N) = f^{-1}(N')$ and $g(N) = g(N')$, then $N = N'$. Indeed, for $x \in N'$ pick $y \in N$ with $g(y) = g(x)$; then $x - y \in N' \cap f(M')$, so $x - y = f(z)$ with $z \in f^{-1}(N') = f^{-1}(N)$, and $x = y + f(z) \in N$. Now given a chain $(N_i)$ in $M$, the chains $f^{-1}(N_i)$ and $g(N_i)$ stop, and by the remark the chain $(N_i)$ stops. $\square$

**Corollary P1.3.6.** Finite direct sums of Noetherian modules are Noetherian. If $A$ is Noetherian, then every finitely generated $A$-module is Noetherian, and finitely presented. Quotients $A/I$ and localizations $S^{-1}A$ of a Noetherian ring $A$ are Noetherian. With Artinian in place of Noetherian, the statements on sums, finitely generated modules and quotients still hold. For localization see Exercise 4.

Lean: Mathlib `isNoetherian_of_isNoetherianRing_of_finite`, `Ideal.Quotient.isNoetherianRing` and `IsLocalization.isNoetherianRing` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* For direct sums use $0 \to M_1 \to M_1 \oplus M_2 \to M_2 \to 0$ and induction. A finitely generated module is a quotient of $A^n$, which is Noetherian, and the kernel of $A^n \to M$ is a submodule of $A^n$, so it is finitely generated. Ideals of $A/I$ are the $A$-submodules of $A/I$. For a localization, every ideal $J$ of $S^{-1}A$ is $S^{-1}(J \cap A)$, by the proof of the theorem on primes in Section 2.1, so a chain of ideals of $S^{-1}A$ comes from a chain of ideals of $A$. $\square$

### Proof in words

The ascending chain condition is the statement that every submodule has a finite set of generators. It passes to submodules, quotients and extensions, so it holds for every finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring. Its main use is the existence of maximal elements: to prove that every ideal has a property, take a maximal counterexample and derive a contradiction. Section 2.4 used this to prove the Lasker-Noether theorem.

In geometry, the ascending chain condition on the ideals of $k[X]$ is the descending chain condition on the closed subsets of $X$ (Section 3.3). A decreasing chain of closed subvarieties must stop. This is the base of every induction on closed subsets, and of Noetherian induction, which the MMP uses whenever it proves a statement on a dense open set and then passes to the complement.

### Examples

**Example P1.3.7 (a subring of a Noetherian ring).** Let $B = k[x, xy, xy^2, xy^3, \dots] \subseteq k[x, y]$. A monomial $x^ay^b$ with $a \geq 1$ is $(xy^b)x^{a-1} \in B$, so $B = k + xk[x, y]$. The ideal $I = (x, xy, xy^2, \dots)B$ is not finitely generated. An element of $(x, xy, \dots, xy^{n-1})B$ is a sum of terms $xy^j(c + xg)$ with $j < n$, and its monomials of $x$-degree $1$ have $y$-degree $< n$. So $xy^n$ is not in it. Hence $B$ is not Noetherian, although it lies in the Noetherian ring $k[x, y]$ and has the same fraction field. Geometrically, $\operatorname{Spec} B$ is the plane with the line $x = 0$ crushed to a point.

**Example P1.3.8 (a local ring that is not Noetherian).** Let $A = k[x_1, x_2, \dots]/(x_1, x_2, \dots)^2$. Its only prime is $\mathfrak{m} = (x_1, x_2, \dots)$, since $\mathfrak{m}^2 = 0$, so $A$ is local of dimension $0$. But $\mathfrak{m}$ is an infinite dimensional vector space with zero multiplication, and its subspaces give infinite increasing and decreasing chains of ideals. So $A$ is neither Noetherian nor Artinian. Section 3.5 shows that for Noetherian rings, dimension $0$ is the same as Artinian.

**Example P1.3.9 (rings of functions).** The ring $C([0, 1])$ of continuous real functions is not Noetherian. The ideals $I_n = \{f : f = 0 \text{ on } [0, 1/n]\}$ form a chain $I_1 \subsetneq I_2 \subsetneq \dots$ that does not stop. The ring of germs of smooth functions at $0 \in \mathbb{R}$ is not Noetherian either, since the ideal of flat germs, those with all derivatives zero at $0$, is not finitely generated. Algebraic geometry works with polynomial rings, which are Noetherian, and complex analytic geometry with convergent power series, which are Noetherian too.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that a principal ideal domain is Noetherian. Show that in a Noetherian domain every nonzero nonunit is a product of irreducible elements. (Hint: a maximal principal ideal $(a)$ with $a$ not such a product.)
2. (A) Show that if $A$ is Noetherian and $\varphi: A \to A$ is a surjective ring homomorphism, then $\varphi$ is injective. (Hint: the chain $\ker \varphi^n$.)
3. (A) Let $M$ be Noetherian and $u: M \to M$ a surjective linear map. Show that $u$ is an isomorphism. Compare with the corollary of Cayley-Hamilton in Section 1.2.
4. (B) Show that if $A$ is Artinian, so is $S^{-1}A$.
5. (B) Let $M$ be an $A$-module and $N_1, N_2$ submodules with $M/N_1$ and $M/N_2$ Noetherian. Show that $M/(N_1 \cap N_2)$ is Noetherian.
6. (C) Let $A$ be a ring in which every prime ideal is finitely generated. Show that $A$ is Noetherian. (Hint: consider a maximal element of the set of ideals that are not finitely generated, and show that it is prime. This is a theorem of Cohen.)

### Notes and history

Emmy Noether introduced the ascending chain condition in her 1921 paper on ideal theory, and she showed that it is the condition behind Hilbert's finiteness theorems and the Lasker decomposition. Artin introduced the descending chain condition in 1927 for noncommutative rings. Cohen's theorem in Exercise 6 is from 1950. The literature state of every result here is theorem. The section follows Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapter 6.
