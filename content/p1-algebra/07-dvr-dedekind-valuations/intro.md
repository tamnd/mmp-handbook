# DVRs, Dedekind domains and valuations

This chapter studies the rings that measure the order of zeros and poles. Section 7.1 defines valuation rings and valuations, proves that every local subring of a field is dominated by a valuation ring, and shows that the integral closure of a domain is the intersection of the valuation rings that contain it. Section 7.2 proves the main characterization of discrete valuation rings: for a Noetherian local domain of dimension one, the conditions normal, principal maximal ideal, embedding dimension one and every ideal a power of the maximal ideal are all the same. It defines the order of vanishing of a function as a length, and so it keeps the promise of Section 3.4. Section 7.3 proves that in a Dedekind domain every nonzero ideal is a unique product of prime ideals, and defines the class group, the obstruction to unique factorization of elements.

For a variety, a discrete valuation ring is the local ring of a normal curve at a point, and also the local ring of a normal variety at the generic point of a prime divisor. Its valuation is the order of vanishing along the divisor: a rational function $f$ has a zero of order $n > 0$, or a pole of order $-n$, along the divisor. A Dedekind domain is the coordinate ring of a normal affine curve, or the ring of integers of a number field. Its class group is the group of divisors modulo principal divisors, the first example of the class group of Chapter 8 and of the Picard group of a curve.

The minimal model program measures singularities with valuations. Let $f: Y \to X$ be a birational morphism of normal varieties and $E \subseteq Y$ a prime divisor. The local ring of $Y$ at the generic point of $E$ is a discrete valuation ring of the function field, and its valuation $\operatorname{ord}_E$ does not depend on the model $Y$. Such a valuation is called a *divisorial valuation*. The discrepancy $a(E, X)$ of Volume 4 is defined by comparing $K_Y$ and $f^*K_X$ along $E$, that is, by applying $\operatorname{ord}_E$ to a rational differential form. The classes terminal, canonical, klt and lc are conditions on the discrepancies of all divisorial valuations. The divisor of a rational function $f$ on a normal variety is the finite sum $\operatorname{div}(f) = \sum_D \operatorname{ord}_D(f) D$ over the prime divisors $D$, and the cone and contraction theorems of Volume 7 are statements about such divisors.

The chapter follows Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapters 5 and 9, Matsumura, Chapter 4, and Eisenbud, Chapter 11. It uses Chapters 1 to 6, in particular the theory of integral extensions of Chapter 4 and the length of Section 3.4.
