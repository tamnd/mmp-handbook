## Tensor products

### Statement

**Definition P1.1.32 (tensor product).** Let $M, N$ be $A$-modules. A *tensor product* of $M$ and $N$ is an $A$-module $T$ with an $A$-bilinear map $\beta: M \times N \to T$ such that for every $A$-module $P$ and every bilinear map $f: M \times N \to P$ there is a unique $A$-linear map $\tilde f: T \to P$ with $f = \tilde f \circ \beta$. One writes $T = M \otimes_A N$ and $m \otimes n = \beta(m, n)$.

Lean: Mathlib `TensorProduct`, with the notation `M ⊗[R] N`, the elements `m ⊗ₜ n` and the universal map `TensorProduct.lift`.

**Theorem P1.1.33 (existence).** The tensor product exists, and it is unique up to a unique isomorphism compatible with $\beta$. It is generated as an $A$-module by the elements $m \otimes n$.

Lean: Mathlib `TensorProduct` and `TensorProduct.lift` (a definition; the uniqueness is `TensorProduct.ext`).

*Proof.* Uniqueness is the usual argument for universal properties: if $(T, \beta)$ and $(T', \beta')$ both have the property, the maps $\tilde\beta': T \to T'$ and $\tilde\beta: T' \to T$ compose to maps $T \to T$ and $T' \to T'$ that are compatible with $\beta$ and $\beta'$. By the uniqueness part of the property these compositions are the identities. For existence, let $F$ be the free $A$-module on the set $M \times N$, with basis elements $[m, n]$. Let $R \subseteq F$ be the submodule generated by all elements

$$[m + m', n] - [m, n] - [m', n], \quad [m, n + n'] - [m, n] - [m, n'], \quad [am, n] - a[m, n], \quad [m, an] - a[m, n].$$

Put $T = F/R$ and $\beta(m, n) = $ the class of $[m, n]$. The relations make $\beta$ bilinear. A bilinear map $f$ defines a linear map $F \to P$ by $[m, n] \mapsto f(m, n)$, which kills $R$ because $f$ is bilinear, so it factors through $T$. The factorization is unique because the $[m, n]$ generate $T$. $\square$

An element of $M \otimes_A N$ is a finite sum $\sum m_i \otimes n_i$. Such a presentation is not unique, and an element $m \otimes n$ can be zero for nonzero $m$ and $n$. To prove that an element is zero one usually maps the tensor product somewhere with the universal property. To prove that a map out of $M \otimes N$ is well defined, one gives a bilinear map on $M \times N$.

**Proposition P1.1.34 (basic isomorphisms).** For $A$-modules $M, N, P$ and a family $(M_\lambda)$ there are unique isomorphisms with

1. $A \otimes_A M \cong M$, $a \otimes m \mapsto am$;
2. $M \otimes N \cong N \otimes M$, $m \otimes n \mapsto n \otimes m$;
3. $(M \otimes N) \otimes P \cong M \otimes (N \otimes P)$, $(m \otimes n) \otimes p \mapsto m \otimes (n \otimes p)$;
4. $(\bigoplus_\lambda M_\lambda) \otimes N \cong \bigoplus_\lambda (M_\lambda \otimes N)$;
5. $\operatorname{Hom}_A(M \otimes N, P) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_A(M, \operatorname{Hom}_A(N, P))$, $u \mapsto (m \mapsto (n \mapsto u(m \otimes n)))$.

In particular $A^m \otimes_A A^n \cong A^{mn}$.

Lean: Mathlib `TensorProduct.lid`, `TensorProduct.comm`, `TensorProduct.assoc`, `TensorProduct.directSum` and `TensorProduct.lift.equiv` (definitions, with their properties proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* In each case the universal property gives a map from left to right, and a map from right to left is defined in the same way. For 1, the inverse is $m \mapsto 1 \otimes m$, and $a \otimes m = 1 \otimes am$ shows that it is onto. For 3, fix $p$; the map $(m, n) \mapsto m \otimes (n \otimes p)$ is bilinear, which gives $(M \otimes N) \times P \to M \otimes (N \otimes P)$, bilinear in the two variables; the inverse is built in the same way. For 4, the inclusions $M_\lambda \to \bigoplus M_\lambda$ give maps $M_\lambda \otimes N \to (\bigoplus M_\lambda) \otimes N$, and the bilinear map $((m_\lambda), n) \mapsto (m_\lambda \otimes n)$ gives the inverse. For 5, a linear map from $M \otimes N$ is the same as a bilinear map on $M \times N$, which is the same as a linear map from $M$ to the linear maps from $N$. In each case the two maps are inverse on the generators $m \otimes n$, so they are inverse. $\square$

**Theorem P1.1.35 (right exactness).** Let $M' \xrightarrow{f} M \xrightarrow{g} M'' \to 0$ be exact and let $N$ be an $A$-module. Then

$$M' \otimes N \xrightarrow{f \otimes 1} M \otimes N \xrightarrow{g \otimes 1} M'' \otimes N \to 0$$

is exact.

Lean: Mathlib `rTensor_exact` and `LinearMap.rTensor_surjective` (proved in Mathlib, in `Mathlib.LinearAlgebra.TensorProduct.RightExactness`).

*Proof.* By the proposition on the left exactness of Hom in Section 1.2, the given sequence is exact if and only if $0 \to \operatorname{Hom}(M'', Q) \to \operatorname{Hom}(M, Q) \to \operatorname{Hom}(M', Q)$ is exact for every module $Q$. Apply this with $Q = \operatorname{Hom}(N, P)$ for an arbitrary $P$. By the isomorphism 5 above, natural in the first variable, the sequence $0 \to \operatorname{Hom}(M'' \otimes N, P) \to \operatorname{Hom}(M \otimes N, P) \to \operatorname{Hom}(M' \otimes N, P)$ is exact for every $P$. By the same proposition again, read in the other direction, the tensored sequence is exact. $\square$

**Corollary P1.1.36.** For an ideal $I$ and a module $M$, $(A/I) \otimes_A M \cong M/IM$, by $\bar a \otimes m \mapsto \overline{am}$. For ideals $I, J$, $A/I \otimes_A A/J \cong A/(I + J)$.

Lean: Mathlib `TensorProduct.quotTensorEquivQuotSMul` (a definition, with its properties proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* Tensor the exact sequence $I \to A \to A/I \to 0$ with $M$. By right exactness, $I \otimes M \to M \to (A/I) \otimes M \to 0$ is exact, where the first map sends $a \otimes m$ to $am$. Its image is $IM$. For the second claim take $M = A/J$, where $I(A/J) = (I + J)/J$ and $(A/J)/((I+J)/J) \cong A/(I + J)$. $\square$

**Definition P1.1.37 (extension of scalars and tensor products of algebras).** Let $A \to B$ be a ring homomorphism and $M$ an $A$-module. Then $B \otimes_A M$ is a $B$-module by $b(b' \otimes m) = bb' \otimes m$. It is the *extension of scalars* of $M$ to $B$. If $B$ and $C$ are $A$-algebras, then $B \otimes_A C$ is an $A$-algebra with $(b \otimes c)(b' \otimes c') = bb' \otimes cc'$.

Lean: Mathlib `TensorProduct.AlgebraTensorModule` for the module structure, `LinearMap.baseChange` for maps, and the algebra structure on `A ⊗[R] B` in the namespace `Algebra.TensorProduct`.

**Proposition P1.1.38 (coproduct of algebras).** Let $B, C$ be $A$-algebras. The maps $B \to B \otimes_A C$, $b \mapsto b \otimes 1$, and $C \to B \otimes_A C$, $c \mapsto 1 \otimes c$, are $A$-algebra maps. For every $A$-algebra $D$ and $A$-algebra maps $u: B \to D$, $v: C \to D$ there is a unique $A$-algebra map $w: B \otimes_A C \to D$ with $w(b \otimes c) = u(b) v(c)$. For example $B \otimes_A A[x_1, \dots, x_n] \cong B[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, and $B \otimes_A A[x]/(f) \cong B[x]/(f)$, where $f$ also denotes the image of $f$ in $B[x]$.

Lean: Mathlib `Algebra.TensorProduct.lift` and `polyEquivTensor` (definitions, with their properties proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* The multiplication on $B \otimes C$ is well defined: for fixed $(b', c')$ the map $(b, c) \mapsto bb' \otimes cc'$ is bilinear, and the resulting map is bilinear in $(b', c')$. The ring axioms hold on generators, hence everywhere. The map $(b, c) \mapsto u(b)v(c)$ is $A$-bilinear, so $w$ exists as a linear map, and it is multiplicative on generators. Uniqueness holds since the $b \otimes c = (b \otimes 1)(1 \otimes c)$ generate. For the polynomial ring, the universal property of $B[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and the one just proved give inverse maps. For the quotient, tensor the exact sequence $A[x] \xrightarrow{f} A[x] \to A[x]/(f) \to 0$ with $B$ and use right exactness. $\square$

### Proof in words

The tensor product is built to turn bilinear maps into linear maps. Everything else follows from this universal property, so a proof about tensor products is usually a proof about bilinear maps. Right exactness comes from the adjunction between $\otimes N$ and $\operatorname{Hom}(N, -)$: Hom out of a module turns cokernels into kernels, and the adjunction moves this across.

In geometry, the tensor product of algebras is the fibre product. If $X = \operatorname{Spec} B$ and $Y = \operatorname{Spec} C$ lie over $Z = \operatorname{Spec} A$, then $X \times_Z Y = \operatorname{Spec}(B \otimes_A C)$; P3 proves this. The most used case is the fibre of a morphism. For a point $\mathfrak{p}$ of $\operatorname{Spec} A$ with residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ (Chapter 2), the fibre of $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$ over $\mathfrak{p}$ is $\operatorname{Spec}(B \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p}))$. When $\mathfrak{p}$ is the maximal ideal of a point $a$ of a variety, $B \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = B/\mathfrak{p}B$, the ring of the fibre over $a$.

### Examples

**Example P1.1.39 (cyclic groups).** Let $d = \gcd(m, n)$. Then $\mathbb{Z}/m \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n \cong \mathbb{Z}/(m, n) = \mathbb{Z}/d$ by the corollary. So $\mathbb{Z}/2 \otimes \mathbb{Z}/3 = 0$, although both factors are nonzero. Also $\mathbb{Q} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/n = 0$: for $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $x \in \mathbb{Z}/n$, $q \otimes x = (q/n) n \otimes x = (q/n) \otimes nx = 0$. Tensoring with $\mathbb{Q}$ kills torsion.

**Example P1.1.40 (two points from one).** Let $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1)$. Then $\mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C}[x]/(x^2 + 1) = \mathbb{C}[x]/((x - i)(x + i)) \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}$, by the coproduct proposition and the Chinese remainder theorem. Geometrically, $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{C}$ is one point over $\mathbb{R}$, and after the base change to $\mathbb{C}$ it becomes two points. A tensor product of two fields need not be a field, nor a domain.

**Example P1.1.41 (fibres of a double cover).** Let $k$ be algebraically closed of characteristic not $2$, $A = k[x]$ and $B = k[x, y]/(y^2 - x)$. The fibre of $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$ over the point $x = a$ has ring $B \otimes_A A/(x - a) \cong k[y]/(y^2 - a)$. For $a \neq 0$ this is $k \times k$, two reduced points $y = \pm\sqrt{a}$. For $a = 0$ it is $k[y]/(y^2)$, one point with a nilpotent. The fibre has dimension $2$ over $k$ at every point: the tensor product counts the branch point with multiplicity $2$. Section 1.4 explains this constancy by flatness.

**Example P1.1.42 (left exactness fails).** The map $\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{2} \mathbb{Z}$ is injective. Tensor it with $\mathbb{Z}/2$. It becomes $\mathbb{Z}/2 \xrightarrow{2} \mathbb{Z}/2$, which is the zero map, and is not injective. So $- \otimes N$ is right exact and not exact in general. The modules $N$ for which it is exact are the subject of Section 1.4.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $k[x] \otimes_k k[y] \cong k[x, y]$ and that $k[x]/(x^2) \otimes_k k[y]/(y^3)$ has dimension $6$ over $k$.
2. (A) Show that $\mathbb{Q} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q} \cong \mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} = 0$.
3. (A) Show that the tensor product of two finitely generated modules is finitely generated, and that $B \otimes_A M$ is a finitely generated $B$-module if $M$ is a finitely generated $A$-module.
4. (B) Let $(A, \mathfrak{m}, k)$ be local and let $M, N$ be finitely generated $A$-modules with $M \otimes_A N = 0$. Show that $M = 0$ or $N = 0$. (Hint: reduce to $k$ with the corollary and Nakayama's lemma. Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapter 2, Exercise 3.)
5. (B) Let $A = k[x, y]$ and $B = k[x, u]$, made an $A$-algebra by $x \mapsto x$, $y \mapsto xu$. This is one chart of the blow-up of the origin of $\mathbb{A}^2$ (P0 Ch. 5). Compute the fibre ring $B \otimes_A A/(x - a, y - b)$ for every point $(a, b)$, and describe the fibres.
6. (B) Let $L/K$ be a finite separable extension of fields, $L = K[x]/(f)$. Show that $L \otimes_K \overline{K} \cong \overline{K}^{[L:K]}$, for an algebraic closure $\overline{K}$.

### Lean

Mathlib builds the tensor product as `TensorProduct`, written `M ⊗[R] N`, with the universal map `TensorProduct.lift` and the basic isomorphisms `TensorProduct.lid`, `TensorProduct.comm`, `TensorProduct.assoc` and `TensorProduct.directSum`. Right exactness is `rTensor_exact` and `lTensor_exact`; the isomorphism $(A/I) \otimes M \cong M/IM$ is `TensorProduct.quotTensorEquivQuotSMul`. The algebra $B \otimes_A C$ is in the namespace `Algebra.TensorProduct`, with `Algebra.TensorProduct.lift`. The handbook adds no `MinimalModel` node here.

### Notes and history

Tensor products of vector spaces were used in differential geometry and physics in the nineteenth century. Whitney defined the tensor product of abelian groups in 1938, and Bourbaki gave the modern definition by the universal property. Cartan and Eilenberg made right exactness the starting point of homological algebra in 1956. The literature state of every result here is theorem. The section follows Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapter 2. P2 recasts right exactness as the statement that a left adjoint preserves colimits, and P3 uses the coproduct proposition to construct fibre products of schemes.
