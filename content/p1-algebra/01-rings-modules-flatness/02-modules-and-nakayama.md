## Modules, exact sequences and Nakayama's lemma

### Statement

**Definition P1.1.17 (modules).** Let $A$ be a ring. An $A$-module is an abelian group $M$ with a map $A \times M \to M$, $(a, m) \mapsto am$, that is bilinear and satisfies $(ab)m = a(bm)$ and $1m = m$. A homomorphism of $A$-modules is an $A$-linear map. The set $\operatorname{Hom}_A(M, N)$ of homomorphisms is an $A$-module. A submodule $N \subseteq M$ gives a quotient module $M/N$. For an ideal $I$, the submodule $IM$ is the set of finite sums $\sum a_i m_i$ with $a_i \in I$, $m_i \in M$. The module $M$ is *finitely generated* if $M = Am_1 + \cdots + Am_n$ for some $m_1, \dots, m_n$, and *free* if it is isomorphic to a direct sum $\bigoplus_{\lambda} A$.

Lean: Mathlib `Module`, `Submodule`, `Module.Finite` (finitely generated) and `Module.Free`.

**Example P1.1.18.** An ideal $I \subseteq A$ and a quotient $A/I$ are $A$-modules. Over a field, a module is a vector space, and it is free. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, a module is an abelian group, and $\mathbb{Z}/n$ for $n \geq 2$ is not free, since $n$ kills it.

**Example P1.1.19.** A module over $k[t]$ is a vector space $V$ over $k$ with one endomorphism, the action of $t$. The module is finitely generated and killed by a nonzero polynomial exactly when $V$ is finite dimensional. The classification of these modules over the principal ideal domain $k[t]$ is the theory of the Jordan normal form.

**Definition P1.1.20 (exact sequences).** A sequence of homomorphisms $\cdots \to M_{i-1} \xrightarrow{f_i} M_i \xrightarrow{f_{i+1}} M_{i+1} \to \cdots$ is *exact* at $M_i$ if $\operatorname{im} f_i = \ker f_{i+1}$, and exact if it is exact at each inner term. A *short exact sequence* is an exact sequence $0 \to M' \xrightarrow{f} M \xrightarrow{g} M'' \to 0$. Then $f$ is injective, $g$ is surjective and $M'' \cong M / f(M')$.

Lean: Mathlib `Function.Exact` (the predicate that the range of one map is the kernel of the next).

**Example P1.1.21.** For an ideal $I$ the sequence $0 \to I \to A \to A/I \to 0$ is exact. For a nonzero integer $n$ the sequence $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{n} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n \to 0$ is exact. For $f \in A$ the sequence $A \xrightarrow{f} A \to A/(f) \to 0$ is exact, and it is exact on the left too exactly when $f$ is not a zero divisor.

**Proposition P1.1.22 (left exactness of Hom).** A sequence $M' \xrightarrow{f} M \xrightarrow{g} M'' \to 0$ is exact if and only if, for every module $N$, the sequence $0 \to \operatorname{Hom}(M'', N) \xrightarrow{g^*} \operatorname{Hom}(M, N) \xrightarrow{f^*} \operatorname{Hom}(M', N)$ is exact. A sequence $0 \to N' \to N \to N''$ is exact if and only if $0 \to \operatorname{Hom}(M, N') \to \operatorname{Hom}(M, N) \to \operatorname{Hom}(M, N'')$ is exact for every $M$.

Lean: not in Lean in this form; Mathlib states the exactness of `LinearMap.lcomp` in special cases.

*Proof.* Suppose $M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$ is exact. If $u \circ g = 0$ then $u = 0$ because $g$ is onto, so $g^*$ is injective. Clearly $f^* g^* = (gf)^* = 0$. If $v \circ f = 0$, then $v$ is zero on $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$, so $v$ factors through $M/\ker g \cong M''$, and $v = g^*(u)$. Conversely, suppose the Hom sequences are exact for all $N$. Take $N = M''/\operatorname{im} g$ and $u$ the projection: $g^* u = 0$, so $u = 0$ and $g$ is onto. Take $N = M''$ and $u = \operatorname{id}$: $f^* g^* u = gf = 0$. Take $N = M/\operatorname{im} f$ and $v$ the projection: $f^* v = 0$, so $v = u \circ g$ for some $u$, and then $\ker g \subseteq \ker v = \operatorname{im} f$. The second statement has the same proof with the arrows reversed; take $M = A$ for the converse, since $\operatorname{Hom}(A, N) = N$. $\square$

**Theorem P1.1.23 (Cayley-Hamilton).** Let $M$ be an $A$-module generated by $n$ elements, let $I$ be an ideal, and let $\varphi: M \to M$ be an $A$-linear map with $\varphi(M) \subseteq IM$. Then there are $a_1, \dots, a_n$ with $a_i \in I^i$ such that $\varphi^n + a_1 \varphi^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0$ as an endomorphism of $M$.

Lean: Mathlib `LinearMap.exists_monic_and_coeff_mem_pow_and_aeval_eq_zero_of_range_le_smul` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* Let $m_1, \dots, m_n$ generate $M$. Since $\varphi(m_i) \in IM$, write $\varphi(m_i) = \sum_j a_{ij} m_j$ with $a_{ij} \in I$. Regard $M$ as a module over the commutative ring $A[\varphi]$. Then $\sum_j (\delta_{ij} \varphi - a_{ij}) m_j = 0$ for each $i$. Let $\Delta$ be the matrix $(\delta_{ij}\varphi - a_{ij})$ with entries in $A[\varphi]$, and let $\operatorname{adj}(\Delta)$ be its adjugate. Multiply the column of the $m_j$ by $\operatorname{adj}(\Delta)$ on the left. Since $\operatorname{adj}(\Delta) \Delta = \det(\Delta) \cdot 1$, this gives $\det(\Delta) m_j = 0$ for all $j$, so $\det(\Delta) = 0$ on $M$. Expanding the determinant gives a monic polynomial of degree $n$ in $\varphi$, whose coefficient of $\varphi^{n-i}$ is a sum of products of $i$ entries $a_{jl}$, so it lies in $I^i$. $\square$

**Corollary P1.1.24.** Let $M$ be a finitely generated $A$-module and $I$ an ideal with $IM = M$. Then there is $a \in A$ with $a \equiv 1 \pmod I$ and $aM = 0$.

Lean: Mathlib `Submodule.exists_sub_one_mem_and_smul_eq_zero_of_fg_of_le_smul` (proved in Mathlib).

*Proof.* Apply the theorem to $\varphi = \operatorname{id}$. Then $(1 + a_1 + \cdots + a_n) m = 0$ for all $m \in M$, and $a = 1 + a_1 + \cdots + a_n \equiv 1 \pmod I$. $\square$

**Theorem P1.1.25 (Nakayama's lemma).** Let $M$ be a finitely generated $A$-module and let $I \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(A)$ be an ideal. If $IM = M$, then $M = 0$. If $N \subseteq M$ is a submodule with $M = N + IM$, then $M = N$.

Lean: Mathlib `Submodule.eq_bot_of_le_smul_of_le_jacobson_bot` (proved in Mathlib; the second form is `Submodule.eq_smul_of_le_smul_of_le_jacobson` and its relatives in `Mathlib.RingTheory.Nakayama`).

*Proof.* By the corollary there is $a = 1 + x$ with $x \in I$ and $aM = 0$. By the proposition on the Jacobson radical in Section 1.1, $1 + x$ is a unit, since $x \in \operatorname{Jac}(A)$. So $M = a^{-1} a M = 0$. For the second claim apply the first to the finitely generated module $M/N$: $I(M/N) = (IM + N)/N = M/N$, so $M/N = 0$. $\square$

**Corollary P1.1.26 (minimal generators).** Let $(A, \mathfrak{m}, k)$ be a local ring and $M$ a finitely generated $A$-module. Elements $m_1, \dots, m_n \in M$ generate $M$ if and only if their images generate the $k$-vector space $M/\mathfrak{m}M$. So every set of generators of $M$ contains a set of $\dim_k M/\mathfrak{m}M$ generators, and every minimal set of generators has $\dim_k M/\mathfrak{m}M$ elements.

Lean: Mathlib `Submodule.exists_injOn_mkQ_image_span_eq_of_span_eq_map_mkQ_of_le_jacobson_bot` (proved in Mathlib; it lifts a spanning set of $M/IM$ to a spanning set of $M$).

*Proof.* If the $m_i$ generate $M$, their images generate $M/\mathfrak{m}M$. Conversely let $N = \sum A m_i$. If the images span $M/\mathfrak{m}M$, then $M = N + \mathfrak{m}M$. Since $\mathfrak{m} = \operatorname{Jac}(A)$, Nakayama's lemma gives $M = N$. A set of generators of $M$ maps to a spanning set of $M/\mathfrak{m}M$, which contains a basis; the elements over the basis generate $M$. A minimal set of generators maps to a spanning set with no redundant element, which is a basis. $\square$

**Corollary P1.1.27 (surjective endomorphisms).** Let $M$ be a finitely generated $A$-module and $\varphi: M \to M$ a surjective $A$-linear map. Then $\varphi$ is an isomorphism.

Lean: Mathlib `OrzechProperty.bijective_of_surjective_endomorphism` (proved in Mathlib, with the instance `OrzechProperty R` for every commutative ring $R$).

*Proof.* Make $M$ a module over $A[t]$ with $t$ acting by $\varphi$, and let $I = (t)$. Since $\varphi$ is onto, $IM = M$, and $M$ is finitely generated over $A[t]$. By the corollary of Cayley-Hamilton, $(1 + t g(t)) M = 0$ for some polynomial $g$. For $m \in \ker \varphi$, $0 = (1 + tg(t)) m = m + g(\varphi)\varphi(m) = m$. $\square$

### Proof in words

The determinant trick is the engine. A finitely generated module on which an endomorphism moves everything into $IM$ is killed by a monic polynomial in that endomorphism, with coefficients in powers of $I$. For the identity map this says that $IM = M$ forces some $1 + x$, $x \in I$, to kill $M$. When $I$ lies in the Jacobson radical, $1 + x$ is a unit and $M = 0$.

Geometrically, let $M$ be a finitely generated module over the local ring of a variety at a point. The vector space $M/\mathfrak{m}M$ is the fibre of $M$ at the point. Nakayama's lemma says that a basis of the fibre lifts to a set of generators of $M$. So the number of generators near a point is the dimension of the fibre, and a module with zero fibre at a point is zero near that point. This is the algebra behind the semicontinuity of fibre dimension in P4. The hypothesis "finitely generated" cannot be dropped, as the examples show.

### Examples

**Example P1.1.28 (embedding dimension).** Let $A = k[x, y]_{(x, y)}/(y^2 - x^3)$, the local ring of the cuspidal cubic at the origin, with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m} = (x, y)$. Then $\mathfrak{m}^2 = (x^2, xy, y^2) = (x^2, xy)$ in $A$, since $y^2 = x^3 \in (x^2)$. The classes of $x$ and $y$ form a basis of $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$: a relation $ax + by \in \mathfrak{m}^2 + (y^2 - x^3)$ in $k[x, y]$ with $a, b \in k$ forces $a = b = 0$, because $y^2 - x^3$ and every element of $\mathfrak{m}^2$ have no linear term. So $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 2$ and $\mathfrak{m}$ needs exactly two generators. For the local ring of the line at the origin the same number is $1$. The jump from $1$ to $2$, with the dimension of the curve still $1$, detects the singular point. P0 Ch. 6 met this as the dimension of the Zariski tangent space, and P1 Ch. 9 makes it the definition of a regular local ring.

**Example P1.1.29 (finite generation is needed).** Let $A = \mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, a local ring with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m} = p\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, and let $M = \mathbb{Q}$. Every rational number is $p$ times a rational number, so $\mathfrak{m}M = M$. But $M \neq 0$. Nakayama's lemma does not apply because $\mathbb{Q}$ is not finitely generated over $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$. The fibre of $\mathbb{Q}$ at the closed point is zero, and $\mathbb{Q}$ lives only at the generic point of $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$.

**Example P1.1.30 (the Jacobson radical is needed).** Let $A = \mathbb{Z}$, $I = (2)$ and $M = \mathbb{Z}/3$. Then $2M = M$, since $2$ is invertible modulo $3$, and $M \neq 0$. Here $(2) \not\subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(\mathbb{Z}) = 0$. The corollary of Cayley-Hamilton still holds, with $a = 3$: then $a \equiv 1 \pmod 2$ and $3M = 0$. The module lives at the point $(3)$ of $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}$, away from the point $(2)$.

**Example P1.1.31 (injective endomorphisms).** The dual of the last corollary fails. The map $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, $m \mapsto 2m$, is injective and not surjective. So an injective endomorphism of a finitely generated module need not be an isomorphism.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Z}/m, \mathbb{Z}/n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m, n)$ and that $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{Z}) = 0$.
2. (A) Apply $\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/2, -)$ to $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$ and show that the result is not exact on the right.
3. (A) Let $(A, \mathfrak{m})$ be local and let $M, N$ be finitely generated modules. Show that a homomorphism $f: M \to N$ is surjective if and only if $M/\mathfrak{m}M \to N/\mathfrak{m}N$ is surjective.
4. (B) Let $(A, \mathfrak{m})$ be a local ring and $M$ a finitely generated free $A$-module of rank $n$. Show that every set of $n$ generators of $M$ is a basis. (Hint: use the corollary on surjective endomorphisms.)
5. (B) Let $A$ be a ring and $M$ a finitely generated module with $\mathfrak{p}M = M$ for a prime $\mathfrak{p}$. Show that there is $s \notin \mathfrak{p}$ with $sM = 0$. Interpret this as: $M$ vanishes on the open set where $s \neq 0$, which contains the generic point of $V(\mathfrak{p})$.
6. (B) Compute $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ for the local ring at the origin of $xy = 0$, of $xy = z^2$, and of $y^2 = x^3 + x^2$. Which of them are singular at the origin?
7. (B) Let $I$ be a finitely generated ideal with $I = I^2$. Show that $I = (e)$ for an idempotent $e$.

### Notes and history

The determinant trick is the proof of the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, which Cayley stated in 1858 and Frobenius proved in general in 1878. Nakayama's lemma has many fathers: Krull and Azumaya proved forms of it, and Nakayama gave the general form in 1951. The surjective endomorphism corollary is due to Vasconcelos (1969), with the proof given here. The literature state of each result is theorem. The section follows Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapter 2, and Eisenbud, Chapter 4, which puts Cayley-Hamilton and Nakayama together for the same reason as here. P1 Ch. 9 uses the minimal generators corollary to define the embedding dimension, and P4 uses Nakayama's lemma to pass from fibres of a coherent sheaf to the sheaf near a point.
