## Cartier divisors, the Picard group and Q-factoriality

### Statement

Throughout, $A$ is a Noetherian domain with fraction field $K$. A *fractional ideal* is a nonzero $A$-submodule $I \subseteq K$ with $dI \subseteq A$ for some nonzero $d \in A$, as in Section 7.3. For fractional ideals $I$ and $J$ put $(J : I) = \{x \in K : xI \subseteq J\}$.

**Definition P1.8.17 (invertible ideals and the Picard group).** A fractional ideal $I$ is *invertible* if $IJ = A$ for some fractional ideal $J$. The invertible fractional ideals form a group under multiplication, and the principal fractional ideals $xA$, $x \in K^*$, form a subgroup. The quotient is the *Picard group* $\operatorname{Pic}(A)$.

Lean: Mathlib `CommRing.Pic` (the Picard group of invertible modules, definition), `Module.Invertible`, and `ClassGroup` with `ClassGroup.equivPic`, which identifies the invertible fractional ideals modulo principal ones with `CommRing.Pic` for a domain.

If $IJ = A$, then $J = (A : I)$: clearly $J \subseteq (A : I)$, and $(A : I) = (A : I)IJ \subseteq AJ = J$. So the inverse is unique, and $I$ is invertible if and only if $I(A : I) = A$. The ideal $xA$ has inverse $x^{-1}A$, and a product of invertible ideals is invertible.

**Proposition P1.8.18 (invertible means locally principal).** A fractional ideal $I$ is invertible if and only if it is finitely generated and $I_\mathfrak{m} = IA_\mathfrak{m}$ is a principal fractional ideal of $A_\mathfrak{m}$ for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$.

Lean: not in Mathlib in this form. Mathlib proves that an invertible module is finite and projective (instances in `Mathlib/RingTheory/PicardGroup.lean`).

*Proof.* Suppose $IJ = A$, and write $1 = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i$ with $a_i \in I$ and $b_i \in J$. For $x \in I$, $x = \sum a_i (b_i x)$ with $b_i x \in A$, so $I = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$. Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be maximal. Some $a_i b_i \notin \mathfrak{m}$, so $a_ib_i$ is a unit of $A_\mathfrak{m}$. For $x \in I_\mathfrak{m}$, $b_i x \in A_\mathfrak{m}$, and $x = a_i \cdot b_i x \cdot (a_i b_i)^{-1} \in a_iA_\mathfrak{m}$. So $I_\mathfrak{m} = a_i A_\mathfrak{m}$.

Conversely, let $I = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and suppose $I_\mathfrak{m} = aA_\mathfrak{m}$ for each $\mathfrak{m}$. First, $(A : I)_\mathfrak{m} = (A_\mathfrak{m} : I_\mathfrak{m})$. The inclusion $\subseteq$ is clear. If $xI_\mathfrak{m} \subseteq A_\mathfrak{m}$, then $xa_i = c_i/s_i$ with $c_i \in A$ and $s_i \notin \mathfrak{m}$, and $s = s_1 \cdots s_n$ satisfies $sxI \subseteq A$, so $x = sx/s \in (A : I)_\mathfrak{m}$. Hence $(I(A : I))_\mathfrak{m} = aA_\mathfrak{m} \cdot a^{-1}A_\mathfrak{m} = A_\mathfrak{m}$. The ideal $I(A : I) \subseteq A$ is therefore contained in no maximal ideal, so it is $A$. $\square$

So an invertible fractional ideal is the algebraic form of a line bundle: locally it is generated by one element, a local equation. The isomorphism `ClassGroup.equivPic` in Mathlib says that every finitely generated projective module of rank one over a domain is isomorphic to an invertible fractional ideal, and Eisenbud, Chapter 11, proves the same statement.

From now on $A$ is a Noetherian normal domain, with the valuations $v_\mathfrak{p}$ and the divisors of Section 8.2. For a Weil divisor $D = \sum n_\mathfrak{q}[\mathfrak{q}]$ and a prime $P$ of $A$, write $D_P = \sum_{\mathfrak{q} \subseteq P} n_\mathfrak{q} [\mathfrak{q}A_P]$ for its image in $\operatorname{Div}(A_P)$, the restriction of $D$ to $\operatorname{Spec} A_P$.

**Definition P1.8.19 (Cartier and $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisors).** A Weil divisor $D$ is *Cartier at* a prime $P$ if $D_P = \operatorname{div}(f)$ in $\operatorname{Div}(A_P)$ for some $f \in K^*$, a *local equation* of $D$ at $P$. It is *Cartier* if it is Cartier at every maximal ideal. It is *$\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier at* $P$ if $rD$ is Cartier at $P$ for some integer $r \geq 1$, and the least such $r$ is the *index* of $D$ at $P$. The ring $A$ is *$\mathbb{Q}$-factorial at* $P$ if every Weil divisor is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier at $P$, and *$\mathbb{Q}$-factorial* if it is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial at every maximal ideal. It is *locally factorial* if $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is factorial for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$.

Lean: not in Mathlib.

Since $D \mapsto D_P$ maps principal divisors to principal divisors (proof of Nagata's sequence in Section 8.2), $D$ is Cartier at $P$ exactly when its image in $\operatorname{Cl}(A_P)$ vanishes. A divisor Cartier at a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ is Cartier at every prime $P \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$, since $A_P$ is a localization of $A_\mathfrak{m}$. Principal divisors are Cartier, so the classes of Cartier divisors form a subgroup of $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$.

For an invertible fractional ideal $I$ and a prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of height one, $I_\mathfrak{p}$ is a nonzero fractional ideal of the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$, so $I_\mathfrak{p} = t^n A_\mathfrak{p}$ for a unique $n = v_\mathfrak{p}(I) \in \mathbb{Z}$. If $I = (a_1, \dots, a_r)$, then $v_\mathfrak{p}(I) = \min_i v_\mathfrak{p}(a_i)$, which is $0$ for all but finitely many $\mathfrak{p}$. Put $\operatorname{div}(I) = \sum v_\mathfrak{p}(I)[\mathfrak{p}]$. Then $\operatorname{div}(fA) = \operatorname{div}(f)$ and $\operatorname{div}(IJ) = \operatorname{div}(I) + \operatorname{div}(J)$, since $(IJ)_\mathfrak{p} = I_\mathfrak{p}J_\mathfrak{p}$.

**Theorem P1.8.20 (Picard group and class group).** Let $A$ be a Noetherian normal domain. The map $[I] \mapsto [\operatorname{div}(I)]$ is an injective homomorphism $\operatorname{Pic}(A) \to \operatorname{Cl}(A)$. Its image is the subgroup of classes of Cartier divisors. If $A$ is locally factorial, it is an isomorphism $\operatorname{Pic}(A) \cong \operatorname{Cl}(A)$.

Lean: not in Mathlib. Mathlib has `ClassGroup.equivPic` for the left side; the Weil class group is not defined.

*Proof.* The map is a well defined homomorphism by the remarks before the theorem. For injectivity, let $\operatorname{div}(I) = \operatorname{div}(f)$. Replacing $I$ by $f^{-1}I$, assume $\operatorname{div}(I) = 0$. Then $I_\mathfrak{p} = A_\mathfrak{p}$ for every $\mathfrak{p}$ of height one, so $I \subseteq \bigcap A_\mathfrak{p} = A$ by Krull's theorem. The inverse $J = (A : I)$ has $\operatorname{div}(J) = 0$ as well, so $J \subseteq A$. Then $A = IJ \subseteq I \subseteq A$, so $I = A$.

The image consists of Cartier classes. Let $I$ be invertible and $\mathfrak{m}$ maximal, with $I_\mathfrak{m} = aA_\mathfrak{m}$. For $\mathfrak{q} \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$ of height one, $I_\mathfrak{q} = aA_\mathfrak{q}$, so $v_\mathfrak{q}(I) = v_\mathfrak{q}(a)$, and $a$ is a local equation of $\operatorname{div}(I)$ at $\mathfrak{m}$.

Every Cartier class is in the image. Let $D = \sum n_\mathfrak{q}[\mathfrak{q}]$ be Cartier, and put
$$
I = \{g \in K^* : \operatorname{div}(g) \geq D\} \cup \{0\} ,
$$
an $A$-submodule of $K$ since each $v_\mathfrak{q}$ satisfies the rule for sums. Let $\mathfrak{q}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{q}_s$ be the primes with $n_\mathfrak{q} \neq 0$. The product of the $\mathfrak{q}_i^{(|n_{\mathfrak{q}_i}|)}$ is a nonzero ideal, and every nonzero $d$ in it satisfies $\operatorname{div}(d) \geq D$ and $\operatorname{div}(d) \geq -D$. So $d \in I$, and $dI \subseteq A$ by the algebraic Hartogs theorem. Hence $I$ is a fractional ideal, finitely generated since $dI$ is an ideal of a Noetherian ring. Now fix a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ and a local equation $f$ of $D$ at $\mathfrak{m}$. Then $I_\mathfrak{m} \subseteq fA_\mathfrak{m}$: for $g \in I$, $v_\mathfrak{q}(g/f) \geq 0$ for all $\mathfrak{q} \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$, so $g/f \in A_\mathfrak{m}$ by the algebraic Hartogs theorem for $A_\mathfrak{m}$. Conversely, the finitely many primes $\mathfrak{q}$ of height one with $v_\mathfrak{q}(f) < n_\mathfrak{q}$ are not contained in $\mathfrak{m}$, and neither is the product of their symbolic powers $\mathfrak{q}^{(N)}$, for any $N$. Choose $N$ large and $s \notin \mathfrak{m}$ in this product. Then $\operatorname{div}(sf) \geq D$, so $sf \in I$ and $f \in I_\mathfrak{m}$. Hence $I_\mathfrak{m} = fA_\mathfrak{m}$. By the proposition, $I$ is invertible, and $v_\mathfrak{q}(I) = v_\mathfrak{q}(f) = n_\mathfrak{q}$ for $\mathfrak{q} \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$. Every $\mathfrak{q}$ lies in some maximal ideal, so $\operatorname{div}(I) = D$.

If every $A_\mathfrak{m}$ is factorial, then $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m}) = 0$ by the proposition on factorial rings in Section 8.2. So every Weil divisor is Cartier, and the map is onto. $\square$

**Corollary P1.8.21 (local class groups).** Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be a maximal ideal of $A$. Then $A$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial at $\mathfrak{m}$ if and only if $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m})$ is a torsion group, and the index of a divisor $D$ at $\mathfrak{m}$ is the order of its image in $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m})$. If $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$ is torsion, then $A$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial.

Lean: not in Mathlib.

*Proof.* The map $\operatorname{Div}(A) \to \operatorname{Div}(A_\mathfrak{m})$ is onto, so the first two claims are the remark after the definition. Nagata's sequence makes $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m})$ a quotient of $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$, which gives the last claim. $\square$

The index is bounded on all of $\operatorname{Spec} A$. If $D_\mathfrak{m} = \operatorname{div}(f)$, then $D - \operatorname{div}(f)$ involves finitely many primes $\mathfrak{q}$, none inside $\mathfrak{m}$, and $D$ is Cartier at every prime outside the closed set $\bigcup V(\mathfrak{q})$. So the set where $rD$ is Cartier is open, and since $\operatorname{Spec} A$ is quasi-compact, a single $r \geq 1$ makes $rD$ Cartier on a $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial ring. This is the form of $\mathbb{Q}$-factoriality used in the minimal model program: every Weil divisor has a multiple that is Cartier.

### Proof in words

A Cartier divisor is a Weil divisor that has a local equation near every point, and an invertible ideal is the ideal sheaf that these local equations generate. A divisor and its ideal determine each other through the valuations, so the Picard group sits inside the class group, and the two agree when every local ring is factorial. In the minimal model program this happens on smooth varieties, since a regular local ring is factorial, a theorem of Auslander and Buchsbaum discussed in Chapter 9.

At a singular point the local class group $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m})$ measures the Weil divisors through the point that have no local equation. When it is finite, a multiple of each divisor has one, and intersection numbers with curves can still be defined with rational coefficients. This is why the minimal model program works with $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial varieties. The two cones of Section 8.2 show the two possibilities.

### Examples

**Example P1.8.22 (the quadric cone is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial).** Let $A = k[x, y, z]/(xy - z^2)$, $\mathfrak{p} = (x, z)$ and $\mathfrak{m} = (x, y, z)$. By Section 8.2, $\operatorname{Cl}(A) = \mathbb{Z}/2$, generated by $[\mathfrak{p}]$. By Example P1.5.24, $\dim_k \mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{p} = 2$, and $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$ is the same vector space, so $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}$ is not principal by Corollary P1.1.26. If $[\mathfrak{p}]$ were Cartier at $\mathfrak{m}$, with $[\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m}] = \operatorname{div}(f)$, then $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m} = fA_\mathfrak{m}$ by the proposition on factorial rings in Section 8.2, applied to $A_\mathfrak{m}$. So $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m}) = \mathbb{Z}/2$: the line $V(\mathfrak{p})$ is not Cartier at the vertex, and $2[\mathfrak{p}] = \operatorname{div}(x)$ is Cartier. The ring $A$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, every Weil divisor has index $1$ or $2$ at the vertex, and $\operatorname{Pic}(A) = 0$, since the only Cartier class is $0$.

**Example P1.8.23 (the cone over a quadric surface is not $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial).** Let $A = k[x, y, z, w]/(xw - yz) \cong k[su, sv, tu, tv] \subseteq B = k[s, t, u, v]$ as in Section 8.2, with $\mathfrak{p} = (x, y)$ and $\mathfrak{m} = (x, y, z, w)$. Then no multiple $n[\mathfrak{p}]$ with $n \neq 0$ is Cartier at $\mathfrak{m}$, and $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

First, $\mathfrak{p} = sB \cap A$. An element of $A$ lies in $sB$ exactly when each of its monomials does, and a monomial $s^a t^b u^c v^d$ of $A$ with $a \geq 1$ has $c + d = a + b \geq 1$, so it is $su$ or $sv$ times a monomial of $A$. Let $R$ be the valuation ring of the restriction to $K$ of the $s$-adic valuation $v_s$ of $\operatorname{Frac} B$. Elements of $A \setminus \mathfrak{p}$ are not divisible by $s$, so they are units of $R$, and $A_\mathfrak{p} \subseteq R$. Also $R \neq K$, since $v_s(x) = 1$. By Proposition P1.7.2, part (4), $R$ is a localization of the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$ at a nonzero prime, so $R = A_\mathfrak{p}$, and $v_\mathfrak{p} = v_s$ on $K$, since both take the value $1$ on $x$. Hence, by the proposition on symbolic powers in Section 8.1,
$$
\mathfrak{p}^{(n)} = s^nB \cap A = (x, y)^n ,
$$
where the last equality holds because a monomial of $A$ with $a \geq n$ has $c + d \geq n$, so it is a product of $n$ factors $su$ or $sv$ and a monomial of $A$.

Grade $A$ by the total degree in $B$, so that $x, y, z, w$ have degree $2$ and $\mathfrak{m}$ is spanned by the elements of positive degree. The ideal $\mathfrak{p}^{(n)} = (x, y)^n$ is generated by the $n + 1$ monomials $x^i y^{n-i} = s^n u^i v^{n-i}$ of degree $2n$, while $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{p}^{(n)}$ lies in degrees at least $2n + 2$. So $\dim_k \mathfrak{p}^{(n)}/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{p}^{(n)} = n + 1 \geq 2$, and $\mathfrak{p}^{(n)}A_\mathfrak{m}$ is not principal. Now suppose $n \geq 1$ and $n[\mathfrak{p}]$ is Cartier at $\mathfrak{m}$, with local equation $f$. Then $f \in A_\mathfrak{m}$, and the proposition on principal ideals in Section 8.1, applied to $A_\mathfrak{m}$, gives $fA_\mathfrak{m} = (\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{m})^{(n)} = \mathfrak{p}^{(n)}A_\mathfrak{m}$, a contradiction. Here the two symbolic powers agree, since both consist of the $y \in A_\mathfrak{m}$ with $v_\mathfrak{p}(y) \geq n$, and $v_\mathfrak{p}(a/h) = v_\mathfrak{p}(a)$ for $h \notin \mathfrak{m}$. For $n \leq -1$ apply this to $-n[\mathfrak{p}]$. So $[\mathfrak{p}]$ has infinite order in $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m})$, which is a quotient of $\operatorname{Cl}(A) = \mathbb{Z}[\mathfrak{p}]$, and $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$.

So the vertex is not $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, although $\mathfrak{p}$ is invertible away from the vertex (Exercise 5). The blow-up of $\mathfrak{p}$ replaces the vertex by a line and is an isomorphism elsewhere: it is a small resolution. The blow-up of $\mathfrak{p}'$ is the other small resolution, and the birational map between them is the Atiyah flop of Volume 8. A small contraction of an extremal ray in Volume 7 produces singularities of this kind, which is why the minimal model program performs a flip and does not stop at the target of the contraction.

**Example P1.8.24 (smooth varieties and Dedekind domains).** If $A$ is locally factorial, then $\operatorname{Pic}(A) = \operatorname{Cl}(A)$ and every Weil divisor is Cartier. This holds for $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, where both groups vanish, for every Dedekind domain, where all local rings are DVRs, and for the coordinate ring of every smooth affine variety, by the theorem of Auslander and Buchsbaum. The affine elliptic curve of Section 7.3 has a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ that is invertible but not principal, so $\operatorname{Pic}$ of a smooth curve need not vanish.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that the invertible fractional ideals form a group with identity $A$, inverse $I^{-1} = (A : I)$, and the principal fractional ideals as a subgroup.
2. (A) Show that an invertible fractional ideal of a local domain is principal, so $\operatorname{Pic}$ of a local domain is $0$. Show that $\operatorname{Pic}(A) = 0$ for a Noetherian factorial domain $A$.
3. (A) Let $A$ be a Dedekind domain. Show that every fractional ideal is invertible and that $\operatorname{Pic}(A) \to \operatorname{Cl}(A)$ is the isomorphism of the Dedekind example in Section 8.2.
4. (B) On the quadric cone, with $\mathfrak{p} = (x, z)$ and $\mathfrak{m} = (x, y, z)$, show that $(A : \mathfrak{p}) = x^{-1}\mathfrak{p}$ and $\mathfrak{p}(A : \mathfrak{p}) = \mathfrak{m}$. Deduce again that $\mathfrak{p}$ is not invertible. (Hint: $g \in (A : \mathfrak{p})$ if and only if $\operatorname{div}(g) \geq -[\mathfrak{p}]$, and Exercise 4 of Section 8.1 gives $\mathfrak{p}^2 = x\mathfrak{m}$.)
5. (B) On $A = k[x, y, z, w]/(xw - yz)$ with $\mathfrak{p} = (x, y)$, show that $\mathfrak{p}A_z = xA_z$ and $\mathfrak{p}A_w = yA_w$. Deduce that $\mathfrak{p}_P$ is principal for every prime $P \neq \mathfrak{m}$, so that $[\mathfrak{p}]$ is Cartier on $\operatorname{Spec} A \setminus \{\mathfrak{m}\}$.
6. (B) Let $n \geq 2$ and let $A = k[u^n, u^{n-1}v, \dots, v^n] \subseteq k[u, v]$, the cone over the rational normal curve of degree $n$. Show that $A$ is normal, that $x = u^n$ lies in exactly one prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of height one, that $v_\mathfrak{p}(x) = n$, and that $A_x = k[u^n, u^{-n}, v/u]$. Deduce that $\operatorname{Cl}(A) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n$ and that $A$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial. (Hint: $v^n$ is a unit in $A_\mathfrak{p}$ and $u^{n-i}v^i = (u/v)^{n-i} v^n$.)
7. (B) Let $A = k[x, y, z]/(xy - z^n)$ and $\mathfrak{m} = (x, y, z)$. Using Exercise 8 of Section 8.2, show that $\mathfrak{p}^{(j)} = (x, z^j)$ needs two generators at $\mathfrak{m}$ for $1 \leq j \leq n - 1$. Deduce that $\operatorname{Cl}(A_\mathfrak{m}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n$ and that the index of $[\mathfrak{p}]$ at the vertex is $n$.
8. (C) For a Weil divisor $D$ put $A(D) = \{g \in K^* : \operatorname{div}(g) + D \geq 0\} \cup \{0\}$. Show that $A(D)$ is a finitely generated fractional ideal, that $A(D) \cong A(D')$ as $A$-modules if and only if $D$ and $D'$ are linearly equivalent, and that $D$ is Cartier if and only if $A(D)$ is invertible. (Hint: an $A$-linear map between fractional ideals is multiplication by an element of $K$.)

### Lean

Mathlib defines the Picard group of a commutative ring, `CommRing.Pic`, as the group of isomorphism classes of invertible modules, with `Module.Invertible` for the condition that $M^\vee \otimes M \to A$ is an isomorphism. It proves that invertible modules are finite and projective, and for a domain it identifies `ClassGroup`, the invertible fractional ideals modulo principal ones, with the Picard group by `ClassGroup.equivPic`. The Weil class group, Cartier divisors, the map $\operatorname{Pic} \to \operatorname{Cl}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$-factoriality are not in Mathlib. The handbook adds no `MinimalModel` node here.

### Notes and history

Cartier introduced the divisors named after him in the 1950s, as sections of the sheaf $\mathcal{K}^*/\mathcal{O}^*$, to work on singular varieties where Weil divisors and line bundles differ. The comparison of the Picard group and the class group for Noetherian normal domains is in Eisenbud, Chapter 11. The condition of $\mathbb{Q}$-factoriality became standard in the minimal model program in the 1980s, in the work of Mori, Kawamata, Reid and Shokurov, and the cone over a quadric surface with its two small resolutions goes back to Atiyah's paper of 1958 on analytic surfaces. The literature state of every result here is theorem. The section follows Eisenbud, Chapter 11, and Matsumura, Chapter 4.
