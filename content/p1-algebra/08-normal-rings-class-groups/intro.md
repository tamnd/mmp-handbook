# Normal rings and class groups

This chapter builds the theory of Weil divisors on a normal variety from commutative algebra. Section 8.1 proves Krull's theorem for a Noetherian normal domain $A$: the associated primes of a principal ideal have height one, every nonzero element lies in finitely many height one primes, and $A$ is the intersection of the discrete valuation rings $A_\mathfrak{p}$ over the primes $\mathfrak{p}$ of height one. Section 8.2 defines the group of Weil divisors, the divisor of a rational function and the class group, proves that the class group vanishes exactly for unique factorization domains, proves Nagata's localization sequence and $\operatorname{Cl}(A[x]) \cong \operatorname{Cl}(A)$, and computes the class groups of the cones $xy = z^n$ and $xw = yz$. Section 8.3 compares Weil divisors with Cartier divisors: the Picard group of invertible modules embeds in the class group, with equality for locally factorial rings, and the quotient measures how far a variety is from being $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial.

For a normal variety $X$, a height one prime of an affine chart is a prime divisor $D$, and the valuation of the discrete valuation ring $\mathcal{O}_{X, D}$ is the order of vanishing $\operatorname{ord}_D$ of Section 7.2. Krull's theorem says that a rational function without poles along any prime divisor is regular, the algebraic form of Hartogs' theorem. It also says that $X$ is smooth in codimension one, since the local ring at the generic point of each prime divisor is a discrete valuation ring, as the introduction to Chapter 4 promised for the normalization. The class group is the group of Weil divisors modulo linear equivalence, and for a smooth variety it is the Picard group of line bundles.

In the minimal model program the canonical divisor $K_X$ of a normal variety is a Weil divisor, defined by its order along each prime divisor, and in general it is not Cartier. The discrepancies of Volume 4 make sense when some multiple $rK_X$ is Cartier, and the least such $r$ is the index. A variety is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial when every Weil divisor has a Cartier multiple, which is the condition that the quotient of the class group by the Picard group is torsion at every point. The quadric cone $xy = z^2$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, since its class group is $\mathbb{Z}/2$ and twice every Weil divisor on it is Cartier, and the cone $xw = yz$ over a quadric surface is not $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial: its two families of planes give its two small resolutions, related by the Atiyah flop of Volume 8. The MMP runs in the category of $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial varieties because divisorial contractions and flips preserve this property, while the target of a small contraction of an extremal ray is not $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial.

The chapter follows Matsumura, Chapter 4, Eisenbud, Chapter 11, and Atiyah-Macdonald, Chapter 9 and its exercises. It uses Chapters 1 to 7, in particular primary decomposition from Section 2.3, the principal ideal theorem of Section 5.2 and the discrete valuation rings of Section 7.2.
