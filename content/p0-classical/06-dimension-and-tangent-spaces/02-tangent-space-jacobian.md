## The tangent space and the Jacobian criterion

### Statement

Let $X$ be a variety and $P \in X$. The local ring $\mathcal{O}_{X,P}$ (Section 5.1) has the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_P$ of functions that vanish at $P$, and $\mathcal{O}_{X,P}/\mathfrak{m}_P = k$. The quotient $\mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2$ is a $k$-vector space of finite dimension, because $\mathfrak{m}_P$ is a finitely generated ideal.

**Definition P0.6.11.** The *Zariski cotangent space* of $X$ at $P$ is $\mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2$, and the *Zariski tangent space* is its dual $T_P X = (\mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2)^*$.

Lean: Mathlib `IsLocalRing.CotangentSpace` (the cotangent space $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ of a local ring, defined with `Ideal.Cotangent`).

**Example P0.6.12.** For $X = \mathbb{A}^n$ and $P = 0$, the ideal $\mathfrak{m}_0$ is generated by $x_1, \dots, x_n$, and $\mathfrak{m}_0/\mathfrak{m}_0^2$ has the basis $\bar{x}_1, \dots, \bar{x}_n$ (the theorem below). So $\dim T_0 \mathbb{A}^n = n$.

**Example P0.6.13.** For the cusp $C = V(y^2 - x^3)$ and $P = 0$, the classes of $x$ and $y$ span $\mathfrak{m}_0/\mathfrak{m}_0^2$, and the relation $y^2 = x^3$ lies in $\mathfrak{m}_0^2$, so it gives no relation between $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$. The theorem below shows $\dim T_0 C = 2$, although $\dim C = 1$.

P1 Ch. 9 proves that a Noetherian local ring $A$ with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ satisfies $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 \geq \dim A$, where $\dim A$ is the Krull dimension. For a variety, $\dim \mathcal{O}_{X,P} = \dim X$ (Hartshorne, Section I.3). So $\dim T_P X \geq \dim X$ at every point.

**Definition P0.6.14.** A point $P$ of a variety $X$ is *nonsingular* if $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2 = \dim X$, that is, if $\mathcal{O}_{X,P}$ is a regular local ring. Otherwise $P$ is *singular*. The set of singular points is $\operatorname{Sing} X$. The variety $X$ is *nonsingular* if $\operatorname{Sing} X = \emptyset$.

Lean: Mathlib `IsRegularLocalRing`.

**Example P0.6.15.** Every point of $\mathbb{A}^n$ is nonsingular, by the first example above and a translation. Every point of $\mathbb{P}^n$ is nonsingular, because it has an open neighbourhood isomorphic to $\mathbb{A}^n$.

**Example P0.6.16.** The origin is a singular point of the cusp, because $\dim T_0 C = 2 > 1$.

**Theorem P0.6.17 (Jacobian criterion).** Let $X \subset \mathbb{A}^n$ be an affine variety with $I(X) = (f_1, \dots, f_t)$, let $P = (a_1, \dots, a_n) \in X$, and let $J(P)$ be the $t \times n$ matrix $(\partial f_i/\partial x_j (P))$. Then

$$ \dim_k \mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2 = n - \operatorname{rank} J(P). $$

So $P$ is nonsingular if and only if $\operatorname{rank} J(P) = n - \dim X$.

Lean: not in Lean; Mathlib has `IsLocalRing.CotangentSpace` and `IsRegularLocalRing`, but not the comparison with the Jacobian matrix.

*Proof.* Let $R = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and $M = (x_1 - a_1, \dots, x_n - a_n)$, the maximal ideal of $P$. Define the $k$-linear map $\theta : R \to k^n$, $\theta(g) = (\partial g/\partial x_1 (P), \dots, \partial g/\partial x_n (P))$. Every $g \in M$ has a Taylor expansion at $P$,

$$ g = \sum_{j} c_j (x_j - a_j) + h, \quad h \in M^2, $$

and $\theta(g) = (c_1, \dots, c_n)$, since every element of $M^2$ has zero partial derivatives at $P$ by the product rule. So $\theta|_M : M \to k^n$ is surjective, with $\theta(x_j - a_j) = e_j$, and its kernel is $M^2$. This gives $M/M^2 \cong k^n$.

The ideal $I = I(X)$ lies in $M$, because $P \in X$. For $g = \sum_i h_i f_i \in I$, the product rule gives $\theta(g) = \sum_i h_i(P) \theta(f_i) + \sum_i f_i(P) \theta(h_i) = \sum_i h_i(P) \theta(f_i)$, because $f_i(P) = 0$. So $\theta(I)$ is the span of the rows $\theta(f_i)$ of $J(P)$, and $\dim \theta(I) = \operatorname{rank} J(P)$.

The maximal ideal of $P$ in $k[X] = R/I$ is $\mathfrak{m} = M/I$, and $\mathfrak{m}^2 = (M^2 + I)/I$. So

$$ \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 \cong M/(M^2 + I) \cong k^n/\theta(I), $$

where the second isomorphism is induced by $\theta$, since $\theta|_M$ has kernel $M^2$. This space has dimension $n - \operatorname{rank} J(P)$.

It remains to replace $k[X]$ by its localization $\mathcal{O}_{X,P} = k[X]_{\mathfrak{m}}$ (Section 5.1). Localization is exact, so $\mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2 = (\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2)_{\mathfrak{m}}$. Each $s \notin \mathfrak{m}$ acts on the $k[X]/\mathfrak{m}$-module $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ by the nonzero scalar $s(P)$, so it acts invertibly, and $(\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2)_{\mathfrak{m}} = \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$. $\square$

**Corollary P0.6.18 (the singular locus is closed).** Let $X \subset \mathbb{A}^n$ be an affine variety of dimension $r$ with $I(X) = (f_1, \dots, f_t)$. Then $\operatorname{Sing} X$ is the closed subset of $X$ where all the $(n - r) \times (n - r)$ minors of the Jacobian matrix vanish. For any variety, $\operatorname{Sing} X$ is closed.

Lean: not in Lean.

*Proof.* By the theorem and the inequality $\dim T_P X \geq r$, the rank of $J(P)$ is at most $n - r$ at every point of $X$, and $P$ is singular if and only if the rank is less than $n - r$. The rank is less than $n - r$ exactly when all the minors of size $n - r$ vanish at $P$, and the minors are polynomials. A general variety is covered by affine open sets, and nonsingularity at $P$ depends only on $\mathcal{O}_{X,P}$. $\square$

**Theorem P0.6.19 (nonsingular points are dense).** For every variety $X$, $\operatorname{Sing} X$ is a proper closed subset of $X$. So the nonsingular points form a nonempty open dense subset.

Lean: not in Lean.

*Proof.* First let $X = V(f) \subset \mathbb{A}^n$ with $f$ irreducible and nonconstant. Then $I(X) = (f)$ and $\dim X = n - 1$ (Section 6.1), so $\operatorname{Sing} X = V(f, \partial f/\partial x_1, \dots, \partial f/\partial x_n)$. Suppose that $\operatorname{Sing} X = X$. Then each $\partial f/\partial x_i$ vanishes on $X$, so it lies in $I(X) = (f)$. The polynomial $\partial f/\partial x_i$ has smaller degree than $f$, so $\partial f/\partial x_i = 0$ for all $i$. If $\operatorname{char} k = 0$, this makes $f$ constant, which is false. If $\operatorname{char} k = p > 0$, every monomial of $f$ has all its exponents divisible by $p$. So $f = \sum c_\alpha x^{p\alpha}$. Every $c_\alpha$ has a $p$-th root in $k$, so $f = (\sum c_\alpha^{1/p} x^\alpha)^p$, and $f$ is not irreducible. This is a contradiction. So $\operatorname{Sing} X \neq X$.

Now let $X$ be any variety of dimension $r$. The field $k$ is perfect, so $k(X)$ has a separating transcendence basis $t_1, \dots, t_r$, and by the primitive element theorem $k(X) = k(t_1, \dots, t_r, y)$ for one element $y$, separable over $k(t_1, \dots, t_r)$. Multiply the minimal polynomial of $y$ by an element of $k[t_1, \dots, t_r]$ to clear the denominators and remove the common factors of the coefficients. By the lemma of Gauss this gives an irreducible polynomial $f(t_1, \dots, t_r, y)$ in $r + 1$ variables. Then $H = V(f) \subset \mathbb{A}^{r+1}$ is a hypersurface with $k(H) \cong k(X)$. By Section 5.2, $X$ and $H$ have isomorphic nonempty open subsets $U \cong V$. The nonsingular points of $H$ form a nonempty open subset, so it meets $V$. Nonsingularity is a property of the local ring, so $U$, and hence $X$, has a nonsingular point. $\square$

### Proof in words

The cotangent space $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ is the space of linear parts of functions that vanish at $P$. For the ambient affine space, the linear part of $g$ is its differential at $P$, and it is computed by partial derivatives. The equations of $X$ remove the linear parts of the elements of $I(X)$, and these are spanned by the rows of the Jacobian matrix. The whole difficulty is that one must use generators of the ideal $I(X)$, not just equations that cut out $X$ as a set.

For the density of nonsingular points, the hypersurface case is a degree argument, and in characteristic $p$ it needs the irreducibility of $f$. The general case reduces to it, because every variety is birational to a hypersurface.

### Examples

**Example P0.6.20.** Let $\operatorname{char} k \neq 2$ and let $C = V(f)$ with $f = y^2 - x^2 - x^3$, the nodal cubic. Then $\partial f/\partial x = -2x - 3x^2$ and $\partial f/\partial y = 2y$. A singular point satisfies $y = 0$, then $x^2 (1 + x) = 0$, so $x = 0$ or $x = -1$. At $(-1, 0)$ the derivative $\partial f/\partial x = 2 - 3 = -1$ is not zero. So $\operatorname{Sing} C = \{(0, 0)\}$, and at the origin $J = (0, 0)$, so $\dim T_0 C = 2$.

**Example P0.6.21.** The Jacobian criterion fails if the equations do not generate $I(X)$. Let $X = V(y) \subset \mathbb{A}^2$, the $x$-axis, and use the equation $g = y^2$, which defines the same set. The matrix $(\partial g/\partial x, \partial g/\partial y) = (0, 2y)$ is zero at every point of $X$. The formula would give $\dim T_P X = 2$ everywhere, but $X \cong \mathbb{A}^1$ is nonsingular. The ideal $(y^2)$ is not $I(X) = (y)$, so the hypothesis of the theorem fails.

### Exercises

1. (A) Find $\operatorname{Sing} C$ for the cusp $C = V(y^2 - x^3)$ in every characteristic.
2. (A) Show that the affine twisted cubic $V(y - x^2, z - x^3) \subset \mathbb{A}^3$ is nonsingular.
3. (A) Let $X \subset \mathbb{A}^n$ be an affine variety and $P \in X$. Show that $T_P X$ is isomorphic to the linear subspace $\{v \in k^n : \theta(g) \cdot v = 0 \text{ for all } g \in I(X)\}$, with $\theta$ as in the proof.
4. (B) Let $p = \operatorname{char} k > 0$ and $f = x^p - y$. Show that $f$ is irreducible, that $\partial f/\partial x = 0$, and that $V(f)$ is nonsingular.
5. (B) Let $d \geq 1$ and $\operatorname{char} k \nmid d$. Show that the Fermat curve $V(x^d + y^d + z^d) \subset \mathbb{P}^2$ is nonsingular. Use the affine charts and the Jacobian criterion in each.

### Notes and history

The definition of nonsingularity by the local ring is due to Zariski (1947), who also proved that it agrees with the Jacobian definition over a perfect field. Hartshorne proves the criterion as Theorem I.5.1 and the density of nonsingular points in Section I.5. Over a non-perfect field the two notions differ: regular points need not be smooth. The literature state is theorem. Section 6.4 uses the criterion for hypersurfaces. P3 Ch. 7 extends smoothness to schemes. Resolution of singularities (P8 Ch. 4 and 5) replaces a variety by a nonsingular one, birational to it.
