## Algebraic sets and the Zariski topology

### Statement

**Definition P0.3.1.** The *affine space* of dimension $n$ over $k$ is the set $\mathbb{A}^n = k^n$ of $n$-tuples $a = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$. A polynomial $f \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a function $\mathbb{A}^n \to k$, $a \mapsto f(a)$.

Lean: not in Lean as a variety; Mathlib uses the set `σ → K` of points with coordinates indexed by `σ`.

**Definition P0.3.2.** For a set $S \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ the *zero set* of $S$ is $V(S) = \{a \in \mathbb{A}^n : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S\}$. A subset of $\mathbb{A}^n$ of the form $V(S)$ is an *algebraic set*. For a subset $X \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n$ the *ideal* of $X$ is $I(X) = \{f \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n] : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } a \in X\}$.

Lean: Mathlib `MvPolynomial.zeroLocus` and `MvPolynomial.vanishingIdeal`.

**Example P0.3.3.** A hypersurface is $V(f)$ for one nonconstant polynomial $f$. In $\mathbb{A}^2$ the set $V(y - x^2)$ is a parabola and $V(xy)$ is the union of the two coordinate axes.

**Example P0.3.4.** A point $a \in \mathbb{A}^n$ is the algebraic set $V(x_1 - a_1, \dots, x_n - a_n)$. A finite set is algebraic: it is the zero set of all products $f_1 f_2 \cdots f_r$ with $f_i$ vanishing at the $i$-th point. The set $\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{A}^1 = \mathbb{C}$ is not algebraic, because a nonzero polynomial in one variable has finitely many roots.

**Lemma P0.3.5.** Let $S \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and let $J$ be the ideal that $S$ generates. Then $V(S) = V(J)$, and there are finitely many $f_1, \dots, f_r \in S$ with $V(S) = V(f_1, \dots, f_r)$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated; Mathlib has `MvPolynomial.zeroLocus` for ideals only and the Hilbert basis theorem as the instance `MvPolynomial.isNoetherianRing`.

*Proof.* A polynomial $\sum g_i f_i$ with $f_i \in S$ vanishes where all $f_i$ vanish, so $V(S) \subseteq V(J)$. The other inclusion holds because $S \subseteq J$. By the Hilbert basis theorem (P1 Chapter 3) the ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is Noetherian, so $J$ is generated by finitely many elements. Each of them is a combination of finitely many elements of $S$, and these finitely many $f_1, \dots, f_r \in S$ generate $J$. $\square$

**Proposition P0.3.6.** The algebraic sets have these properties.

1. $V(0) = \mathbb{A}^n$ and $V(1) = \emptyset$.
2. If $S \subseteq T$ then $V(T) \subseteq V(S)$.
3. For ideals $J_1, J_2$, $V(J_1) \cup V(J_2) = V(J_1 J_2) = V(J_1 \cap J_2)$.
4. For any family of ideals $J_\lambda$, $\bigcap_\lambda V(J_\lambda) = V(\sum_\lambda J_\lambda)$.

So the algebraic sets are the closed sets of a topology on $\mathbb{A}^n$, the *Zariski topology*.

Lean: not in Lean for $\mathbb{A}^n$ as a set of points; Mathlib has the Zariski topology on `PrimeSpectrum`.

*Proof.* Items 1, 2 and 4 follow from the definitions. For item 3, $J_1 J_2 \subseteq J_1 \cap J_2 \subseteq J_i$ gives $V(J_i) \subseteq V(J_1 \cap J_2) \subseteq V(J_1 J_2)$. Suppose $a \in V(J_1 J_2)$ and $a \notin V(J_1)$. Then some $f \in J_1$ has $f(a) \neq 0$. For each $g \in J_2$, $fg \in J_1 J_2$, so $f(a) g(a) = 0$ and $g(a) = 0$. So $a \in V(J_2)$. $\square$

**Definition P0.3.7.** A subset $Y \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n$ has the *induced Zariski topology*: its closed sets are the sets $Y \cap Z$ with $Z$ algebraic. The *Zariski closure* $\overline{Y}$ is the smallest algebraic set that contains $Y$.

Lean: not in Lean.

**Example P0.3.8.** On $\mathbb{A}^1$ the closed sets are $\mathbb{A}^1$ and the finite sets, because a nonzero ideal of $k[x]$ is principal and a nonzero polynomial has finitely many roots. So two nonempty open sets of $\mathbb{A}^1$ always meet, and the topology is not Hausdorff.

**Example P0.3.9.** The Zariski topology on $\mathbb{A}^2$ is not the product topology of $\mathbb{A}^1 \times \mathbb{A}^1$. In the product topology the complement of a basic open set $U \times U'$ is a finite union of vertical and horizontal lines. So every closed set other than $\mathbb{A}^2$ is a finite union of vertical lines, horizontal lines and points. The diagonal $V(x - y)$ is closed in the Zariski topology of $\mathbb{A}^2$, and it is not of that form.

**Proposition P0.3.10.** For subsets $X, Y \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n$ and $S \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$:

1. $I(X)$ is a radical ideal, and $X \subseteq Y$ implies $I(Y) \subseteq I(X)$;
2. $S \subseteq I(V(S))$ and $X \subseteq V(I(X))$;
3. $V(I(X)) = \overline{X}$; in particular $V(I(X)) = X$ when $X$ is algebraic.

Lean: Mathlib `MvPolynomial.le_vanishingIdeal_zeroLocus` and `MvPolynomial.zeroLocus_vanishingIdeal_le` for item 2.

*Proof.* If $f^m$ vanishes on $X$ then $f$ vanishes on $X$, since $k$ has no nilpotents. The rest of 1 and 2 is direct. For 3, $V(I(X))$ is algebraic and contains $X$, so it contains $\overline{X}$. Write $\overline{X} = V(J)$. Every $f \in J$ vanishes on $X$, so $J \subseteq I(X)$ and $V(I(X)) \subseteq V(J) = \overline{X}$. $\square$

### Examples

The algebraic set $X = V(y^2 - x^3) \subset \mathbb{A}^2$ is the cuspidal cubic of Chapter 7. The map $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$ from $\mathbb{A}^1$ is a bijection onto $X$: a point $(a, b)$ of $X$ with $a \neq 0$ comes from $t = b / a$, and the only point of $X$ with $a = 0$ is the origin, which comes from $t = 0$. The ideal $I(X)$ is $(y^2 - x^3)$. To see this, divide $f \in I(X)$ by $y^2 - x^3$ as a polynomial in $y$: $f = q \cdot (y^2 - x^3) + g(x) y + h(x)$. Then $g(t^2) t^3 + h(t^2) = 0$ for all $t \in k$, so this is the zero polynomial in $t$. The first term has only odd powers of $t$ and the second only even powers, so $g = h = 0$ and $f \in (y^2 - x^3)$.

A failure of the hypothesis on $k$: over $\mathbb{R}$ the polynomial $x^2 + y^2 + 1$ has no zeros, so $V(x^2 + y^2 + 1) = \emptyset = V(1)$, but $x^2 + y^2 + 1$ generates a proper ideal. So over a field that is not algebraically closed the zero set does not see the ideal. Section 3.2 shows that over an algebraically closed field this cannot happen.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $V(xy, xz) \subset \mathbb{A}^3$ is the union of a plane and a line, and find them.
2. (A) Show that the set $\{(t, e^t) : t \in \mathbb{C}\}$ is not an algebraic subset of $\mathbb{A}^2_{\mathbb{C}}$. (Hint: intersect with a horizontal line.)
3. (A) Show that every proper closed subset of $\mathbb{A}^1$ is finite, and that every map $\mathbb{A}^1 \to \mathbb{A}^1$ that is injective is continuous for the Zariski topology.
4. (B) Show that $V(J_1) \cup V(J_2) = V(J_1 J_2)$ can fail for infinite unions: find algebraic sets $Z_1, Z_2, \dots$ in $\mathbb{A}^1$ whose union is not algebraic.
5. (B) Show that $I(V(x^2 + y^2)) = (x^2 + y^2)$ over $\mathbb{C}$, and that over $\mathbb{R}$ the set $V(x^2 + y^2)$ is one point with ideal $(x, y)$.

### Lean

Mathlib has the zero locus and the vanishing ideal for any field extension $k \subseteq K$ as `MvPolynomial.zeroLocus` and `MvPolynomial.vanishingIdeal`, with points in `σ → K`. It does not put the Zariski topology on this set of points. The scheme-theoretic Zariski topology lives on `PrimeSpectrum`, which P3 uses. The handbook does not state these classical sets in `MinimalModel`.

### Notes and history

Algebraic sets in affine space are the oldest objects of algebraic geometry. The Zariski topology is named after Oscar Zariski, who introduced it in the 1940s in his work on the foundations of algebraic geometry. Hilbert proved the basis theorem in 1890. The statements of this section are classical theorems. P3 Chapter 2 replaces the set of points by the prime spectrum.
