## The Nakai-Moishezon criterion

### Statement

Throughout, $X$ is a projective scheme over $k$, and a *subvariety* of $X$ is an integral closed subscheme. Ampleness is defined in Definition P3.6.22, and by Theorem P4.2.10 it is equivalent to the eventual vanishing of $H^1(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes L^m)$ for every coherent $\mathcal{F}$.

**Proposition P7.3.1 (reductions).** Let $L$ be a line bundle on $X$.

1. $L$ is ample if and only if $L|_{X_{\mathrm{red}}}$ is ample.
2. Let $X_1, \dots, X_s$ be the irreducible components of $X$ with their reduced structures. Then $L$ is ample if and only if every $L|_{X_i}$ is ample.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P7.3.2 (the Nakai-Moishezon criterion).** A line bundle $L$ on $X$ is ample if and only if
$$(L^{\dim V} \cdot V) > 0$$
for every subvariety $V \subseteq X$ of positive dimension.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Corollary P7.3.3 (finite morphisms).** Let $f \colon Y \to X$ be a finite morphism of projective schemes and $L$ a line bundle on $X$. If $L$ is ample, then $f^* L$ is ample. If $f$ is surjective and $f^* L$ is ample, then $L$ is ample.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Corollary P7.3.4 (ampleness is numerical).** Let $L$ and $L'$ be line bundles on $X$ with $L \equiv L'$. Then $L$ is ample if and only if $L'$ is ample.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

### Proof in words

The criterion is proved by induction on the dimension. The hypothesis passes to every subvariety, so by induction $L$ is ample on every proper closed subscheme, in particular on every effective divisor. Ampleness on the divisors controls the higher cohomology of $L^m$ on $X$ through the exact sequences of the divisors, and asymptotic Riemann-Roch with $(L^n) > 0$ then forces $h^0(L^m)$ to grow like $m^n$. So some multiple of $L$ is effective, $L^m = \mathcal{O}_X(D)$.

Next the exact sequence $0 \to \mathcal{O}_X((m-1)D) \to \mathcal{O}_X(mD) \to \mathcal{O}_D(mD) \to 0$ and the ampleness of $L|_D$ show that the dimensions $h^1(X, mD)$ stop decreasing, so sections on $D$ lift to $X$ for large $m$. Away from $D$ the section defining $mD$ generates. So $mD$ is generated by global sections and defines a morphism to projective space. A curve contracted by this morphism would have $L \cdot C = 0$, which the hypothesis excludes, so the morphism is finite, and a finite morphism pulls an ample bundle back to an ample bundle.

The two corollaries follow because intersection numbers behave well under finite morphisms, by the projection formula, and depend only on numerical classes, by Chapter 2.

### Examples

**Example P7.3.5 (surfaces).** On a projective surface $S$, the subvarieties of positive dimension are $S$ and the curves on $S$. So Theorem P7.3.2 says that $L$ is ample if and only if $(L^2) > 0$ and $(L \cdot C) > 0$ for every curve $C$. For a smooth projective surface this is Theorem P5.12.5. The condition $(L^2) > 0$ cannot be replaced by $(L^2) \geq 0$, and it cannot simply be dropped either. If $L \cdot C > 0$ for every curve, then $L$ is nef and $L^2 \geq 0$ by Lemma P5.12.4, so the only way the criterion can fail is $L^2 = 0$. Mumford's example, Example P7.3.24, shows that this happens.

**Example P7.3.6 (all dimensions are needed).** On $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ let $L = \mathcal{O}(-1, -1)$. Then $(L^2) = 2 > 0$ by Example P5.5.9, and $L$ is not ample, since $L \cdot \ell_1 = -1$ for the curve $\ell_1$ of Proposition P7.2.21. On $\tilde X = \operatorname{Bl}_p \mathbb{P}^n$ let $H$ be the pullback of $\mathcal{O}(1)$. Then $(H^n) = 1$ by part 2 of Proposition P7.1.23, and $(H^k \cdot V) > 0$ for every subvariety $V$ of dimension $k$ not contained in $E$, by Theorem P7.1.15, since $\pi$ maps $V$ birationally onto its image. But $H \cdot e = 0$ for a line $e \subseteq E$, so $H$ is not ample. The condition fails exactly on the subvarieties of $E$.

**Example P7.3.7 (a double cover of the plane).** Let $\varphi \colon \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^2$ be the morphism $([s : t], [u : v]) \mapsto [su : sv + tu : tv]$, which sends a pair of points of $\mathbb{P}^1$ to the binary quadratic form vanishing on them. The three sections $su$, $sv + tu$, $tv$ of $\mathcal{O}(1, 1)$ have no common zero: if $su = tv = 0$ and $s = 0$, then $t \neq 0$, so $v = 0$ and $u \neq 0$, and $sv + tu = tu \neq 0$; the case $u = 0$ is symmetric. So $\varphi$ is a morphism with $\varphi^* \mathcal{O}(1) = \mathcal{O}(1, 1)$ (Theorem P3.6.21). A binary quadratic form determines its roots, so the fibres of $\varphi$ have at most two points, and $\varphi$ is finite by Corollary P4.6.17. It is surjective, since every binary quadratic form over the algebraically closed field $k$ factors. By Corollary P7.3.3, $\mathcal{O}(1, 1)$ is ample, which agrees with Example P3.10.34. By Theorem P7.1.15, $(\mathcal{O}(1,1)^2) = \deg \varphi \cdot (\mathcal{O}(1)^2) = \deg \varphi$, and $(\mathcal{O}(1,1)^2) = 2$ by Example P5.5.9, so $\varphi$ has degree $2$.

### Proof

*Proposition P7.3.1.* If $L$ is ample, its restriction to every closed subscheme is ample by part 1 of Corollary P4.2.11. Conversely, part 1. Let $\mathcal{N}$ be the nilradical of $\mathcal{O}_X$, with $\mathcal{N}^e = 0$, and $i \colon X_{\mathrm{red}} \to X$ the inclusion. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be coherent on $X$. The filtration $\mathcal{F} \supseteq \mathcal{N} \mathcal{F} \supseteq \dots \supseteq \mathcal{N}^e \mathcal{F} = 0$ has subquotients $\mathcal{G}_j = \mathcal{N}^j \mathcal{F} / \mathcal{N}^{j+1} \mathcal{F}$ killed by $\mathcal{N}$, so $\mathcal{G}_j = i_* \mathcal{G}_j'$ for a coherent sheaf $\mathcal{G}_j'$ on $X_{\mathrm{red}}$. As $i$ is affine, Proposition P4.1.19 gives $H^1(X, \mathcal{G}_j \otimes L^m) = H^1(X_{\mathrm{red}}, \mathcal{G}_j' \otimes L|_{X_{\mathrm{red}}}^m)$, which vanishes for $m$ large by Theorem P4.2.10 on $X_{\mathrm{red}}$. The long exact sequences of $0 \to \mathcal{N}^{j+1} \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{N}^j \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{G}_j \to 0$, twisted by $L^m$, give by descending induction on $j$ that $H^1(X, \mathcal{N}^j \mathcal{F} \otimes L^m) = 0$ for $m$ large, and $j = 0$ is the vanishing for $\mathcal{F}$. So $L$ is ample by Theorem P4.2.10.

Part 2. By part 1, assume $X$ reduced, and argue by induction on $s$, the case $s = 1$ being empty. Let $\mathcal{I}$ be the ideal of $X_1$ and $\mathcal{J}$ the ideal of the reduced union $X' = X_2 \cup \dots \cup X_s$. Then $\mathcal{I} \mathcal{J} \subseteq \mathcal{I} \cap \mathcal{J}$, which is the ideal of functions vanishing on all of $X$, and is $0$ because $X$ is reduced. For coherent $\mathcal{F}$, the sequence $0 \to \mathcal{J} \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{F} / \mathcal{J} \mathcal{F} \to 0$ has its first term killed by $\mathcal{I}$ and its last term killed by $\mathcal{J}$. So they are pushforwards of coherent sheaves on $X_1$ and on $X'$, on which $L$ is ample, by hypothesis and by induction. As in part 1, their twists by $L^m$ have vanishing $H^1$ for $m$ large, and so does $\mathcal{F} \otimes L^m$. $\square$

*Theorem P7.3.2.* If $L$ is ample, then so is $L|_V$ for every subvariety $V$ (part 1 of Corollary P4.2.11), and $(L^{\dim V} \cdot V) > 0$ by part 2 of Proposition P7.1.14.

Conversely, assume the condition, and argue by induction on $n = \dim X$. If $n = 0$, $X$ is affine and every line bundle on it is ample (part 2 of Proposition P3.6.24). Let $n \geq 1$. The condition passes to every closed subscheme $Y \subseteq X$, since the subvarieties of $Y$ are subvarieties of $X$ and the intersection numbers agree by part 7 of Theorem P7.1.6. So $L|_Y$ is ample for every closed subscheme $Y$ of dimension less than $n$, by induction. By Proposition P7.3.1 it is enough to prove that $L|_{X_i}$ is ample for every irreducible component $X_i$ with its reduced structure; the components of dimension less than $n$ are handled by induction, so assume that $X$ is integral of dimension $n$. Every proper closed subscheme of $X$ then has dimension less than $n$.

Step 1: $L^m$ has a nonzero section for some $m \geq 1$. By part 2 of Lemma P7.1.1, $L \cong A \otimes B^{-1}$ with $A$ and $B$ very ample. By part 1 of Lemma P7.1.1, choose sections $\alpha \in H^0(A)$ and $\beta \in H^0(B)$ that do not vanish at the generic point, the only associated point of $\mathcal{O}_X$. Their zero schemes $Z_\alpha$ and $Z_\beta$ are proper closed subschemes, and Lemma P7.1.2 gives exact sequences
$$0 \to L^m \otimes B^{-1} \xrightarrow{\beta} L^m \to L^m|_{Z_\beta} \to 0, \qquad 0 \to L^{m+1} \otimes A^{-1} \xrightarrow{\alpha} L^{m+1} \to L^{m+1}|_{Z_\alpha} \to 0,$$
with $L^{m+1} \otimes A^{-1} \cong L^m \otimes B^{-1}$. As $L$ is ample on $Z_\alpha$ and $Z_\beta$, Theorem P4.2.10 gives $m_0$ with $H^i(L^m|_{Z_\alpha}) = H^i(L^m|_{Z_\beta}) = 0$ for $i \geq 1$ and $m \geq m_0$. The long exact sequences then give, for $i \geq 2$ and $m \geq m_0$,
$$H^i(X, L^m) \cong H^i(X, L^m \otimes B^{-1}) \cong H^i(X, L^{m+1}).$$
So $\sum_{i \geq 2} (-1)^i h^i(X, L^m)$ is a constant $c$ for $m \geq m_0$, and $h^0(X, L^m) \geq \chi(X, L^m) - c$. By part 2 of Theorem P7.1.17, $\chi(X, L^m) = \frac{(L^n)}{n!} m^n + O(m^{n-1})$, and $(L^n) > 0$ by hypothesis with $V = X$. So $h^0(X, L^m) > 0$ for $m$ large.

Step 2: some multiple of $L$ is generated by global sections. The condition holds for $L^m$, since $((L^m)^{\dim V} \cdot V) = m^{\dim V} (L^{\dim V} \cdot V)$ by part 4 of Theorem P7.1.6, and $L$ is ample if $L^m$ is (part 1 of Proposition P3.6.24). So replace $L$ by a multiple and let $0 \neq \sigma \in H^0(X, L)$. As $X$ is integral, $\sigma$ does not vanish at the generic point, and its zero scheme $D$ is a proper closed subscheme, on which $L$ is ample. For $m \geq 1$, Lemma P7.1.2 gives
$$0 \to L^{m-1} \xrightarrow{\sigma} L^m \to L^m|_D \to 0.$$
There is $m_1$ with $H^1(D, L^m|_D) = 0$ and $L^m|_D$ generated by global sections for $m \geq m_1$ (Definition P3.6.22 and Theorem P4.2.10). Then $H^1(X, L^{m-1}) \to H^1(X, L^m)$ is surjective for $m \geq m_1$. These are finite-dimensional, so $h^1(X, L^m)$ is nonincreasing for $m \geq m_1$, hence constant for $m \geq m_2$, and then the surjections are isomorphisms. The long exact sequence shows that $H^0(X, L^m) \to H^0(D, L^m|_D)$ is surjective for $m > m_2$. Let $x \in X$ be a point. If $x \notin D$, then $\sigma^m$ generates $L^m$ at $x$. If $x \in D$, a section of $L^m|_D$ that generates the fibre at $x$ lifts to a section of $L^m$ that generates the fibre $L^m(x)$, hence the stalk by Nakayama's lemma. So $L^m$ is generated by global sections for $m > m_2$.

Step 3: $L^m$ is ample. Choose $m$ as in Step 2 and generating sections $s_0, \dots, s_N$ of $L^m$, and let $\varphi \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^N$ be the morphism of Theorem P3.6.21, with $\varphi^* \mathcal{O}(1) \cong L^m$. Suppose that a fibre of $\varphi$ is infinite. It is a closed subscheme of $X$ of positive dimension, so it contains a curve $C$, and $\varphi(C)$ is a point. By Theorem P7.1.15, $m (L \cdot C) = (\varphi^* \mathcal{O}(1) \cdot C) = 0$, which contradicts the hypothesis. So the fibres of $\varphi$ are finite. The morphism $\varphi$ is projective, since $X$ is projective, so it is finite by Corollary P4.6.17. Then $L^m = \varphi^* \mathcal{O}(1)$ is ample by part 2 of Corollary P4.2.11 and Theorem P3.6.23, and $L$ is ample by part 1 of Proposition P3.6.24. $\square$

*Corollary P7.3.3.* The first statement is part 2 of Corollary P4.2.11. Assume that $f$ is surjective and $f^* L$ ample, and let $W \subseteq X$ be a subvariety of dimension $r \geq 1$. The closed subscheme $f^{-1}(W)$ maps onto $W$, and it has finitely many irreducible components, whose images are closed because $f$ is finite. These images cover the irreducible $W$, so one component $V$, with its reduced structure, maps onto $W$. As $f$ is finite, $\dim V = r$. By Theorem P7.1.15 and Theorem P7.3.2 on $Y$,
$$[K(V) : K(W)] \, (L^r \cdot W) = ((f^* L)^r \cdot V) > 0.$$
So $(L^r \cdot W) > 0$, and $L$ is ample by Theorem P7.3.2. $\square$

*Corollary P7.3.4.* By Proposition P7.2.2, $(L^r \cdot V) = (L'^r \cdot V)$ for every subvariety $V$ of dimension $r$. Apply Theorem P7.3.2 to both. $\square$

### Exercises

1. (A) Use Theorem P7.3.2 to show that $\mathcal{O}(a, b)$ on $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ is ample if and only if $a > 0$ and $b > 0$.
2. (A) Let $f \colon Y \to X$ be a surjective morphism of projective schemes that is not finite. Show that $f^* L$ is not ample for any line bundle $L$ on $X$.
3. (B) Let $X$ be a projective scheme and $L$ a line bundle such that $L|_{X_i}$ is ample for every irreducible component $X_i$. Show directly from Theorem P7.3.2 that $L$ is ample, without Proposition P7.3.1.
4. (B) Let $X$ be a projective variety and $\nu \colon \tilde X \to X$ a finite surjective morphism, for instance the normalization of a curve. Show that a line bundle $L$ is ample if and only if $\nu^* L$ is ample, and deduce that a line bundle on a projective curve is ample if and only if its degree on every irreducible component is positive.
5. (B) Let $X$ be a projective variety of dimension $n$ and $L$ a line bundle such that $L|_D$ is ample for some effective Cartier divisor $D$ with $\mathcal{O}_X(D) \cong L$. Show that $L$ is ample. (Steps 2 and 3 of the proof.)
6. (C) Let $X$ be a complete scheme over $k$, not necessarily projective. Explain which steps of the proof of Theorem P7.3.2 use projectivity, and how the existence of the morphism $\varphi$ in Step 3 makes $X$ projective a posteriori. Kleiman proves the criterion for complete schemes (Kle66).

### Lean

Mathlib has no intersection numbers of line bundles, so the criterion cannot be stated there. The project library defines ampleness of a Cartier divisor by affine nonvanishing loci of sections, `MinimalModel.IsAmpleOn` after EGA II 4.5.2, and an ample $\mathbb{R}$-divisor, `MinimalModel.IsAmple`, as one $\mathbb{R}$-linearly equivalent to a positive combination of ample Cartier divisors. It states Kleiman's criterion rather than the Nakai-Moishezon criterion (Section 3.2). The handbook adds no `MinimalModel` node here.

### Notes and history

Nakai (1963) proved the criterion for projective schemes, and Moishezon (1964) found it independently for surfaces and complex spaces. Kleiman (Kle66, Chapter III) extended it to complete schemes and gave the proof by asymptotic Riemann-Roch. The proof here is the one of Lazarsfeld, Theorem 1.2.23, and Corollary P7.3.3 is Lazarsfeld, Corollary 1.2.28. The literature state is theorem. Lazarsfeld also proves the criterion for $\mathbb{R}$-divisors, after Campana and Peternell, where the argument by sections is not available; this edition does not need that form.
