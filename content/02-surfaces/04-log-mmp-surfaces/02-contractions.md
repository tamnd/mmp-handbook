## Extremal contractions of surface pairs

### Statement

Theorem 2.4.4 finds the negative extremal rays of a surface pair. This section contracts them. The square of a generator decides the type, as for smooth surfaces in Theorem P5.12.15: a ray of negative square is spanned by one curve, which is contracted to a point; a ray of square zero gives a fibration over a curve; a ray of positive square occurs only for Picard number one. The new difficulty is the first case. On a smooth surface the contracted curve is a $(-1)$-curve and Castelnuovo's theorem contracts it. On a singular surface the curve can pass through singular points, its strict transform on the minimal resolution together with the exceptional curves over these points forms a configuration that Artin's criterion must contract, and the result must again be $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial and klt. The klt condition is what makes Artin's criterion apply.

The notation is that of Section 1: $(X, \Delta)$ is a surface pair, $f \colon Y \to X$ the minimal resolution, and $K_Y + \Delta_Y = f^*(K_X + \Delta)$.

**Lemma 2.4.8 (a rationality criterion).** Let $\Sigma_1, \dots, \Sigma_s$ be a connected negative definite configuration of integral curves on a smooth projective surface $Y$, and $\Theta = \sum_i \theta_i \Sigma_i$ with $0 \le \theta_i < 1$. Suppose $(K_Y + \Theta) \cdot \Sigma_j \le 0$ for every $j$. Then every $\Sigma_j$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^1$, and $p_a(Z) \le 0$ for every positive cycle $Z$ supported on $\Sigma_1 \cup \dots \cup \Sigma_s$. So the configuration is contractible to a rational singularity, by Theorem P5.8.16.

**Corollary 2.4.9 (klt surface singularities are rational).** Let $X$ be a normal projective surface and $\Delta$ a boundary such that $K_X + \Delta$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier and $(X, \Delta)$ is klt. Then every singular point of $X$ is a rational singularity, and $X$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial.

**Theorem 2.4.10 (the divisorial contraction).** Let $(X, \Delta)$ be a klt surface pair and $R$ a $(K_X + \Delta)$-negative extremal ray with $x^2 < 0$ for $x \in R \setminus \{0\}$. By Lemma 2.4.3, $R = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}[C]$ for an integral curve $C$ with $C^2 < 0$, the only integral curve with class in $R$. Then there is a birational morphism $\varphi \colon X \to X'$ onto a surface with the following properties.

1. $\varphi_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_{X'}$, $\varphi(C)$ is a point $p'$, and $\varphi$ restricts to an isomorphism $X \setminus C \to X' \setminus \{p'\}$. The curve $C$ is rational.
2. $X'$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, $p'$ is a rational singularity or a smooth point, $\varphi^* N^1(X') = C^\perp$, and $\rho(X') = \rho(X) - 1$.
3. With $\Delta' = \varphi_* \Delta$, $K_X + \Delta = \varphi^*(K_{X'} + \Delta') + aC$ with $a = (K_X + \Delta) \cdot C / C^2 > 0$.
4. For every prime divisor $F$ over $X$, $a(F, X', \Delta') = a(F, X, \Delta) + a \operatorname{mult}_F(h^*C)$, where $h \colon W \to X$ is any proper birational morphism from a normal surface on which $F$ is a divisor. So $a(F, X', \Delta') \ge a(F, X, \Delta)$, with strict inequality exactly when the centre of $F$ on $X$ lies on $C$. In particular $a(C, X', \Delta') = a(C, X, \Delta) + a$, and $(X', \Delta')$ is a klt surface pair.

**Theorem 2.4.11 (the contraction of a ray of square zero).** Let $(X, \Delta)$ be a surface pair, not necessarily klt, and $R = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} x$ a $(K_X + \Delta)$-negative extremal ray with $x^2 = 0$.

1. $\Delta \cdot x \ge 0$, $K_X \cdot x < 0$ and $\rho(X) = 2$.
2. There is a morphism $\varphi \colon X \to B$ onto a smooth projective curve with $\varphi_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_B$ such that an integral curve is contracted by $\varphi$ if and only if its class lies in $R$.
3. Every fibre of $\varphi$ is irreducible, and the integral curves with class in $R$ are exactly the reduced fibres $\varphi^{-1}(b)_{\mathrm{red}}$. A general fibre is a smooth rational curve contained in the smooth locus of $X$, with $-K_X \cdot F = 2$.

**Theorem 2.4.12 (the contraction theorem for klt surface pairs).** Let $(X, \Delta)$ be a klt surface pair and $R = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}x$ a $(K_X + \Delta)$-negative extremal ray. Then the contraction $\varphi_R \colon X \to Z$ of $R$ exists: a morphism onto a normal projective variety with $\varphi_{R*}\mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_Z$, such that an integral curve is contracted if and only if its class lies in $R$. Exactly one of the following holds.

1. ($x^2 < 0$.) $\varphi_R$ is the divisorial contraction of Theorem 2.4.10.
2. ($x^2 = 0$.) $\rho(X) = 2$, and $\varphi_R$ is the fibration over a curve of Theorem 2.4.11.
3. ($x^2 > 0$.) $\rho(X) = 1$, $-(K_X + \Delta)$ is ample, and $Z$ is a point.

In particular $\varphi_R$ is never small: if it is birational, its exceptional locus is the curve $C$, a divisor. So the minimal model program for surface pairs has no flips.

### Proof in words

For the divisorial contraction, the strict transform $\tilde C$ and the exceptional curves over the points of $C$ form a connected configuration $\Sigma$ on $Y$. The coefficients of $f^*C$ give a positive vector on which the intersection matrix is negative, so $\Sigma$ is negative definite. The crepant boundary $\Delta_Y$ has coefficients less than $1$ on $\Sigma$, because the pair is klt, and $K_Y + \Delta_Y$ is nonpositive on $\Sigma$, because it is trivial on the exceptional curves and negative on $\tilde C$. A minimal counterexample argument with the genus formula then shows that every cycle on $\Sigma$ has arithmetic genus at most $0$, so Artin's criterion contracts $\Sigma$ to a rational point of a projective surface $T$. This contraction does not yet live on $X$: it contracts $\Sigma$ but not the exceptional curves over the other singular points. Pushing an ample divisor of $T$ down to $X$ gives a $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor that is zero on $C$ and positive on every other curve, whose linear systems have only finitely many base points. The theorem of Zariski and Fujita makes it semiample, and its Stein factorization is the contraction. Rational surface singularities are $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, by Lipman, and discrepancies only go up, because $K_X + \Delta$ differs from the pullback of $K_{X'} + \Delta'$ by a positive multiple of $C$.

For a ray of square zero, the boundary cannot be negative on it, since that would force a curve of negative square. A multiple of the pullback of a generating curve moves on $Y$ by Riemann-Roch. Its moving part has square zero and is a pullback from $X$, so it is base point free and defines a fibration of $Y$ that factors through $X$. Extremality and the positivity of intersections of distinct curves that meet make every fibre irreducible, and a Riemann-Roch argument on $Y$ shows that every class orthogonal to the fibre is a multiple of the fibre, so $\rho(X) = 2$.

### Examples

**Example 2.4.13 (the $(-n)$-curve of $F_n$).** Let $n \ge 2$ and $(X, \Delta) = (F_n, bC_-)$ with $1 - \frac2n < b < 1$, as in Example 2.4.6. The pair is klt, since $X$ is smooth and $C_-$ is a smooth curve with coefficient less than $1$ (Theorem 4.2.14 with the identity as log resolution). The ray $R = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}[C_-]$ is negative with $C_-^2 = -n < 0$, so Theorem 2.4.10 applies. The contraction is the toric morphism $F_n \to \mathbb{P}(1,1,n)$ of Example 3.5.7, which contracts $C_-$ and nothing else: both morphisms have connected fibres and contract exactly $C_-$, so each factors through the other by Lemma P5.7.2, and they agree up to isomorphism. The point $p' = \varphi(C_-)$ is the singularity $\frac1n(1,1)$ of Example 4.3.7, whose minimal resolution has one exceptional curve $E$ with $E^2 = -n$. Here $K \cdot C_- = n - 2$, so
$$a = \frac{(K + bC_-) \cdot C_-}{C_-^2} = \frac{n - 2 - bn}{-n} = b - \frac{n-2}{n} > 0,$$
and $\Delta' = \varphi_*(bC_-) = 0$. Part 4 gives $a(C_-, X', 0) = a(C_-, X, bC_-) + a = -b + b - \frac{n-2}{n} = -\frac{n-2}{n}$, independent of $b$, as it must be. This is the discrepancy of Example 4.3.7, computed there from $K \cdot E = n - 2$ and $E^2 = -n$. For $n = 2$ the point is the $A_1$ singularity, with $a = 0$, and the condition on $b$ is $b > 0$: the $(-2)$-curve is $K$-trivial, and any positive coefficient makes it negative. For $n = 1$ the curve $C_-$ is a $(-1)$-curve, every $b \in [0, 1)$ makes it negative, the contraction is the blow-down $F_1 \to \mathbb{P}^2$, and $a(C_-, \mathbb{P}^2, 0) = 1$.

**Example 2.4.14 (blowing down a line arrangement).** Let $g \colon X = F_1 \to \mathbb{P}^2$ be the blow-up of a point $p$, with exceptional curve $E = C_-$, and $\Delta = \sum_i a_i g^{-1}_*L_i + eE$ for lines $L_i$ in $\mathbb{P}^2$ and $0 \le a_i, e < 1$, with the $L_i$ through $p$ and otherwise in general position, so that $\Delta$ has simple normal crossings and the pair is klt. Let $S_p = \sum_i a_i$. Then $g^{-1}_*L_i \cdot E = 1$, $K \cdot E = -1$ and $E^2 = -1$, so $(K + \Delta) \cdot E = S_p - e - 1$. The ray of $E$ is negative if and only if $S_p < 1 + e$, and then
$$a = \frac{S_p - e - 1}{-1} = 1 + e - S_p, \qquad a(E, \mathbb{P}^2, \Delta') = -e + a = 1 - S_p,$$
with $\Delta' = \sum_i a_i L_i$. This is the discrepancy $1 - S_p$ of $G_p$ found in Example 2.4.5, from the other side. The contraction increases the discrepancy of $E$ from $-e$ to $1 - S_p$, and the pair $(\mathbb{P}^2, \Delta')$ is klt, since $S_p < 1 + e < 2$.

**Example 2.4.15 (a double fibre).** Let $W_0 = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ with the first projection $\pi$ and a fibre $F_0$. Blow up a point of $F_0$, with exceptional curve $E_1$, and then the point where $E_1$ meets the strict transform of $F_0$, with exceptional curve $E_2$, to get $W$. On $W$ the strict transforms $F''$ of $F_0$ and $E_1'$ of $E_1$ are disjoint $(-2)$-curves, $E_2$ is a $(-1)$-curve meeting each of them once, and $\pi^*(\pi(F_0)) = F'' + E_1' + 2E_2$. The two $(-2)$-curves are contracted to two $A_1$ points by Proposition P5.8.17 and Theorem P5.8.16, giving $f \colon W \to X$, which is the minimal resolution of $X$, with $\rho(X) = \rho(W) - 2 = 2$ by part 1 of Lemma 2.4.2. Let $\Gamma = f(E_2)$. Then $f^*\Gamma = E_2 + \frac12(F'' + E_1')$, since $(E_2 + \frac12 F'' + \frac12 E_1') \cdot F'' = 1 - 1 = 0$ and the same for $E_1'$. So
$$\Gamma^2 = f^*\Gamma \cdot E_2 = -1 + \frac12 + \frac12 = 0, \qquad K_X \cdot \Gamma = f^*K_X \cdot E_2 = K_W \cdot E_2 = -1,$$
using $f^*K_X = K_W$ for $A_1$ points, by Example 4.3.7 with $n = 2$. The fibration $\pi$ contracts $F''$ and $E_1'$, so it factors through $X$ by Lemma P5.7.2, and the fibre of $X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ through the two singular points is $2\Gamma$. With $\Delta = 0$ the ray $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}[\Gamma]$ is $K_X$-negative of square zero, and Theorem 2.4.11 applies: its fibration has an irreducible fibre that is not reduced, and $-K_X \cdot \Gamma = 1$, half of $-K_X \cdot F = 2$ for a general fibre $F$.

### Proof

*Lemma 2.4.8.* For each $i$, $\Theta \cdot \Sigma_i \ge \theta_i \Sigma_i^2$, since the other components meet $\Sigma_i$ nonnegatively. By the genus formula of Definition P5.8.12,
$$2p_a(\Sigma_i) - 2 = \Sigma_i^2 + K_Y \cdot \Sigma_i \le \Sigma_i^2 - \Theta \cdot \Sigma_i \le (1 - \theta_i)\Sigma_i^2 < 0.$$
An integral curve has $p_a \ge 0$, so $p_a(\Sigma_i) = 0$ and $\Sigma_i \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ by Corollary P5.5.15. Then $K_Y \cdot \Sigma_i + \Sigma_i^2 = -2$.

Suppose some positive cycle has $p_a \ge 1$, and choose such a $Z = \sum_i z_i \Sigma_i$ with $\sum_i z_i$ minimal. By the first step $Z$ is not a single $\Sigma_j$. Let $j$ be an index with $z_j \ge 1$. Then $Z - \Sigma_j$ is a positive cycle, so $p_a(Z - \Sigma_j) \le 0$ by minimality, and part 3 of Proposition P5.8.13 gives
$$1 \le p_a(Z) = p_a(Z - \Sigma_j) + p_a(\Sigma_j) + (Z - \Sigma_j) \cdot \Sigma_j - 1 \le (Z - \Sigma_j) \cdot \Sigma_j - 1.$$
So $(Z - \Sigma_j) \cdot \Sigma_j \ge 2$ and $(K_Y + Z) \cdot \Sigma_j = -2 + (Z - \Sigma_j) \cdot \Sigma_j \ge 0$. With $(K_Y + \Theta) \cdot \Sigma_j \le 0$ this gives $(Z - \Theta) \cdot \Sigma_j \ge 0$ for every $j$ with $z_j \ge 1$. Write $Z - \Theta = A - B$, where $A = \sum_{z_i \ge 1}(z_i - \theta_i)\Sigma_i$ and $B = \sum_{z_i = 0}\theta_i \Sigma_i$. Since $\theta_i < 1 \le z_i$ on the support of $Z$, the divisor $A$ is effective and nonzero with support equal to that of $Z$, and $B \ge 0$ has no component in common with $A$. Then
$$A^2 = (Z - \Theta) \cdot A + B \cdot A = \sum_{z_j \ge 1}(z_j - \theta_j)(Z - \Theta) \cdot \Sigma_j + B \cdot A \ge 0,$$
which contradicts negative definiteness. So $p_a(Z) \le 0$ for every positive cycle, and parts 1 and 2 of Theorem P5.8.16 give the contraction to a rational singularity.

*Corollary 2.4.9.* Let $p \in X$ be a singular point, $E_1, \dots, E_s$ the exceptional curves of the minimal resolution over $p$, a connected negative definite configuration by Theorem P5.8.3, and $\Delta_Y = f^*(K_X + \Delta) - K_Y$. Its exceptional part is determined by its intersection numbers with the $E_j$, since their intersection matrix is negative definite, so $\Delta_Y = f^*\Delta - \sum_i a_i E_i$ with the numerical pullback of Definition 4.3.3 and the numerical discrepancies $a_i$. Here $f^*\Delta \ge 0$ and $a_i \le 0$ by part 2 of Proposition 4.3.5, so $\Delta_Y \ge 0$, as in part 7 of Lemma 2.4.2. Let $\theta_i$ be the coefficient of $E_i$ in $\Delta_Y$. Then $0 \le \theta_i < 1$, because $\theta_i = -a(E_i, X, \Delta)$ and the pair is klt. Put $\Theta = \sum_{i \le s} \theta_i E_i$. The divisor $\Delta_Y - \Theta$ is effective and has no component among $E_1, \dots, E_s$, so for $j \le s$,
$$(K_Y + \Theta) \cdot E_j = (K_Y + \Delta_Y) \cdot E_j - (\Delta_Y - \Theta) \cdot E_j = 0 - (\Delta_Y - \Theta) \cdot E_j \le 0,$$
using that $K_Y + \Delta_Y$ is a pullback. By Lemma 2.4.8, $p_a(Z) \le 0$ for every positive cycle supported over $p$. Artin's criterion (Art66, Theorem 3, the equivalence of Theorem P5.8.16) says that $p$ is a rational singularity. A rational surface singularity has a finite local class group (Lipman, *Rational singularities with applications to algebraic surfaces and unique factorization*, Publ. Math. IHES 36, 1969, Proposition 17.1; see also part 2 of Theorem 2.3.13), so for every Weil divisor $D$ some multiple of $D$ is Cartier near $p$. There are finitely many singular points, and a common multiple of these integers makes $D$ Cartier everywhere. So $X$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial.

*Theorem 2.4.10.* Write $f^*C = \tilde C + \sum_i c_i E_i$, and let $\Sigma$ be the configuration formed by $\tilde C$ and the exceptional curves over the points of $C$. These are exactly the curves of $f^{-1}(C)$.

*Step 1: $\Sigma$ is contractible to a rational singularity.* The fibre over each point of $C$ is connected and meets $\tilde C$, so $\Sigma$ is connected. By the proof of part 4 of Lemma 2.4.2, $c_i > 0$ for every exceptional curve $E_i$ of $\Sigma$. The vector $z$ of coefficients of $f^*C$ on the components of $\Sigma$ is therefore positive, and $f^*C \cdot \tilde C = C^2 < 0$, $f^*C \cdot E_i = 0$, so the intersection matrix $M$ of $\Sigma$ satisfies $Mz \le 0$ and $Mz \ne 0$. By part 3 of Lemma P5.8.1, $\Sigma$ is negative definite. Let $\Theta$ be the part of $\Delta_Y$ supported on $\Sigma$. By part 7 of Lemma 2.4.2 its coefficient on $\tilde C$ is the coefficient of $C$ in $\Delta$, which is less than $1$ because the pair is klt, and its coefficients on the $E_i$ are $-a(E_i, X, \Delta) < 1$. All coefficients are $\ge 0$ because $\Delta_Y \ge 0$. The divisor $\Delta_Y - \Theta$ is effective and has no component in $\Sigma$. So $(K_Y + \Theta) \cdot E_i \le (K_Y + \Delta_Y) \cdot E_i = 0$ for the $E_i$ in $\Sigma$, and $(K_Y + \Theta) \cdot \tilde C \le (K_Y + \Delta_Y) \cdot \tilde C = f^*(K_X + \Delta) \cdot \tilde C = (K_X + \Delta) \cdot C < 0$. By Lemma 2.4.8, $\tilde C \cong \mathbb{P}^1$, so $C$ is rational, and there is a birational morphism $g_0 \colon Y \to T$ onto a normal projective surface whose exceptional curves are exactly the curves of $\Sigma$ (Definition P5.8.10).

*Step 2: a divisor on $X$ that is zero exactly on $C$.* Let $A$ be a very ample divisor on $T$ and $B = g_0^*A$, a base point free Cartier divisor on $Y$ with $B \cdot \Gamma' = 0$ for the curves $\Gamma'$ of $\Sigma$ and $B \cdot \Gamma' > 0$ for every other integral curve. Let $D_X = f_*B$ and $N = f^*D_X - B$, an $f$-exceptional $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor with $N \cdot E_i = -B \cdot E_i \le 0$ for every $i$. By Corollary P5.8.4, $N \ge 0$. Over a point of $C$ all the exceptional curves lie in $\Sigma$, so the part of $N$ over that point is orthogonal to these curves, and it is $0$ by negative definiteness. So $N$ is supported on the exceptional curves over the finitely many singular points of $X$ outside $C$. Since $X$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, there is $m \ge 1$ with $L = mD_X$ Cartier, and then $f^*L = mB + mN$, with $mB$ and $mN$ Cartier. For an integral curve $\Gamma \ne C$ on $X$, the curve $\tilde \Gamma$ is not in $\Sigma$, so $L \cdot \Gamma = (mB + mN) \cdot \tilde \Gamma \ge mB \cdot \tilde\Gamma > 0$. Also $L \cdot C = mB \cdot \tilde C + mN \cdot \tilde C = 0$, since $\tilde C \in \Sigma$ and $\tilde C$ is disjoint from the support of $N$. Finally $L^2 = m^2 (B + N) \cdot B \ge m^2 B^2 = m^2 A^2 > 0$, using $(B + N) \cdot N = f^*D_X \cdot N = 0$ and $B \cdot N \ge 0$.

*Step 3: $L$ is semiample.* Let $k \ge 1$ and $x \in X \setminus f(\operatorname{Supp} N)$. Since $X$ is normal, $f_*\mathcal{O}_Y = \mathcal{O}_X$, so $H^0(X, kL) = H^0(Y, kmB + kmN)$. Let $\sigma$ be the section of $\mathcal{O}_Y(kmN)$ with divisor $kmN$. Choose $y \in f^{-1}(x)$; it does not lie on $\operatorname{Supp} N$, which maps to points other than $x$. Since $kmB$ is base point free, some $s \in H^0(Y, kmB)$ has $s(y) \ne 0$, and $s\sigma$ is a section of $f^*(kL)$ that does not vanish at $y$. So the corresponding section of $kL$ does not vanish at $x$. Hence the base locus of $|kL|$ lies in the finite set $f(\operatorname{Supp} N)$. By the theorem of Zariski and Fujita (Laz04, Remark 2.1.32), a line bundle whose base locus is finite is semiample. So $|k_0 L|$ is base point free for some $k_0 \ge 1$.

*Step 4: the contraction.* Let $X \to \mathbb{P}^M$ be the morphism of $|k_0 L|$ and $\varphi \colon X \to X'$ its Stein factorization (Theorem P4.6.15), so $\varphi_*\mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_{X'}$, $X'$ is a normal projective variety, and $k_0 L = \varphi^* A'$ for an ample $A'$ on $X'$. An integral curve $\Gamma$ is contracted by $\varphi$ if and only if $L \cdot \Gamma = 0$, so if and only if $\Gamma = C$. Since $L^2 > 0$, the image is a surface, so $\varphi$ is finite over a dense open subset of $X'$, and there $\varphi_*\mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_{X'}$ makes it an isomorphism. So $\varphi$ is birational. The fibre $\varphi^{-1}(\varphi(C))$ is connected and its curves are contracted, so it is $C$, and $\varphi$ is quasi-finite over $X' \setminus \{p'\}$, where $p' = \varphi(C)$. By Corollary P4.6.10 it is an isomorphism from $X \setminus C$ onto $X' \setminus \{p'\}$. This proves part 1.

*Step 5: $X'$.* The composite $\varphi \circ f \colon Y \to X'$ is a resolution, and its fibre over $p'$ is $f^{-1}(C)$, the support of $\Sigma$. By Step 1 and Artin's criterion (Art66, Theorem 3), $p'$ is a rational singularity or a smooth point. By Lipman's theorem, as in the proof of Corollary 2.4.9, a Weil divisor on $X'$ has a multiple that is Cartier near $p'$. Away from $p'$, $X'$ is isomorphic to $X \setminus C$, which is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial. So $X'$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial. For a $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor $D'$ on $X'$, $\varphi^*D' \cdot \Gamma = D' \cdot \varphi_*\Gamma$ by the projection formula, so $\varphi^*$ is well defined on $N^1$, injective because $\varphi_*\varphi^* = \mathrm{id}$, and its image lies in $C^\perp$. Conversely, if $D \cdot C = 0$, then $D - \varphi^*\varphi_*D$ is supported on $C$, say equal to $tC$, and $0 = tC^2$ gives $t = 0$. So $\varphi^*N^1(X') = C^\perp$, a hyperplane because the form is nondegenerate and $[C] \ne 0$, and $\rho(X') = \rho(X) - 1$. This proves part 2.

*Step 6: discrepancies.* Since $X'$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, $K_{X'} + \Delta'$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier. Choose $K_{X'} = \varphi_* K_X$. The $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor $K_X + \Delta - \varphi^*(K_{X'} + \Delta')$ has pushforward $0$, so it equals $aC$ for some $a \in \mathbb{Q}$. Intersecting with $C$ gives $(K_X + \Delta) \cdot C = aC^2$, and $a > 0$ since both numbers are negative. This is part 3. For $h \colon W \to X$ and a prime divisor $F$ on $W$, define $\Delta_W$ and $\Delta'_W$ by $K_W + \Delta_W = h^*(K_X + \Delta)$ and $K_W + \Delta'_W = (\varphi h)^*(K_{X'} + \Delta')$. Then $\Delta'_W = \Delta_W - a h^*C$, and with Definition 4.1.4, $a(F, X', \Delta') = a(F, X, \Delta) + a \operatorname{mult}_F(h^*C)$. The multiplicity is positive exactly when $h(F) \subseteq C$. For $F = C$, $a(C, X', \Delta') = -\operatorname{mult}_C\Delta + a$. Every divisor over $X'$ is a divisor over $X$, and the exceptional ones are $C$ and the divisors exceptional over $X$. Both have discrepancy greater than $-1$ over $X'$: the first since $\operatorname{mult}_C\Delta < 1$ and $a > 0$, the second since $(X, \Delta)$ is klt. The coefficients of $\Delta'$ are coefficients of $\Delta$, so lie in $[0, 1)$. So $(X', \Delta')$ is a klt surface pair.

*Theorem 2.4.11.* (1) If $\Delta \cdot x < 0$, part 1 of Theorem 2.4.4 gives a generator of $R$ of negative square, against $x^2 = 0$. So $\Delta \cdot x \ge 0$ and $K_X \cdot x < 0$. By Theorem 2.4.4, $R = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}[\Gamma]$ for an integral curve $\Gamma$, with $\Gamma^2 = 0$. The proof that $\rho(X) = 2$ is given at the end.

(2) Choose $N \ge 1$ such that $G = N f^*\Gamma$ is an integral effective divisor on $Y$, possible by part 6 of Lemma 2.4.2. Then $G^2 = 0$, and $K_Y \cdot G = N K_X \cdot \Gamma < 0$ by part 7 of Lemma 2.4.2. Let $H$ be ample on $X$. For large $n$, $(K_Y - nG) \cdot f^*H < 0$ while $f^*H$ is nef, so $h^0(K_Y - nG) = 0$, and Riemann-Roch (Theorem P5.5.13) gives
$$h^0(nG) \ge \chi(\mathcal{O}_Y) + \tfrac12 nG \cdot (nG - K_Y) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_Y) - \tfrac{n}{2} K_Y \cdot G,$$
which tends to infinity. Fix $n$ with $h^0(nG) \ge 2$ and write $|nG| = |M| + Z$ with fixed part $Z \ge 0$ and moving part $M$, so $|M|$ has no fixed component and $\dim |M| \ge 1$. Then $M$ is nef and $M^2 \ge 0$. An effective exceptional divisor has negative square unless it is $0$, so $M$ is not exceptional and $f_*M \ne 0$. Now $f_*M + f_*Z = nN\Gamma$, so $f_*M$ and $f_*Z$ lie in $R$ by extremality, and $(f_*M)^2 = 0$. Let $P = f^*f_*M - M$, an exceptional divisor with $P \cdot E_i = -M \cdot E_i \le 0$. By Corollary P5.8.4, $P \ge 0$. Then
$$0 = (f^*f_*M)^2 = f^*f_*M \cdot M = M^2 + P \cdot M,$$
with both terms $\ge 0$, so $M^2 = 0$ and $P \cdot M = 0$. Also $P^2 = (f^*f_*M - M) \cdot P = -M \cdot P = 0$, so $P = 0$ by negative definiteness, and $M = f^*f_*M$. Two members of $|M|$ without common components exist, because $|M|$ has no fixed component, and they meet in $M^2 = 0$ points, so they are disjoint and $|M|$ is base point free. Its morphism has image a curve, since $M \ne 0$ and $M^2 = 0$. Let $\psi \colon Y \to B$ be its Stein factorization, with $B$ a normal, hence smooth, projective curve, $\psi_*\mathcal{O}_Y = \mathcal{O}_B$, and $M \sim \psi^* A_B$ for an ample divisor $A_B$ on $B$. Each exceptional curve $E_i$ has $M \cdot E_i = 0$, so it is contracted by $\psi$. A fibre of $f$ is a point or a connected union of exceptional curves, so $\psi$ maps it to a point, and by Lemma P5.7.2, $\psi = \varphi \circ f$ for a unique morphism $\varphi \colon X \to B$. Then $\varphi_*\mathcal{O}_X = \varphi_* f_* \mathcal{O}_Y = \mathcal{O}_B$, and $\varphi^*A_B = f_*f^*\varphi^*A_B \sim f_*M$, a positive multiple of $[\Gamma]$. So an integral curve $\Gamma'$ is contracted by $\varphi$ if and only if $\Gamma' \cdot \Gamma = 0$.

(3) For a point $b \in B$, the fibre $\varphi^*b$ is numerically equivalent to $\varphi^*A_B / \deg A_B$, a positive multiple of $[\Gamma]$. By extremality the class of each component of $\varphi^*b$ lies in $R$. Classes in $R$ are multiples of $x$, and $x^2 = 0$, so any two components $\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2$ have $\Gamma_1 \cdot \Gamma_2 = 0$. The fibre is connected by Theorem P4.6.7. If it had two components, two of them would meet, and part 4 of Lemma 2.4.2 would give $\Gamma_1 \cdot \Gamma_2 > 0$. So every fibre is irreducible. An integral curve with class in $R$ has intersection $0$ with $\Gamma$, so it is contracted and is the reduced fibre containing it. Conversely the reduced fibres have classes in $R$, as shown. By generic smoothness (Har77, Corollary III.10.7), a general fibre $F$ of $\psi$ is smooth, and it is connected by Theorem P4.6.7, so it is a smooth integral curve with $F^2 = 0$. Since $M \equiv (\deg A_B) F$ and $K_Y \cdot M = K_Y \cdot f^*f_*M = K_X \cdot f_*M < 0$, also $K_Y \cdot F < 0$. The adjunction formula gives $2p_a(F) - 2 = K_Y \cdot F < 0$, so $F \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ and $K_Y \cdot F = -2$. The exceptional curves lie in finitely many fibres of $\psi$, so a general $F$ is disjoint from them, $f$ is an isomorphism near $F$, and $f(F)$ is a general fibre of $\varphi$, in the smooth locus, with $-K_X \cdot f(F) = 2$.

(1, end) Let $D$ be a Weil divisor on $X$ with $D \cdot \Gamma = 0$, and $L = f^*(dD)$ with $d$ as in part 6 of Lemma 2.4.2, an integral divisor on $Y$. Let $F$ be a general fibre of $\psi$. Then $L \cdot F = dD \cdot \varphi^*b = 0$ for $b = \psi(F)$, since $\varphi^*b$ is a multiple of $\Gamma$. For $k \ge 1$, Riemann-Roch gives
$$\chi(L + kF) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_Y) + \tfrac12 (L + kF) \cdot (L + kF - K_Y) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_Y) + \tfrac12 L \cdot (L - K_Y) + k,$$
using $F^2 = 0$, $L \cdot F = 0$ and $K_Y \cdot F = -2$. For large $k$, $(K_Y - L - kF) \cdot f^*H < 0$, so $h^2(L + kF) = h^0(K_Y - L - kF) = 0$ and $h^0(L + kF) \ge \chi(L + kF) > 0$. Let $D_k \sim L + kF$ be effective. Then $D_k \cdot F = 0$. A component of $D_k$ that is not contained in a fibre of $\psi$ maps onto $B$ and meets every fibre, so it has positive intersection with the nef divisor $F$. So every component of $D_k$ lies in a fibre of $\psi$, and $f_*D_k$ is a combination of fibres of $\varphi$, so a multiple of $[\Gamma]$. Since $f_*L = dD$ and $f_*F$ is a fibre of $\varphi$, the class of $D$ is a multiple of $[\Gamma]$. The classes of Weil divisors span $N^1(X)$, and $\Gamma^\perp$ is spanned by classes of Weil divisors, since $\Gamma$ is an integral class. So $\Gamma^\perp = \mathbb{R}[\Gamma]$. The form is nondegenerate and $[\Gamma] \ne 0$, so $\Gamma^\perp$ has dimension $\rho(X) - 1$, and $\rho(X) = 2$.

*Theorem 2.4.12.* The three cases are exclusive. If $x^2 < 0$, Theorem 2.4.10 gives the contraction, and $C$ is the only curve with class in $R$. If $x^2 = 0$, Theorem 2.4.11 gives it. If $x^2 > 0$, then $\rho(X) = 1$ by part 4 of Lemma 2.4.3, so $\overline{NE}(X) = R$, and $-(K_X + \Delta)$ is positive on $\overline{NE}(X) \setminus \{0\}$. Some multiple of $-(K_X + \Delta)$ is Cartier, so Kleiman's criterion (KM98, Theorem 1.18) shows that $-(K_X + \Delta)$ is ample, and the map to a point is the contraction. In the first case the morphism is birational with exceptional locus $C$, and in the other two it is not birational. So no contraction is small.

### Notes and history

The contraction theorem for log surfaces is due to Fujino, *Minimal model theory for log surfaces* (Publ. RIMS 48, 2012), and in every characteristic to Tanaka, *Minimal models and abundance for positive characteristic log surfaces* (Nagoya Math. J. 216, 2014). Tanaka's proof works without the Kawamata-Viehweg vanishing theorem, which fails in positive characteristic. The proof here contracts first on the minimal resolution by Artin's criterion and then descends to $X$ by the theorem of Zariski and Fujita. In higher dimension the contraction of a klt ray comes from the basepoint-free theorem (KM98, Theorem 3.3 and Theorem 3.7), which needs the vanishing theorem. Lemma 2.4.8 is the surface form of the fact that klt singularities are rational (KM98, Theorem 5.22), and Theorem 4.3.18 identifies klt surface germs as quotient singularities, from which rationality also follows. Exercise 3 shows why the klt condition matters: an lc pair can contain a curve on which $K + \Delta$ is trivial and whose contraction is not rational, as for the cone over a cubic in Example P5.8.21. Fujino shows by a different argument that the lc case still has contractions onto projective surfaces; Section 3 returns to this.
