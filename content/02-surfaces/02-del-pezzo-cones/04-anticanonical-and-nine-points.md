## Degrees two and one, and the plane blown up in nine points

### Statement

By Theorem P5.9.28, $-K$ is very ample on a del Pezzo surface of degree $d \geq 3$. This section treats the two remaining degrees, where the anticanonical map is a double cover and the involution of the cover realizes the isometries $\gamma$ and $\beta$ of Example 2.2.11, and then the first surface past the del Pezzo range, the plane blown up in the nine base points of a cubic pencil, whose cone of curves is not polyhedral.

**Theorem 2.2.18 (degree two: the double plane).** Let $S = S_7$, of degree $2$.

1. $h^0(-K) = 3$, the linear system $|-K|$ is base point free, and it defines a finite morphism $\varphi \colon S \to \mathbb{P}^2$ of degree $2$.
2. For each $(-1)$-curve $D$, the class $D' = -K - D$ is the class of a $(-1)$-curve, $D' \neq D$, $D \cdot D' = 2$, and $D + D' = \varphi^* L_D$ for a line $L_D \subseteq \mathbb{P}^2$, with $\varphi(D) = \varphi(D') = L_D$. The $56$ curves fall into $28$ pairs, and distinct pairs give distinct lines.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

**Theorem 2.2.19 (degree one: the double cone).** Let $S = S_8$, of degree $1$.

1. $h^0(-K) = 2$, and the pencil $|-K|$ has exactly one base point $o$. Every member of $|-K|$ is an integral curve of arithmetic genus $1$, smooth at $o$.
2. $|-2K|$ is base point free and $h^0(-2K) = 4$. If $x, y$ is a basis of $H^0(-K)$ and $z \in H^0(-2K)$ is not in the span of $x^2, xy, y^2$, then $[x^2 : xy : y^2 : z]$ is a finite morphism $\psi \colon S \to Q$ of degree $2$ onto the quadric cone $Q = \{X_0X_2 = X_1^2\} \subseteq \mathbb{P}^3$, and $\psi(o)$ is the vertex.
3. For each $(-1)$-curve $D$, the class $D' = -2K - D$ is the class of a $(-1)$-curve, $D \cdot D' = 3$, and $D + D' = \psi^* P_D$ for a plane $P_D$ with $\psi(D) = \psi(D') = P_D \cap Q$, a conic. The $240$ curves fall into $120$ pairs.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

**Theorem 2.2.20 (the plane blown up in nine points).** Let $C_t$ be a cubic pencil satisfying (H) of Definition P5.10.19, $X = X_9$ the blow-up of its nine base points, $f \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ the elliptic fibration of Theorem P5.10.21 and $F = -K$ its fibre class.

1. $\overline{NE}(X) \cap F^\perp = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F$, and this is an extremal ray, $K$-trivial.
2. $NE(X) = \overline{NE}(X) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F + \sum_C \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C]$, the sum over the $(-1)$-curves $C$ of $X$. There are infinitely many of them, each spans an extremal ray, and these rays accumulate only at $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F$. So $\overline{NE}(X)$ is closed and not polyhedral.
3. The contraction of any $(-1)$-curve of $X$ is a del Pezzo surface of degree $1$. Every run of the program from $X$ first contracts a $(-1)$-curve and then continues as in Theorem 2.2.14 with $r = 8$. So $X$ has infinitely many runs, all ending at $\mathbb{P}^2$, $F_1$ or $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

### Proof in words

In degree $2$ the contraction of a $(-1)$-curve is a cubic surface, and the sections of $-K$ are the hyperplane sections of the cubic through the contracted point, which have no common point on the blow-up. The anticanonical map then has degree $K^2 = 2$, and a $(-1)$-curve and its partner $-K - D$ together form an anticanonical curve, the pullback of a line. In degree $1$ the pencil $|-K|$ has one base point, and the restriction to a member, a curve of genus $1$ on which $-2K$ has degree $2$, produces a section of $-2K$ that does not vanish there; the squares of the two sections of $-K$ already satisfy the equation of the quadric cone. For nine points the anticanonical class is the fibre class of an elliptic fibration, nef and of square $0$. Any curve that is not a fibre has positive degree on $F$ and square at least $-1$, and a bound on its component orthogonal to $F$ and a section shows that limits of effective classes with $F$-degree tending to $0$ are multiples of $F$. The cone theorem then gives the cone, and the infinitely many sections of the fibration give infinitely many rays.

### Examples

**Example 2.2.21 (the $28$ bitangents).** In Theorem 2.2.18, a finite double cover of $\mathbb{P}^2$ by a smooth surface in characteristic $0$ is branched along a smooth curve $B$ of even degree $2m$, with $K_S = \varphi^*(K_{\mathbb{P}^2} + mL)$, where $L$ is a line (Kol96, Chapter III, Section 3, and Dolgachev, Classical algebraic geometry, Section 8.7). Here $K_S = -\varphi^* L$, so $-3 + m = -1$, $m = 2$ and $B$ is a smooth quartic. Over $k = \mathbb{C}$ the Euler numbers agree: $e(S_7) = 3 + 7 = 10$ and $e(S) = 2e(\mathbb{P}^2) - e(B) = 6 - e(B)$, so $e(B) = -4$ and $B$ has genus $3$, as a smooth plane quartic does. For each pair $\{D, D'\}$ of Theorem 2.2.18 the preimage of $L_D$ splits into two components, so $L_D$ meets $B$ with even multiplicity at each point, and the $28$ lines $L_D$ are the $28$ bitangents of the quartic. The covering involution of $\varphi$ exchanges $D$ and $D'$, so on $\operatorname{Num}(S)$ it is the Geiser involution $\gamma$ of Example 2.2.11. For instance $E_7$ and $3\ell - E_1 - \dots - E_6 - 2E_7$, the strict transform of the cubic through the seven points singular at $p_7$, map to the same bitangent.

**Example 2.2.22 (the $120$ tritangent planes).** In Theorem 2.2.19 the double cover $\psi$ is branched over the vertex of $Q$ and over a curve $B = Q \cap Q_3$ cut out by a cubic surface, of genus $4$. In the weighted projective space $\mathbb{P}(1, 1, 2, 3)$ with coordinates $x, y, z, w$ of weights $1, 1, 2, 3$, the ring $\bigoplus_n H^0(-nK)$ is generated by $x, y, z, w$ with one relation $w^2 = z^3 + a_2(x, y) z^2 + a_4(x, y) z + a_6(x, y)$, where $a_i$ is a form of degree $i$ (Kol96, Chapter III, Section 3, and Dolgachev, Classical algebraic geometry, Section 8.8). Over $\mathbb{C}$ the Euler numbers are $e(S_8) = 11$ and $e(Q) = 3$, and the cover is branched over $B$ and over the vertex, so $11 = 2 \cdot 3 - e(B) - 1$, $e(B) = -6$, and $B$ has genus $4$. Each plane $P_D$ of Theorem 2.2.19 has preimage $D + D'$, so it meets $B$ with even multiplicity at each of its $6$ intersection points counted with multiplicity: the $120$ planes are the tritangent planes of $B$. The covering involution is the Bertini involution $\beta$ of Example 2.2.11, and for instance $E_8$ and $6\ell - 2E_1 - \dots - 2E_7 - 3E_8$ map to the same conic.

**Example 2.2.23 (the rays $D_n$ approach the fibre class).** In Theorem 2.2.20 the classes $D_n$ of Proposition P5.10.17 (4) are classes of $(-1)$-curves by Theorem P5.10.22, and by Theorem P5.10.23, $D_n / (3n^2) \to -\frac13 K = \frac13 F$. So the extremal rays $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} D_n$ converge to the $K$-trivial ray $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F$, in accordance with Theorem P5.12.14 (3), which allows accumulation only on $K^\perp$. The nef cone is the dual, and it is not polyhedral either, by Theorem P5.10.23. The sections can be listed. Let $V = \{E_9, F\}^\perp$. A class orthogonal to $E_9$ lies in $I^{1,8}$, and for it $x \cdot F = -x \cdot k_8$, so $V = k_8^\perp \subseteq I^{1,8}$, the lattice $E_8$ of Proposition 2.2.7, negative definite and even. For $v \in V$ put $D_v = E_9 + v + \frac12 |v|^2 F$, with $|v|^2 = -v^2$. Then $D_v^2 = -1 - |v|^2 + |v|^2 = -1$ and $K \cdot D_v = -F \cdot D_v = -1$, so $D_v$ is exceptional. Conversely an exceptional class $D$ has $D \cdot F = 1$, so $D = E_9 + tF + v$ with $v \in V$, and $D^2 = -1 + 2t - |v|^2 = -1$ gives $t = \frac12 |v|^2$. So $v \mapsto D_v$ is a bijection from the lattice $E_8$ onto the $(-1)$-curves of $X$, by Theorem P5.10.22 (2), and $D_v \cdot E_9 = -1 + \frac12 |v|^2$. The curves disjoint from $E_9$ are the $D_v$ with $|v|^2 = 2$, one for each of the $240$ roots of $E_8$. These are the $240$ $(-1)$-curves of the contraction $S_8$ of $E_9$: a $(-1)$-curve of $S_8$ through the image point of $E_9$ would have a strict transform of square at most $-2$, and no curve of $X$ has that, by the proof of Theorem 2.2.20 (1).

### Proof

*Theorem 2.2.18.* (1) By Theorem P5.9.26 (3), $h^0(-K) = 10 - 7 = 3$. Let $\pi \colon S \to S'$ contract $E_7$, so $S' = S_6$ and $\pi(E_7) = p$. By Theorem P5.9.28, $-K_{S'}$ embeds $S'$ in $\mathbb{P}^3$ as a cubic surface with $\mathcal{O}_{S'}(1) = \mathcal{O}_{S'}(-K_{S'})$, and by Theorem P5.6.3 (3), $-K = \pi^*(-K_{S'}) - E_7$. For a hyperplane $H$ through $p$, Proposition P5.6.4 (2) gives $\pi^*(H \cap S') = \widetilde{H \cap S'} + mE_7$ with $m \geq 1$, so $\pi^*(H \cap S') - E_7$ is a member of $|-K|$. This gives an injective linear map from the $3$-dimensional space of linear forms vanishing at $p$ to $H^0(-K)$, which is bijective since $h^0(-K) = 3$. So the members of $|-K|$ are the curves $\pi^*(H \cap S') - E_7$ for the hyperplanes $H$ through $p$. Let $q \in S$. If $q \notin E_7$, a hyperplane through $p$ not through $\pi(q)$ gives a member not containing $q$. If $q \in E_7$, it is a tangent direction at $p$, spanning a line $\lambda \subseteq T_p S'$. The hyperplanes through $p$ form a net, those containing $\lambda$ form a pencil in it, and $T_p S'$ itself is in that pencil. Choose $H$ through $p$ outside the pencil. Then $H \not\supseteq T_pS'$, so $H \cap S'$ is smooth at $p$ with tangent line $H \cap T_p S' \neq \lambda$. Its member $\pi^*(H \cap S') - E_7$ is the strict transform, which meets $E_7$ only in the tangent direction of $H \cap S'$ by Proposition P5.6.4 (3), so it misses $q$. So $|-K|$ is base point free and defines $\varphi \colon S \to \mathbb{P}^2$ with $\varphi^* \mathcal{O}(1) = \mathcal{O}(-K)$. If $\varphi$ contracted an integral curve $\Gamma$, then $-K \cdot \Gamma = 0$, against the ampleness of $-K$. So $\varphi$ has finite fibres, and being projective, it is finite. Its degree is $(\varphi^* L)^2 = K^2 = 2$.

(2) Let $D$ be a $(-1)$-curve and $D' = -K - D$. Then $D'^2 = K^2 + 2K \cdot D + D^2 = 2 - 2 - 1 = -1$ and $K \cdot D' = -2 + 1 = -1$, so $D'$ is the class of a unique $(-1)$-curve by Theorem P5.9.26 (2). Also $D \cdot D' = -K \cdot D - D^2 = 2$, so $D' \neq D$. The divisor $D + D'$ lies in $|-K|$, since $\operatorname{Pic}(S) = \operatorname{Num}(S)$ for the rational surface $S$ (Theorem P5.9.19). The members of $|-K|$ are the pullbacks of the lines, because $\varphi$ is defined by the complete linear system and $h^0(-K) = 3$, so $D + D' = \varphi^* L_D$ for a line $L_D$. Then $\varphi(D) \subseteq L_D$, and $\varphi(D)$ is a curve because $\varphi$ is finite, so $\varphi(D) = L_D$, and likewise for $D'$. The map $D \mapsto D'$ is an involution without fixed points on the $56$ curves, giving $28$ pairs, and the pair is recovered from the line as the components of $\varphi^* L_D$. $\square$

*Theorem 2.2.19.* (1) By Theorem P5.9.26 (3), $h^0(-K) = 2$. Two distinct members $C, C'$ of $|-K|$ have no common component: a member is integral, because $-K \cdot \Gamma \geq 1$ for every integral curve by ampleness and $(-K)^2 = 1$, so a member has a single component, of multiplicity $1$. So $C \cdot C' = K^2 = 1$, and by Proposition P5.5.5 the two curves meet in exactly one point $o$, transversally, so both are smooth at $o$. Every member passes through $o$, since the members are $sC + tC'$ in terms of sections, and no other point is a base point. By Theorem P5.5.14, $2p_a(C) - 2 = C^2 + K \cdot C = 1 - 1 = 0$, so $p_a(C) = 1$.

(2) By Riemann-Roch (Theorem P5.5.13) and $\chi(\mathcal{O}_S) = 1$ (Proposition P5.9.19 (1)), $\chi(-2K) = 1 + \frac12(-2K)(-2K - K) = 1 + 3K^2 = 4$, and $h^2(-2K) = h^0(3K) = 0$ by Serre duality, since $-K \cdot 3K < 0$ for the ample $-K$. The sequence $0 \to \mathcal{O}_S(-K) \to \mathcal{O}_S(-2K) \to \mathcal{O}_C(-2K) \to 0$ for a member $C$ and $h^1(-K) = 0$ (Theorem P5.9.26 (3)) give $h^1(-2K) \leq h^1(\mathcal{O}_C(-2K))$. Write $M = \mathcal{O}_C(-2K)$, of degree $2$ on the integral curve $C$ of arithmetic genus $1$. By Theorem P5.5.14 (1), $\omega^\circ_C = \mathcal{O}_C(K + C) = \mathcal{O}_C$, so by Theorem P5.3.10, $h^1(M) = h^0(M^{-1}) = 0$, as an invertible sheaf of negative degree on an integral curve has no nonzero section. Hence $h^1(-2K) = 0$ and $h^0(-2K) = 4$, and $H^0(-2K) \to H^0(M)$ is surjective. By Theorem P5.3.10 in the same way, $h^0(M) = 2 + 1 - 1 = 2$ and $h^0(M(-o)) = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1$, where $M(-o)$ is invertible because $o$ is a smooth point of $C$. So some section of $M$ does not vanish at $o$, and it lifts to $z \in H^0(-2K)$ with $z(o) \neq 0$. Away from $o$ the sections $x^2$ and $y^2$ have no common zero. So $|-2K|$ is base point free. The sections $x^2, xy, y^2$ are linearly independent, since a relation would make the nonconstant rational function $x / y$ a root of a quadratic polynomial over $k$, hence constant. They vanish at $o$. The sections vanishing at $o$ form a subspace of dimension $3$, since some section does not vanish there, so this subspace is the span of $x^2, xy, y^2$. Hence a section $z$ outside the span has $z(o) \neq 0$, and $x^2, xy, y^2, z$ is a basis of $H^0(-2K)$. The morphism $\psi$ satisfies $X_0 X_2 = X_1^2$, so it maps into $Q$. It is finite because $-2K$ is ample, as in Theorem 2.2.18, so $\psi(S)$ is a surface, equal to the irreducible surface $Q$. Its degree is $(-2K)^2 / \deg Q = 4 / 2 = 2$. The point $\psi(o) = [0 : 0 : 0 : z(o)]$ is the vertex.

(3) As in Theorem 2.2.18, $D' = -2K - D$ has $D'^2 = 4 - 4 - 1 = -1$ and $K \cdot D' = -2 + 1 = -1$, so it is the class of a $(-1)$-curve, and $D \cdot D' = 2 + 1 = 3$. The divisor $D + D'$ lies in $|-2K|$, whose members are the pullbacks of the planes, as $h^0(-2K) = 4$ and $\psi$ is given by the complete system. So $D + D' = \psi^* P_D$. The curve $D$ does not pass through $o$: otherwise choose $q \in D \setminus \{o\}$ and the member $C$ of the pencil $|-K|$ through $q$. Then $C \neq D$, since $C^2 = 1$ and $D^2 = -1$, and $C$ meets $D$ at $o$ and at $q$, so $C \cdot D \geq 2$ by Proposition P5.5.5, against $-K \cdot D = 1$. Since $\psi^{-1}(\psi(o)) = \{x = y = 0\} = \{o\}$, the curve $\psi(D)$ does not pass through the vertex. Every line on the cone $Q$ passes through the vertex, so $\psi(D)$ is not a line. As $\psi_*[D]$ has degree $-2K \cdot D = 2$ and $\psi(D) \subseteq P_D \cap Q$, a plane conic, $\psi(D) = P_D \cap Q$ and $\psi$ maps $D$ birationally onto it. The same holds for $D'$. The involution $D \mapsto D'$ has no fixed points, so there are $120$ pairs. $\square$

*Theorem 2.2.20.* (1) Let $e = E_9$, a section of $f$ with $e \cdot F = 1$ and $e^2 = -1$ (Theorem P5.10.21 (3)). The plane spanned by $e$ and $F$ has Gram matrix with determinant $-1 \cdot 0 - 1 = -1$, so it has signature $(1, 1)$, and since the form on $N^1(X)$ has signature $(1, 9)$ by Lemma P5.12.3 (1), the form on the orthogonal complement $V$ of the plane is negative definite. Write $|v| = (-v^2)^{1/2}$ for $v \in V \otimes \mathbb{R}$. Every class is $x = me + tF + v$ with $m = x \cdot F$, $t = x \cdot e + m$ and $v \in V \otimes \mathbb{R}$, and $x^2 = -m^2 + 2mt - |v|^2$.

Let $\Gamma$ be an integral curve. If $F \cdot \Gamma = 0$, then $f(\Gamma)$ is a point, and $\Gamma$ is the fibre through it, which is integral by Theorem P5.10.21 (2); so $\Gamma$ has class $F$. Otherwise $m = F \cdot \Gamma \geq 1$, and by Corollary P5.5.15 (1), $\Gamma^2 \geq -2 - K \cdot \Gamma = m - 2 \geq -1$. If $\Gamma \neq e$, then $t = \Gamma \cdot e + m \geq 1$; if $\Gamma = e$, then $m = 1$, $t = 0$ and $v = 0$. In both cases $t \geq 0$ and
$$|v|^2 = 2mt - m^2 - \Gamma^2 \leq 2mt + 1 \leq m(2t + 1).$$
For every $\delta > 0$, $|v| \leq \sqrt{m(2t + 1)} \leq \frac12(\delta(2t + 1) + m/\delta) \leq \delta t + m(\delta + \delta^{-1})$, using $m \geq 1$.

Let $y \in \overline{NE}(X)$ with $F \cdot y = 0$, and $y_n \to y$ with $y_n = f_n F + \sum_j c_{nj} [\Gamma_{nj}]$, $f_n, c_{nj} \geq 0$, the $\Gamma_{nj}$ integral curves with $F \cdot \Gamma_{nj} \geq 1$. Write $y_n = M_n e + T_n F + V_n$ as above. Then $M_n = F \cdot y_n \to 0$, $T_n = f_n + \sum_j c_{nj} t_{nj}$ with every term nonnegative and $T_n \to t(y)$, and $|V_n| \leq \sum_j c_{nj} |v_{nj}| \leq \delta T_n + M_n(\delta + \delta^{-1})$. Letting $n \to \infty$ gives $|v(y)| \leq \delta\, t(y)$ for every $\delta > 0$, so $v(y) = 0$, and $y = t(y) F$ with $t(y) = \lim T_n \geq 0$. So $\overline{NE}(X) \cap F^\perp = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F$. The class $F$ is nef, as the class of a fibre (Lemma P5.12.4), so $F^\perp \cap \overline{NE}(X)$ is a face, and the ray $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F$ is extremal. It is $K$-trivial since $K = -F$ and $F^2 = 0$.

(2) Since $K = -F$, $\overline{NE}(X)_{K \geq 0} = \overline{NE}(X) \cap F^\perp = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F$, using $F \cdot y \geq 0$ on $\overline{NE}(X)$. By Theorem P5.12.14 (1), $\overline{NE}(X) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F + \sum_i \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C_i]$ over the $K$-negative extremal rays, and since $\rho(X) = 10$ these are all of type 1 by Theorem P5.12.15, spanned by $(-1)$-curves. Conversely every $(-1)$-curve spans a $K$-negative extremal ray by Lemma P5.12.11. The right side is spanned by classes of curves, so it is contained in $NE(X)$, and $NE(X) = \overline{NE}(X)$. By Theorem P5.10.22 (3) there are infinitely many $(-1)$-curves, spanning distinct extremal rays, and a polyhedral cone has finitely many extremal rays. By Theorem P5.12.14 (3) a limit of normalized generators of infinitely many of these rays lies in $K^\perp \cap \overline{NE}(X) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} F$, and it is nonzero, as it lies on the compact slice of Lemma P5.12.2 (3).

(3) Let $C$ be a $(-1)$-curve and $\pi \colon X \to X'$ its contraction. By Theorem P5.6.3 (3), $\pi^*(-K_{X'}) = -K + C = F + C$, and $K_{X'}^2 = 1$. Let $\Gamma'$ be an integral curve of $X'$ with strict transform $\Gamma$. Then $-K_{X'} \cdot \Gamma' = (F + C) \cdot \Gamma$ by Lemma P5.11.15 (3). If $\Gamma$ is a fibre, this is $0 + 1 = 1$; otherwise $F \cdot \Gamma \geq 1$ and $C \cdot \Gamma \geq 0$. So $-K_{X'}$ is ample by Theorem P5.12.5, and $X'$ is del Pezzo of degree $1$, isomorphic to some $S_8$ by Theorem P5.9.27. Since $K_X = -F$ is not nef, a run from $X$ starts with the contraction of a $K$-negative extremal ray, which by (2) is a $(-1)$-curve, and continues as a run from $X'$, which Theorem 2.2.14 describes. Distinct $(-1)$-curves give distinct first steps, so there are infinitely many runs. $\square$

### Notes and history

The double plane branched along a quartic and the $28$ bitangents go back to Aronhold and Geiser, and the double cone with its $120$ tritangent planes to Bertini. The description of $\bigoplus_n H^0(-nK)$ by weighted projective spaces is in Kollár, Rational curves on algebraic varieties, Kol96, Chapter III, Section 3, together with the base point freeness of $|-K|$ for $d \geq 2$ and of $|-2K|$ for $d = 1$. The plane blown up in nine points was Nagata's tool in his 1960 papers on rational surfaces: for nine very general points the only member of $|-K|$ is a single cubic, there is no fibration, and $X_9$ still has infinitely many $(-1)$-curves, the orbit of $E_9$ under the infinite Weyl group $W_9$. That case is not treated here. For ten or more very general points the shape of the cone near $K^\perp$ is the subject of the Nagata and Segre-Harbourne-Gimigliano-Hirschowitz conjectures. The non-polyhedral cone of curves of a rational surface in Theorem 2.2.20 is the basic example that the cone theorem cannot be improved: the $K$-negative part is locally polyhedral, but the rays accumulate at $K^\perp$.
