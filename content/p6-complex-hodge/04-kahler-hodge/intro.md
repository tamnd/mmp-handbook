# Kähler manifolds and Hodge theory

A Hermitian metric on a complex manifold gives a fundamental $(1,1)$-form $\omega$. The metric is Kähler when $d\omega = 0$. This one condition changes the cohomology of a compact complex manifold completely. It makes the de Rham Laplacian twice the Dolbeault Laplacian, so the harmonic forms that represent de Rham classes split by type, and the de Rham cohomology splits into Dolbeault groups $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X)$. It also makes wedge product with $\omega$ commute with the Laplacian, and this gives a representation of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ on cohomology. The results are strong topological restrictions: odd Betti numbers are even, even Betti numbers are positive, the powers of the Kähler class give isomorphisms between cohomology groups of complementary degrees, and the intersection form on a surface has a sign pattern fixed by the Hodge numbers.

Section 4.1 defines Kähler manifolds. It shows that the Kähler condition is equivalent to the existence of local potentials $\omega = i\partial\bar\partial\varphi$, and also to the existence of holomorphic coordinates in which the metric agrees with the flat metric up to second order. It then checks that complex tori, Riemann surfaces, projective space and all projective manifolds are Kähler, and that the Hopf surface is not, because its second Betti number is $0$. Section 4.2 is linear algebra on one tangent space followed by one local computation. The operators $L$, $\Lambda$ and $[L, \Lambda]$ give the Lefschetz decomposition of forms into primitive pieces. Weil's formula computes the Hodge star on each piece. The Kähler identities $[\Lambda, \bar\partial] = -i\partial^*$ and $[\Lambda, \partial] = i\bar\partial^*$ are proved first on $\mathbb{C}^n$, where every sign is checked, and then transported to a Kähler manifold by normal coordinates.

Section 4.3 states the Hodge theorem for the Laplacians of a compact Hermitian manifold. It is the one analytic input of the chapter, and it is cited, not proved. From it the section derives finiteness, Serre duality, the Hodge decomposition $H^k(X, \mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(X)$ with Hodge symmetry, and the $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma. Section 4.4 proves the hard Lefschetz theorem and the Hodge-Riemann bilinear relations by applying the linear algebra of Section 4.2 to harmonic forms. It deduces the Hodge index theorem for surfaces, $\sigma = 2 + 2h^{2,0} - h^{1,1}$, and computes Hodge diamonds: projective space, curves of every genus, plane curves, abelian surfaces, products of curves and the quartic surface in $\mathbb{P}^3$. The last example is a closed semipositive class on $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ for which hard Lefschetz fails.

The later chapters use these results in three ways. Chapter 5 uses the Hodge decomposition of $H^1$ and $H^2$ to study the Picard torus and the Lefschetz theorem on $(1,1)$ classes. Chapter 6 extends the Kähler identities to forms with values in a Hermitian line bundle and proves the Kodaira vanishing theorem, which P7 cites as a black box. The Hodge index theorem of Section 4.4 is the analytic counterpart of Theorem P5.5.22.

The chapter follows Huybrechts, Chapter 3 (Huy05), Voisin, Chapters 3, 5 and 6 (Voi02), Griffiths and Harris, Sections 0.6 and 0.7 (GH78), and Wells, Chapter V (Wel80).
