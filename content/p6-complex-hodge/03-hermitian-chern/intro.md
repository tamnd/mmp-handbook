# Hermitian geometry and Chern classes

A holomorphic line bundle has two kinds of invariants. The first is the class $[L] \in H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X^*)$ of its transition functions, together with the integral class $c_1(L) \in H^2(X, \mathbb{Z})$ that the exponential sequence extracts from it. The second is a closed differential form, built from a Hermitian metric on $L$ and the curvature of a connection. This chapter constructs the forms, shows that their classes do not depend on the choices, and proves that the two invariants agree in $H^2(X, \mathbb{C})$. Positivity of line bundles, Kähler metrics and the vanishing theorems of Chapter 6 are all statements about these curvature forms.

Section 3.1 is linear algebra on a manifold. A Hermitian metric on a complex manifold is the same thing as a Riemannian metric compatible with the complex structure, and also the same thing as a real positive $(1,1)$-form, its fundamental form. Positive $(1,1)$-forms are the forms whose coefficient matrix is positive definite. Partitions of unity give Hermitian metrics on every bundle. The flat metrics on $\mathbb{C}^n$ and on tori and the Fubini-Study form $\omega_{FS}$ on $\mathbb{P}^n$ are the basic examples, and the standard metric of $\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$ induces metrics on $\mathcal{O}(-1)$ and $\mathcal{O}(1)$.

Section 3.2 introduces connections, their curvature and the Bianchi identity, and proves the theorem of Chern: a holomorphic bundle with a Hermitian metric has exactly one connection that preserves the metric and whose $(0,1)$-part is $\bar\partial_E$. In a holomorphic frame its connection matrix is $H^{-1} \partial H$, and its curvature $\bar\partial(H^{-1}\partial H)$ has type $(1,1)$. For a line bundle the curvature is $-\partial\bar\partial \log h$, and for $\mathcal{O}(1)$ with the induced metric, $\frac{i}{2\pi}$ times the curvature is $\omega_{FS}$. Curvature decreases in holomorphic subbundles, and an explicit metric connection on the trivial bundle of $\mathbb{C}^2$ shows what goes wrong without holomorphy.

Section 3.3 is Chern-Weil theory. An invariant polynomial evaluated on the curvature of any connection gives a closed form whose de Rham class does not depend on the connection. The Chern classes come from $\det(I + \frac{i}{2\pi} F)$, and they satisfy naturality and the Whitney formula. The integral first Chern class is defined as $c_1(L) = -\delta([L])$ through the exponential sequence, with the sign that makes $c_1(\mathcal{O}(1))$ positive, and its image in $H^2(X, \mathbb{C})$ is the class of $\frac{i}{2\pi} F$. On a compact Riemann surface the integral of $c_1(L)$ is the number of zeros minus the number of poles of a meromorphic section. The examples compute $c(T_{\mathbb{P}^n}) = (1 + h)^{n+1}$ and the canonical degree $d(d-3)$ of a smooth plane curve.

The chapter follows Huybrechts, Sections 1.2 and 4.1 to 4.4 (Huy05), Griffiths and Harris, Sections 0.5 and 1.1 (GH78), Wells, Chapter III (Wel80), and Voisin, Chapter 3 (Voi02). The topological facts used, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem among them, are cited from Bott and Tu, Chapter IV (BT82).
