# Singular metrics and analytic multiplier ideals

Kodaira's theorem in Chapter 6 asks for a smooth metric of positive curvature. The minimal model program works with line bundles that are only big, or with $\mathbb{Q}$-divisors whose coefficients are fractional, and the natural metrics on such bundles are singular: the metric $1/|s|^2$ defined by a section $s$ is infinite along the zero set of $s$, and its curvature is the current of integration along the divisor of $s$. Analysis tolerates such metrics. The weight of a singular metric is a plurisubharmonic function, its curvature is a closed positive current, the size of its singularity at a point is measured by the Lelong number, and the holomorphic functions that are square integrable against the metric form the analytic multiplier ideal. Hörmander's $L^2$ estimates for the $\bar\partial$ operator then hold with the singular weight, and they give Nadel's vanishing theorem, in which the multiplier ideal is the correction term. For the metric of an effective $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor, the analytic multiplier ideal is the algebraic one of P7, Chapter 10, and the analytic theorem becomes the algebraic Nadel vanishing.

Section 8.1 defines plurisubharmonic functions, proves their basic properties and their regularization by convolution, introduces closed positive currents of bidegree $(1,1)$, proves the Poincaré-Lelong formula on the smooth part of a zero set, and defines singular Hermitian metrics, curvature currents and pseudo-effective line bundles. The metrics of a linear system and of a divisor are the main examples, and the exceptional curve of the blow-up of the plane gives a pseudo-effective bundle with no smooth semipositive metric. Section 8.2 defines Lelong numbers through the growth of the maximum on small balls, proves that they are invariant under coordinate changes, that they compute multiplicities and that they are upper semicontinuous, states Siu's theorem and Skoda's integrability lemma, proves the half of Skoda's lemma that bounds the multiplier ideal from above, proves Nadel's coherence theorem from Hörmander's estimate, and proves that the analytic multiplier ideals of divisors and ideals agree with the algebraic ones through a change of variables on a log resolution. The cusp is computed twice, once on its log resolution and once by a weighted scaling. Section 8.3 states Hörmander's theorem on pseudoconvex domains and its form for $(n, q)$-forms with values in a line bundle, sketches the proof from the Bochner-Kodaira-Nakano inequality of Chapter 6, proves that $\bar\partial$ equations extend across hypersurfaces, proves Nadel vanishing on a projective manifold, and deduces the algebraic Nadel vanishing of P7, Chapter 10, in the case where $L - D$ is ample. The cuspidal cubic in the plane shows that the vanishing is sharp.

Throughout the chapter complex manifolds are second countable and connected unless stated otherwise, $n$ is the complex dimension, and $d\lambda$ is Lebesgue measure in local coordinates. The normalizations are fixed in Section 8.1: a singular metric is written $|e|_h^2 = e^{-2\varphi}$ on a local frame $e$, its curvature current is $i\Theta_h = 2 i \partial\bar\partial \varphi$, and the multiplier ideal is defined by the integrability of $|f|^2 e^{-2\varphi}$. With these conventions the weight of a divisor $D$ is $\sum_j c_j \log|g_j|$, its Lelong numbers are multiplicities, and $\frac{i}{2\pi}\Theta_h$ represents the first Chern class.

The chapter follows Demailly's book, Chapters I, III and VIII (Dem97a), Demailly's lectures on analytic methods (Dem12a), Hörmander's book, Chapter IV (Hor90), and Lazarsfeld, Section 9.3 (Laz04), for the comparison with the algebraic theory.
