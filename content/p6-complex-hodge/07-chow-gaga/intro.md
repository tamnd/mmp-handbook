# Chow, GAGA and the Lefschetz principle

Chapters 4 to 6 work with compact complex manifolds, Hermitian metrics and harmonic forms. The rest of the handbook works with schemes, coherent sheaves and their cohomology over an algebraically closed field. This chapter builds the bridge between the two. A scheme of finite type over $\mathbb{C}$ has an associated complex analytic space, and a coherent sheaf on the scheme has an associated coherent analytic sheaf. For projective schemes Serre's theorems, called GAGA after the title of his paper, say that nothing is lost in this passage: the cohomology groups agree, the morphisms of sheaves agree, and every coherent analytic sheaf is algebraic. With GAGA in hand, the analytic theorems of Chapters 4 to 6 become algebraic theorems, first over $\mathbb{C}$ and then, by the Lefschetz principle, over every algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$.

Section 7.1 constructs the analytification $X^{\mathrm{an}}$ of a scheme of finite type over $\mathbb{C}$ and the analytification $\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{an}}$ of a coherent sheaf, proves that the local rings of $X^{\mathrm{an}}$ are faithfully flat over those of $X$, states the GAGA theorems for projective schemes, and proves them. The proof computes the analytic cohomology of the line bundles $\mathcal{O}(m)$ on projective space from the Hodge decomposition of Chapter 4, proves Serre's analytic form of Theorems A and B on projective space from the finiteness theorem of Cartan and Serre, and then follows Serre's descending induction. Section 7.2 deduces Chow's theorem that closed analytic subsets of projective space are algebraic, and its consequences: line bundles, meromorphic functions and holomorphic maps on projective varieties are algebraic, compact complex manifolds with a positive line bundle are projective varieties, and the Kodaira vanishing theorem of Chapter 6 holds for ample line bundles on smooth projective varieties over $\mathbb{C}$. The worked examples are the graph of the exponential function, which is analytic and not algebraic, and a complex torus with no line bundle of positive degree. Section 7.3 states the Lefschetz principle in the precise form that a variety over a field of characteristic $0$ comes from a finitely generated field, which embeds into $\mathbb{C}$, and that coherent cohomology does not change under extension of the field. It compares algebraic and analytic Dolbeault cohomology, defines algebraic de Rham cohomology, proves that it computes the singular cohomology of $X^{\mathrm{an}}$ for smooth projective $X$, and deduces that the Hodge to de Rham spectral sequence degenerates and the Hodge numbers are symmetric over every field of characteristic $0$. The worked examples are projective space, a plane cubic, a quartic surface in $\mathbb{P}^3$ and the affine and punctured lines.

Throughout the chapter schemes are of finite type over $\mathbb{C}$ in Sections 7.1 and 7.2, and over a field $k$ of characteristic $0$ in Section 7.3. Two analytic inputs are cited and not proved: the coherence theorems of Oka and Cartan, and the finiteness theorem of Cartan and Serre for compact complex manifolds. Cartan's Theorem B is cited for the affine pieces of projective space.

The chapter follows Serre's paper (Ser56), Grothendieck, SGA 1, Exposé XII (SGA1), Hartshorne, Appendix B (Har77), Griffiths and Harris, Chapter 1 (GH78), and Grothendieck's paper on algebraic de Rham cohomology (Gro66a).
