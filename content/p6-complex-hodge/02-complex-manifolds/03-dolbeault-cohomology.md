## Dolbeault cohomology

### Statement

The Dolbeault-Grothendieck lemma (Theorem P6.1.36) says that $\bar\partial$-closed forms are locally $\bar\partial$-exact. Globally they need not be, and the obstruction is Dolbeault cohomology. This section identifies it with the sheaf cohomology of holomorphic bundles, identifies de Rham cohomology with the cohomology of the constant sheaf, relates the two by the Frölicher spectral sequence, and computes examples. In this section $X$ is a complex manifold of dimension $n$ and $E$ a holomorphic vector bundle on $X$.

**Definition P6.2.22 (Dolbeault and de Rham cohomology).** In a local holomorphic frame $e_1, \dots, e_r$ of $E$ define $\bar\partial_E \colon \mathcal{A}^{p,q}_X(E) \to \mathcal{A}^{p,q+1}_X(E)$ by
$$\bar\partial_E \Big( \sum_a \alpha_a \otimes e_a \Big) = \sum_a \bar\partial \alpha_a \otimes e_a.$$
This does not depend on the frame, satisfies $\bar\partial_E^2 = 0$ and $\bar\partial_E(f \sigma) = \bar\partial f \wedge \sigma + f \bar\partial_E \sigma$, and its kernel on $\mathcal{A}^{p,0}_X(E)$ is the sheaf $\Omega^p_X \otimes E$ of holomorphic sections of $\Lambda^p \Omega_X \otimes E$. The *Dolbeault cohomology* of $X$ with values in $E$ is
$$H^{p,q}(X, E) = \frac{\ker(\bar\partial_E \colon A^{p,q}(X, E) \to A^{p,q+1}(X, E))}{\bar\partial_E A^{p,q-1}(X, E)},$$
where $A^{p,q}(X, E)$ is the space of global sections of $\mathcal{A}^{p,q}_X(E)$ and $A^{p,-1} = 0$. For $E = \mathcal{O}_X$ write $H^{p,q}(X)$, and $h^{p,q}(X) = \dim_{\mathbb{C}} H^{p,q}(X) \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\} \cup \{\infty\}$, the *Hodge numbers*. The *de Rham cohomology* is $H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C}) = \ker(d \colon A^k(X) \to A^{k+1}(X)) / d A^{k-1}(X)$ for complex forms, and $H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{R})$ for real forms. Since $d$ is real, $H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C}) = H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{R}) \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$, and the *Betti numbers* are $b_k(X) = \dim_{\mathbb{C}} H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C})$, possibly infinite.

**Lemma P6.2.23 (sheaves of $\mathcal{A}_X$-modules are acyclic).** Let $M$ be a smooth manifold, Hausdorff and second countable, and $\mathcal{F}$ a sheaf of modules over the sheaf $C^\infty_M$ of real valued smooth functions. Then $H^i(M, \mathcal{F}) = 0$ for all $i > 0$. This applies to every sheaf of $\mathcal{A}_X$-modules on a complex manifold, in particular to $\mathcal{A}^k_X$, $\mathcal{A}^{p,q}_X$ and $\mathcal{A}^{p,q}_X(E)$.

**Theorem P6.2.24 (Dolbeault isomorphism).** For all $p, q \geq 0$ there is an isomorphism
$$H^q(X, \Omega^p_X \otimes E) \cong H^{p,q}(X, E).$$
In particular $H^q(X, E) \cong H^{0,q}(X, E)$, $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X) \cong H^{p,q}(X)$, and $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X \otimes E) = 0$ for $q > n$.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

**Theorem P6.2.25 (de Rham theorem).** Let $M$ be a smooth manifold of dimension $m$. For all $k$ there are isomorphisms
$$H^k(M, \mathbb{C}_M) \cong H^k_{dR}(M, \mathbb{C}), \qquad H^k(M, \mathbb{R}_M) \cong H^k_{dR}(M, \mathbb{R}),$$
where the left sides are the cohomology of the constant sheaves. In particular both vanish for $k > m$. The sheaf cohomology of the constant sheaf, and so de Rham cohomology, is also isomorphic to singular cohomology with coefficients in $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$; this comparison is cited as a black box from BT82 (Chapter II, the Čech-de Rham complex, and Chapter III, singular cohomology) and is used only to interpret Betti numbers topologically.

**Proposition P6.2.26 (the holomorphic de Rham complex and the Frölicher spectral sequence).**

1. The *holomorphic de Rham complex*
$$0 \to \mathbb{C}_X \to \mathcal{O}_X \xrightarrow{\partial} \Omega^1_X \xrightarrow{\partial} \Omega^2_X \to \cdots \xrightarrow{\partial} \Omega^n_X \to 0$$
is an exact sequence of sheaves; on holomorphic forms $\partial = d$.
2. The spaces $A^{p,q}(X)$ with $d_h = \partial$ and $d_v = \bar\partial$ form a first quadrant double complex with total complex $(A^\bullet(X), d)$. Its first spectral sequence is a convergent spectral sequence, the *Frölicher spectral sequence*,
$$E_1^{p,q} = H^{p,q}(X) \cong H^q(X, \Omega^p_X) \Rightarrow H^{p+q}_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C}) \cong H^{p+q}(X, \mathbb{C}_X),$$
with $d_1 \colon H^{p,q}(X) \to H^{p+1,q}(X)$ induced by $\partial$.
3. If $h^{p,q}(X) < \infty$ for all $p + q = k$, then $b_k(X) \leq \sum_{p+q=k} h^{p,q}(X)$. If all Hodge numbers are finite, then all Betti numbers are finite and
$$\sum_k (-1)^k b_k(X) = \sum_{p,q} (-1)^{p+q} h^{p,q}(X).$$

### Proof in words

A sheaf of modules over smooth functions has no higher cohomology, because a partition of unity glues local solutions of the equation that a cohomology class poses, and an induction along the Godement resolution reduces all degrees to degree one. The Dolbeault-Grothendieck lemma makes $\mathcal{A}^{p,\bullet}_X(E)$ a resolution of $\Omega^p_X \otimes E$ and the Poincaré lemma makes $\mathcal{A}^\bullet_X$ a resolution of $\mathbb{C}_X$; both resolutions are acyclic, so their global sections compute sheaf cohomology. The sheaves $\mathcal{A}^{p,q}_X$ form a double complex whose columns resolve the $\Omega^p_X$ and whose total complex resolves $\mathbb{C}_X$; comparing the two gives the exactness of the holomorphic de Rham complex, and the spectral sequence of the double complex of global sections relates Dolbeault and de Rham cohomology. On a torus, Fourier series diagonalize $d$ and $\bar\partial$: on the Fourier mode of a nonzero character they act as exterior multiplication by a nonzero constant form, which has no cohomology, so only invariant forms survive.

### Examples

**Example P6.2.27 (the projective line).** On $\mathbb{P}^1$ let $U_0, U_1$ be the standard charts with coordinates $t$ and $s = 1/t$, so $U_0 \cong U_1 \cong \mathbb{C}$ and $U_0 \cap U_1 \cong \mathbb{C}^*$. By Theorem P6.2.24 and Theorem P6.1.36, applied to the disc $\{|z| < \infty\}$ and the annulus $\{0 < |z| < \infty\}$, $H^q(U, \mathcal{O}) = H^{0,q}(U) = 0$ for $q > 0$ and $U = U_0, U_1, U_0 \cap U_1$. Since $\mathcal{O}(k)$ is trivial on $U_0$ and $U_1$, Leray's theorem (Theorem P2.7.21) computes $H^q(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(k))$ by the Čech complex of Definition P2.7.18:
$$0 \to \mathcal{O}(k)(U_0) \times \mathcal{O}(k)(U_1) \to \mathcal{O}(k)(U_0 \cap U_1) \to 0, \qquad (\sigma_0, \sigma_1) \mapsto \sigma_1 - \sigma_0.$$
Write sections on $U_0 \cap U_1$ in the frame $e_0$. A section over $U_0$ is $f_0(t) e_0$ with $f_0$ entire, and since $e_1 = g_{01} e_0 = t^k e_0$, a section over $U_1$ is $f_1(1/t) t^k e_0$ with $f_1$ entire. By Proposition P6.1.35, a holomorphic function on $\mathbb{C}^*$ has a unique Laurent expansion $\sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} c_m t^m$ converging on $\mathbb{C}^*$, whose parts with $m \geq 0$ and $m < 0$ converge separately; entire functions of $t$ have only $m \geq 0$, and $f_1(1/t) t^k$ has only $m \leq k$. So the kernel of the Čech differential, $H^0$, consists of the pairs with $f_0(t) = t^k f_1(1/t)$, that is, of the polynomials $f_0$ of degree at most $k$; and the image consists of the Laurent series without the terms $t^m$, $k < m < 0$, so their classes form a basis of $H^1$. Therefore
$$h^0(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(k)) = \max(k + 1, 0), \qquad h^1(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(k)) = \max(-k - 1, 0),$$
in accordance with Proposition P6.2.17. Since $\Omega^1_{\mathbb{P}^1} = K_{\mathbb{P}^1} \cong \mathcal{O}(-2)$ (Proposition P6.2.18), Theorem P6.2.24 gives the Hodge numbers
$$h^{0,0} = 1, \quad h^{1,0} = h^0(\mathcal{O}(-2)) = 0, \quad h^{0,1} = h^1(\mathcal{O}) = 0, \quad h^{1,1} = h^1(\mathcal{O}(-2)) = 1,$$
with $H^{1,1}(\mathbb{P}^1) \cong H^1(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(-2))$ spanned by the class of $t^{-1}$. By Proposition P6.2.26, $b_1 = 0$ and $b_2 \leq 1$; Example P6.2.31 gives $b_2 = 1$, so the inequality is an equality here.

**Example P6.2.28 (the punctured plane).** Let $X = \mathbb{C}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, covered by $U_1 = \{z_1 \neq 0\} = \mathbb{C}^* \times \mathbb{C}$ and $U_2 = \{z_2 \neq 0\} = \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}^*$, with $U_1 \cap U_2 = (\mathbb{C}^*)^2$. These are products of discs and annuli with radii in $[0, \infty]$, so $H^q(U, \mathcal{O}) = 0$ for $q > 0$ on each of them by Theorems P6.2.24 and P6.1.36, and by Leray's theorem
$$H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \cong \frac{\mathcal{O}((\mathbb{C}^*)^2)}{\mathcal{O}(U_1) + \mathcal{O}(U_2)}, \qquad H^q(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = 0 \text{ for } q \geq 2.$$
A holomorphic function on $(\mathbb{C}^*)^2$ has a unique Laurent expansion $\sum_{a, b \in \mathbb{Z}} c_{ab} z_1^a z_2^b$ (Proposition P6.1.35). By the same proposition applied to the product $\mathbb{C}^* \times \mathbb{C}$ of an annulus and a disc, the restriction of a function in $\mathcal{O}(U_1)$ has $c_{ab} = 0$ for $b < 0$, and similarly functions in $\mathcal{O}(U_2)$ have $c_{ab} = 0$ for $a < 0$. Conversely the part of a Laurent series with $b \geq 0$ converges on $U_1$, and the part with $a \geq 0, b < 0$ on $U_2$. So $H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$ is isomorphic to the space of Laurent series $\sum_{a, b \leq -1} c_{ab} z_1^a z_2^b$ converging on $(\mathbb{C}^*)^2$, and the classes of $z_1^{-a} z_2^{-b}$, $a, b \geq 1$, are linearly independent: $h^{0,1}(X) = h^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = \infty$. Here $H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = \mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C}^2)$ by Corollary P6.1.15. This is the global form of Example P6.1.41: on the noncompact manifold $\mathbb{C}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, which is not a product of discs and annuli, Dolbeault cohomology is infinite dimensional and the Dolbeault-Grothendieck lemma fails. Its quotient, the Hopf surface, is compact, and there Chapter 4 shows that the Hodge numbers are finite.

**Theorem P6.2.29 (cohomology of complex tori).** Let $X = V/\Lambda$ be a complex torus, $V = \mathbb{C}^n$. Call a form on $X$ *invariant* if its coefficients in the basis $dz_I \wedge d\bar z_J$ are constant; these are the forms invariant under all translations, and they are $d$-, $\partial$- and $\bar\partial$-closed.

1. Every class in $H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C})$ contains exactly one invariant $k$-form, so $b_k = \binom{2n}{k}$.
2. Every class in $H^{p,q}(X)$ contains exactly one invariant $(p,q)$-form, so $h^{p,q} = \binom{n}{p} \binom{n}{q}$, and $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X) \cong H^{p,q}(X)$ has the basis $dz_I \wedge d\bar z_J$, $|I| = p$, $|J| = q$.
3. $H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(X)$, the classes of type $(p,q)$ being those with an invariant representative of type $(p,q)$, and the Frölicher spectral sequence degenerates at $E_1$.

**Example P6.2.30 (elliptic curves).** For $E_\tau = \mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z} + \mathbb{Z}\tau)$, $\operatorname{Im} \tau > 0$, Theorem P6.2.29 gives $h^{0,0} = h^{1,0} = h^{0,1} = h^{1,1} = 1$, with representatives $1$, $dz$, $d\bar z$, $dz \wedge d\bar z$, and $b_0 = b_2 = 1$, $b_1 = 2$. Concretely, a real linear form $\lambda(z) = a z + \bar a \bar z$ lies in the dual lattice when $\lambda(1) = 2 \operatorname{Re} a$ and $\lambda(\tau) = 2 \operatorname{Re}(a\tau)$ are integers, and then $e_\lambda = e^{2\pi i \lambda}$ satisfies $\bar\partial e_\lambda = 2\pi i \bar a \, e_\lambda \, d\bar z$. So $e_\lambda \, d\bar z = \bar\partial (e_\lambda/(2\pi i \bar a))$ is exact for $\lambda \neq 0$, while $d\bar z$ is not: if $d\bar z = \bar\partial u$, then $\partial u/\partial \bar z = 1$, and with $d\bar z \wedge dz = 2i \, dx \wedge dy$ Stokes' theorem gives
$$0 = \int_{E_\tau} d(u \, dz) = \int_{E_\tau} \frac{\partial u}{\partial \bar z} \, d\bar z \wedge dz = 2i \operatorname{Im} \tau \neq 0.$$
In the same way $g \, d\bar z$ is $\bar\partial$-exact exactly when $\int_{E_\tau} g \, dx \wedge dy = 0$. By Theorem P6.2.24, $H^1(E_\tau, \mathcal{O}) \cong \mathbb{C}$ and $H^0(E_\tau, \Omega^1) = \mathbb{C} \, dz$, the canonical bundle being trivial (Example P6.2.14). The equality $b_1 = 2 = h^{1,0} + h^{0,1}$ is the degeneration of part 3.

A noncompact quotient. For the rank one subgroup $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ the quotient $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{C}^*$ is not a complex torus, and Theorem P6.2.29 fails for it. The invariant forms $1, dz, d\bar z, dz \wedge d\bar z$ would give $h^{0,1} = 1$ and $b_1 = 2$, but $H^{0,1}(\mathbb{C}^*) = 0$ by Theorem P6.1.36, $H^{1,0}(\mathbb{C}^*) = H^0(\mathbb{C}^*, \Omega^1)$ contains the independent forms $z^m \, dz$, $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $h^{1,0} = \infty$, and $b_1 = 1$ because $\mathbb{C}^*$ is homotopy equivalent to $S^1$ (homotopy invariance, BT82, Chapter I). In the proof the dual group $\{\lambda : \lambda(\mathbb{Z}) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}\}$ is not discrete, as $\lambda(i)$ is arbitrary, and there is no Fourier series in the direction of $i$.

**Example P6.2.31 (Betti numbers of projective space).** For $n \geq 1$,
$$b_k(\mathbb{P}^n) = 1 \text{ for } k = 0, 2, 4, \dots, 2n, \qquad b_k(\mathbb{P}^n) = 0 \text{ otherwise}.$$
The proof is by induction on $n$, with the black boxes from BT82, Chapter I: homotopy invariance of de Rham cohomology, the Mayer-Vietoris sequence, and $H^*_{dR}(S^m) = \mathbb{C}$ in degrees $0$ and $m$. For $n = 0$ the claim $b_0 = 1$ is clear. Let $p = [1 : 0 : \dots : 0]$, $U = \mathbb{P}^n \setminus \{p\}$ and $W = U_0 = \{Z_0 \neq 0\} \cong \mathbb{C}^n$. The map $h_t([Z]) = [t Z_0 : Z_1 : \dots : Z_n]$, $t \in [0, 1]$, is a smooth homotopy on $U$ (well defined since $(Z_1, \dots, Z_n) \neq 0$ on $U$) from the retraction onto the hyperplane $H = \{Z_0 = 0\} \cong \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ to the identity, fixing $H$; so $H^k_{dR}(U) \cong H^k_{dR}(\mathbb{P}^{n-1})$. The set $W$ is contractible and $U \cap W \cong \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$ retracts onto $S^{2n-1}$. All three are connected. The Mayer-Vietoris sequence reads
$$\cdots \to H^{k-1}(U \cap W) \to H^k(\mathbb{P}^n) \to H^k(U) \oplus H^k(W) \to H^k(U \cap W) \to \cdots.$$
For $1 \leq k \leq 2n - 2$ both $H^{k-1}(U \cap W)$ and $H^k(U \cap W)$ vanish, except that $H^0(U \cap W) = \mathbb{C}$ for $k = 1$; but $H^0(U) \oplus H^0(W) \to H^0(U \cap W)$, $(a, b) \mapsto a - b$ on constants, is onto, so the map $H^0(U \cap W) \to H^1(\mathbb{P}^n)$ is zero. Hence $H^k(\mathbb{P}^n) \cong H^k(\mathbb{P}^{n-1})$ for $1 \leq k \leq 2n - 2$. The groups $H^{2n-1}(U) \cong H^{2n-1}(\mathbb{P}^{n-1})$ and $H^{2n}(U)$ vanish because $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ has real dimension $2n - 2$, so the sequence ends with
$$H^{2n-2}(U) \oplus H^{2n-2}(W) \to H^{2n-2}(U \cap W) \to H^{2n-1}(\mathbb{P}^n) \to 0 \to H^{2n-1}(U \cap W) \to H^{2n}(\mathbb{P}^n) \to 0.$$
The first map is onto: for $n \geq 2$ its target is $H^{2n-2}(S^{2n-1}) = 0$, and for $n = 1$ it is the surjection on $H^0$ above. So $H^{2n-1}(\mathbb{P}^n) = 0$ and $H^{2n}(\mathbb{P}^n) \cong H^{2n-1}(S^{2n-1}) = \mathbb{C}$, which completes the induction. By Proposition P6.2.26, $\sum_{p+q=2k} h^{p,q}(\mathbb{P}^n) \geq 1$ for $0 \leq k \leq n$; Chapter 4 shows $h^{p,q}(\mathbb{P}^n) = 1$ for $p = q \leq n$ and $0$ otherwise.

### Proof

Proof of the claims in Definition P6.2.22. If $e'_b = \sum_a h_{ab} e_a$ is another holomorphic frame, then $\sum_b \alpha'_b \otimes e'_b = \sum_a (\sum_b h_{ab} \alpha'_b) \otimes e_a$ and $\bar\partial(h_{ab} \alpha'_b) = h_{ab} \bar\partial \alpha'_b$ since $h_{ab}$ is holomorphic, so both frames give the same $\bar\partial_E$. The identities $\bar\partial_E^2 = 0$ and the Leibniz rule follow from those for $\bar\partial$ (Proposition P6.2.10) in a frame, and the kernel on $(p, 0)$-forms consists of the forms $\sum_a \alpha_a \otimes e_a$ with holomorphic $\alpha_a \in \Omega^p$ by part 4 of Proposition P6.2.10. The equality $H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C}) = H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{R}) \otimes \mathbb{C}$ holds because $d$ commutes with taking real and imaginary parts.

Proof of Lemma P6.2.23. The black box is the existence of smooth partitions of unity (BT82, Chapter I): for every open cover $(U_k)_{k \in K}$ of $M$ there are smooth functions $\rho_k \geq 0$ with $\operatorname{supp} \rho_k \subseteq U_k$, locally finite supports, and $\sum_k \rho_k = 1$.

Step 1: if $0 \to \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{G} \to \mathcal{H} \to 0$ is an exact sequence of sheaves of $C^\infty_M$-modules, then $\mathcal{G}(M) \to \mathcal{H}(M)$ is onto. Let $\sigma \in \mathcal{H}(M)$. Since $\mathcal{G} \to \mathcal{H}$ is onto as a map of sheaves, there are an open cover $(U_k)$ and $t_k \in \mathcal{G}(U_k)$ mapping to $\sigma|_{U_k}$; choose a partition of unity $(\rho_k)$ subordinate to it. Then $f_{kl} = t_k - t_l \in \mathcal{F}(U_k \cap U_l)$, and $f_{kl} + f_{lm} = f_{km}$ on triple intersections. For $f \in \mathcal{F}(U_k \cap U_l)$ the section $\rho_l f$ extends by zero to a section of $\mathcal{F}$ over $U_k$: it is $\rho_l f$ on $U_k \cap U_l$ and $0$ on $U_k \setminus \operatorname{supp} \rho_l$, these two open sets cover $U_k$ because $\operatorname{supp} \rho_l \subseteq U_l$, and the two sections agree on the overlap. Put $g_k = \sum_l \rho_l f_{kl} \in \mathcal{F}(U_k)$, a sum that is finite near each point by local finiteness and so defines a section by gluing. On $U_k \cap U_m$,
$$g_k - g_m = \sum_l \rho_l (f_{kl} - f_{ml}) = \sum_l \rho_l f_{km} = f_{km},$$
where each term is computed on $U_k \cap U_m \cap U_l$ and is $0$ off $\operatorname{supp} \rho_l$. So the sections $t_k - g_k \in \mathcal{G}(U_k)$ agree on overlaps, $(t_k - g_k) - (t_m - g_m) = f_{km} - f_{km} = 0$, and glue to $t \in \mathcal{G}(M)$, which maps to $\sigma$ because each $g_k$ maps to $0$ in $\mathcal{H}$.

Step 2. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a sheaf of $C^\infty_M$-modules. By Proposition P2.7.12, applied to the ringed space $(M, C^\infty_M)$, the Godement sheaf $\mathcal{C}^0(\mathcal{F})$ is a flasque sheaf of $C^\infty_M$-modules containing $\mathcal{F}$, so $\mathcal{Q} = \mathcal{C}^0(\mathcal{F})/\mathcal{F}$ is also a sheaf of $C^\infty_M$-modules. By part 2 of Theorem P2.7.11, $H^i(M, \mathcal{C}^0(\mathcal{F})) = 0$ for $i > 0$, and the long exact sequence of Theorem P2.7.3 gives
$$\mathcal{C}^0(\mathcal{F})(M) \to \mathcal{Q}(M) \to H^1(M, \mathcal{F}) \to 0, \qquad H^i(M, \mathcal{Q}) \cong H^{i+1}(M, \mathcal{F}) \text{ for } i \geq 1.$$
The first map is onto by Step 1, so $H^1(M, \mathcal{F}) = 0$ for every sheaf of $C^\infty_M$-modules $\mathcal{F}$. If $H^i$ vanishes for all such sheaves, then $H^{i+1}(M, \mathcal{F}) \cong H^i(M, \mathcal{Q}) = 0$; by induction $H^i(M, \mathcal{F}) = 0$ for all $i > 0$. By part 4 of Theorem P2.7.11 it does not matter whether cohomology is computed for sheaves of abelian groups or of modules. Finally a sheaf of $\mathcal{A}_X$-modules is a sheaf of $C^\infty_X$-modules through the inclusion of real valued functions into complex valued ones, and $\mathcal{A}^{p,q}_X(E)$ is a module over $\mathcal{A}_X$ by multiplication of forms by functions.

Proof of Theorem P6.2.24. The sequence of sheaves
$$0 \to \Omega^p_X \otimes E \to \mathcal{A}^{p,0}_X(E) \xrightarrow{\bar\partial_E} \mathcal{A}^{p,1}_X(E) \xrightarrow{\bar\partial_E} \cdots \xrightarrow{\bar\partial_E} \mathcal{A}^{p,n}_X(E) \to 0$$
is exact: exactness is local, and on an open set $U$ with a holomorphic frame of $E$ of rank $r$ it is the direct sum of $r$ copies of the Dolbeault resolution $0 \to \Omega^p_U \to \mathcal{A}^{p,0}_U \to \cdots \to \mathcal{A}^{p,n}_U \to 0$, which is exact by Corollary P6.1.37. By Lemma P6.2.23 it is a resolution by $\Gamma(X, -)$-acyclic sheaves, so by Theorem P2.3.25 the cohomology of its complex of global sections, which is $H^{p,q}(X, E)$ by definition, is $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X \otimes E)$. The resolution has length $n$, which gives the vanishing for $q > n$.

Proof of Theorem P6.2.25. The sequence $0 \to \mathbb{C}_M \to \mathcal{A}^0_M \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{A}^1_M \xrightarrow{d} \cdots \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{A}^m_M \to 0$ is exact. At $\mathcal{A}^0_M$ the kernel of $d$ consists of the functions with vanishing differential, which are locally constant, that is, sections of $\mathbb{C}_M$. At $\mathcal{A}^k_M$, $k \geq 1$, a closed form near a point $x$ restricts to a closed form on a coordinate ball around $x$, which is star shaped, so it is exact there by the Poincaré lemma (Theorem P6.1.34). By Lemma P6.2.23 the sheaves $\mathcal{A}^k_M$ are acyclic, and Theorem P2.3.25 gives $H^k(M, \mathbb{C}_M) \cong H^k_{dR}(M, \mathbb{C})$. The same argument with real forms and $\mathbb{R}_M$ gives the real statement, and the resolution has length $m$.

Proof of Proposition P6.2.26. Consider the double complex of sheaves $\mathcal{C}^{p,q} = \mathcal{A}^{p,q}_X$, $0 \leq p, q \leq n$, with $d_h = \partial$ and $d_v = \bar\partial$. By Proposition P6.2.10, $\partial^2 = \bar\partial^2 = 0$ and $\partial\bar\partial + \bar\partial\partial = 0$, which are the conditions of Definition P2.4.13, and the total complex has terms $\bigoplus_{p+q=k} \mathcal{A}^{p,q}_X = \mathcal{A}^k_X$ and differential $\partial + \bar\partial = d$. By Corollary P6.1.37 the columns $(\mathcal{A}^{p,\bullet}_X, \bar\partial)$ are exact in positive degrees, with $\ker(\bar\partial \colon \mathcal{A}^{p,0}_X \to \mathcal{A}^{p,1}_X) = \Omega^p_X$. By Corollary P2.4.16, with rows and columns exchanged, in the abelian category of sheaves of abelian groups on $X$, the inclusion $(\Omega^\bullet_X, \partial) \to (\mathcal{A}^\bullet_X, d)$ is a quasi-isomorphism. By the proof of Theorem P6.2.25 the cohomology sheaves of $(\mathcal{A}^\bullet_X, d)$ are $\mathbb{C}_X$ in degree $0$ and zero otherwise, and the kernel of $\partial$ on $\mathcal{O}_X$ is the sheaf of locally constant functions. This is part 1, and $\partial = d$ on holomorphic forms by part 4 of Proposition P6.2.10. For part 2, the global sections $A^{p,q}(X)$ form a first quadrant double complex with the same differentials and total complex $(A^\bullet(X), d)$. Theorem P2.4.15 gives a convergent spectral sequence with ${}^{I}E_1^{p,q} = H_v^q(A^{p,\bullet}(X)) = H^{p,q}(X)$, $d_1$ induced by $d_h = \partial$, and abutment $H^{p+q}(A^\bullet(X), d) = H^{p+q}_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C})$. Theorems P6.2.24 and P6.2.25 identify $E_1$ and the abutment with sheaf cohomology. For part 3, convergence means that $H^k_{dR}(X, \mathbb{C})$ has a finite filtration whose graded pieces are the $E_\infty^{p,q}$, $p + q = k$, and $E_\infty^{p,q}$ is a subquotient of $E_1^{p,q}$, since each $E_{r+1}$ is the cohomology of $(E_r, d_r)$ and $E_r = E_\infty$ for $r > n$, the differential $d_r$ mapping $E_r^{p,q}$ to $E_r^{p+r, q-r+1}$, which is zero when $r > n$ because $p \leq n$. So $b_k = \sum_{p+q=k} \dim E_\infty^{p,q} \leq \sum_{p+q=k} h^{p,q}$. If all Hodge numbers are finite, each $E_r$ is a finite dimensional complex graded by total degree $p + q$, with differential of total degree $+1$, so its Euler characteristic $\sum (-1)^{p+q} \dim E_r^{p,q}$ equals that of its cohomology $E_{r+1}$; for $r = 1$ it is $\sum (-1)^{p+q} h^{p,q}$, and for $r > n$ it is $\sum_k (-1)^k b_k$.

Proof of Theorem P6.2.29. Fix an $\mathbb{R}$-basis $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_{2n}$ of $V$ generating $\Lambda$, so that $t \mapsto \sum t_k \lambda_k$ identifies $X$ with $T = \mathbb{R}^{2n}/\mathbb{Z}^{2n}$, and use the Lebesgue measure $dt$ of total mass $1$ on $T$. The *dual lattice* $\Lambda^* = \{\lambda \in \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(V, \mathbb{R}) : \lambda(\Lambda) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}\}$ corresponds to $\mathbb{Z}^{2n}$ by $\lambda \mapsto (\lambda(\lambda_k))_k$, and $e_\lambda = e^{2\pi i \lambda}$ becomes $e^{2\pi i m \cdot t}$. Fix norms. The following three facts of Fourier analysis on $T$ are standard. (F1) For smooth $f$ the coefficients $c_\lambda(f) = \int_T f \, \bar e_\lambda \, dt$ decrease rapidly: integration by parts, with no boundary terms by periodicity, gives $c_m(\partial f/\partial t_k) = 2\pi i m_k c_m(f)$, hence $|m_k|^N |c_m(f)| \leq (2\pi)^{-N} \sup |\partial^N f/\partial t_k^N|$. (F2) If $(c_\lambda)$ decreases rapidly, $\sum_\lambda c_\lambda e_\lambda$ converges uniformly with all derivatives, because derivatives of $e_\lambda$ are polynomials in $\lambda$ times $e_\lambda$ and $\sum_m (1 + |m|)^{-2n-1} < \infty$; the sum $g$ is smooth with $c_\lambda(g) = c_\lambda$, by orthonormality $\int_T e_\lambda \bar e_\mu \, dt = \delta_{\lambda\mu}$ and uniform convergence. (F3) A continuous $f$ with all $c_\lambda(f) = 0$ is zero. For this the black box is the Stone-Weierstrass theorem of real analysis: the trigonometric polynomials form an algebra of continuous functions on the compact space $T$ that contains the constants, is closed under conjugation and separates points, so it is dense in the continuous functions for the supremum norm. Taking trigonometric polynomials $P_j \to \bar f$ uniformly gives $\int_T |f|^2 \, dt = \lim_j \int_T f P_j \, dt = 0$. So every smooth function is the sum of its Fourier series in the $C^\infty$ sense.

Expand a smooth form $\alpha = \sum_{I,J} \alpha_{IJ} \, dz_I \wedge d\bar z_J$ coefficientwise: $\alpha = \sum_{\lambda \in \Lambda^*} e_\lambda \alpha_\lambda$ with $\alpha_\lambda$ invariant forms decreasing rapidly in $\lambda$, and by (F1) to (F3) every rapidly decreasing family of invariant forms defines a smooth form in this way, with uniqueness. Write $\lambda = \ell + \bar\ell$ with $\ell(z) = \sum_j a_j z_j$ complex linear, which is possible for every real linear form. Then
$$\bar\partial e_\lambda = e_\lambda \theta_\lambda, \quad \theta_\lambda = 2\pi i \sum_j \bar a_j \, d\bar z_j, \qquad d e_\lambda = e_\lambda \eta_\lambda, \quad \eta_\lambda = 2\pi i \, d\lambda,$$
and $\theta_\lambda$, $\eta_\lambda$ are invariant forms that vanish only for $\lambda = 0$. Since $\lambda \mapsto \theta_\lambda$ and $\lambda \mapsto \eta_\lambda$ are injective and real linear on the finite dimensional space $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(V, \mathbb{R})$, there is $c > 0$ with $|\theta_\lambda| \geq c |\lambda|$ and $|\eta_\lambda| \geq c|\lambda|$. Invariant forms have constant coefficients, so they are closed for $d$, $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$, and differentiating the Fourier series termwise, which (F2) allows, gives
$$\bar\partial \alpha = \sum_\lambda e_\lambda \, \theta_\lambda \wedge \alpha_\lambda, \qquad d\alpha = \sum_\lambda e_\lambda \, \eta_\lambda \wedge \alpha_\lambda.$$
For $\lambda \neq 0$ with $\theta_\lambda = \sum_j b_j \, d\bar z_j$, let $v_\lambda = \sum_j \bar b_j \, \partial/\partial \bar z_j / \sum_j |b_j|^2$, a constant vector field of type $(0,1)$ with $\theta_\lambda(v_\lambda) = 1$ and $|v_\lambda| \leq C/|\lambda|$. Contraction $\iota_v$ with a vector of type $(0,1)$ maps invariant $(p,q)$-forms to invariant $(p, q-1)$-forms, and since it is an antiderivation, $\iota_v(\theta \wedge \beta) = \theta(v) \beta - \theta \wedge \iota_v \beta$; so $\theta_\lambda \wedge \iota_{v_\lambda} + \iota_{v_\lambda}(\theta_\lambda \wedge \cdot) = 1$. Now let $\alpha$ be a $\bar\partial$-closed $(p,q)$-form, so $\theta_\lambda \wedge \alpha_\lambda = 0$ for all $\lambda$, and put
$$\beta = \sum_{\lambda \neq 0} e_\lambda \, \iota_{v_\lambda} \alpha_\lambda.$$
Its coefficients are bounded by $C |\alpha_\lambda|/|\lambda|$, so they decrease rapidly and $\beta$ is a smooth $(p, q-1)$-form, and
$$\bar\partial \beta = \sum_{\lambda \neq 0} e_\lambda \, \theta_\lambda \wedge \iota_{v_\lambda} \alpha_\lambda = \sum_{\lambda \neq 0} e_\lambda \big( \alpha_\lambda - \iota_{v_\lambda}(\theta_\lambda \wedge \alpha_\lambda) \big) = \alpha - \alpha_0.$$
So the class of $\alpha$ contains the invariant form $\alpha_0$. If an invariant form $\alpha_0$ is $\bar\partial \gamma$, comparing the coefficients of $e_0$ gives $\alpha_0 = \theta_0 \wedge \gamma_0 = 0$. So the invariant $(p,q)$-forms map isomorphically onto $H^{p,q}(X)$; they have the basis $dz_I \wedge d\bar z_J$, which gives $h^{p,q} = \binom{n}{p}\binom{n}{q}$, and Theorem P6.2.24 gives the statement about $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X)$. This is part 2. Part 1 is the same argument with $d$, $\eta_\lambda$ and the complex vector $v_\lambda = w_\lambda/(2\pi i \, \lambda(w_\lambda))$, where $w_\lambda \in V$ is a real vector with $\lambda(w_\lambda) = |\lambda|^2$ for a Euclidean structure; the invariant $k$-forms have the basis $dx_I \wedge dy_J$ with $|I| + |J| = k$, of size $\binom{2n}{k}$. For part 3, an invariant $k$-form is uniquely a sum of invariant forms of types $(p,q)$ with $p + q = k$, each of which is $d$-closed, so part 1 gives $H^k_{dR} = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} \{\text{classes of invariant }(p,q)\text{-forms}\}$, and by part 2 the summand is isomorphic to $H^{p,q}(X)$. Then $b_k = \binom{2n}{k} = \sum_{p+q=k} \binom{n}{p} \binom{n}{q} = \sum_{p+q=k} h^{p,q}$, and by part 3 of Proposition P6.2.26 and its proof every $E_\infty^{p,q}$ equals $E_1^{p,q}$, so all differentials $d_r$, $r \geq 1$, vanish.

### Lean

Mathlib has differential forms on normed spaces, smooth manifolds, sheaf cohomology as a derived functor in a general form, and Fourier series on the additive circle with their completeness in $L^2$. It has no de Rham cohomology of manifolds, no Dolbeault cohomology, no partitions of unity argument for acyclicity, and no Frölicher spectral sequence. The handbook adds no `MinimalModel` node here.

### Notes and history

De Rham proved in 1931 that de Rham cohomology computes the real cohomology of a compact manifold, and Weil gave in 1952 the proof by sheaves and covers that underlies Theorem P6.2.25. Dolbeault proved Theorem P6.2.24 in 1953 (Dol53), using the then new sheaf cohomology of Leray and Cartan, and the acyclicity of fine sheaves is from the Cartan seminar (Wel80, Section II.3). Frölicher introduced the spectral sequence of Proposition P6.2.26 in 1955. It is also the first hypercohomology spectral sequence of Theorem P2.4.24 for the holomorphic de Rham complex, computed with the acyclic resolution by the double complex $\mathcal{A}^{\bullet,\bullet}_X$ (Voi02, Chapter 8); the text does not need this identification. The computation of the Dolbeault cohomology of tori by Fourier series is classical, see GH78, Section 0.3, and Huy05, Section 2.6. Example P6.2.28 is the standard example of a manifold with infinite dimensional Dolbeault cohomology, and it explains why the finiteness theorems of Chapter 4 need compactness.
