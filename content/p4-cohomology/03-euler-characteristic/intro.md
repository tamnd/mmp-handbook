# Euler characteristic and Hilbert polynomial

The individual cohomology groups of a coherent sheaf on a projective scheme jump in families and are hard to compute. Their alternating sum, the Euler characteristic, is additive on exact sequences and is the first invariant that the cohomology of Chapter 2 makes computable in general. This chapter proves that the Euler characteristics of the twists $\mathcal{F}(n)$ of a coherent sheaf form a polynomial in $n$, the Hilbert polynomial, and reads the dimension, the degree and the arithmetic genus off it.

Section 3.1 defines the Euler characteristic of a coherent sheaf on a projective scheme over a field, proves its additivity and its invariance under extension of the base field, and proves that $n \mapsto \chi(\mathcal{F}(n))$ is a polynomial of degree $\dim \operatorname{Supp} \mathcal{F}$, equal to $h^0(\mathcal{F}(n))$ for large $n$ and to the Hilbert polynomial of P3 Ch. 13 for the structure sheaf. The proof cuts by a hyperplane in general position, as in Section 2.3. Section 3.2 defines the degree and the arithmetic genus, proves that cutting by a hypersurface of degree $e$ multiplies the degree by $e$, and deduces Bezout's theorem for plane curves. Section 3.3 proves the Riemann-Roch theorem on a normal projective curve in its first form, $\chi(\mathcal{O}_C(D)) = \deg D + 1 - p_a(C)$, with the consequences that need no duality: principal divisors have degree $0$, divisors of negative degree have no sections, and a divisor of large degree moves.

In the minimal model program the Euler characteristic is the bridge between cohomology and intersection numbers. Asymptotic Riemann-Roch writes $\chi(\mathcal{O}_X(mD))$ as a polynomial in $m$ whose leading coefficient is the self-intersection $D^n/n!$. It is how bigness and the volume of a divisor are computed, and it is the form in which the Riemann-Roch theorem enters the non-vanishing and base point free theorems behind the cone theorem. The curve case of Section 3.3 is the base of all of it.

The chapter follows Hartshorne, Sections III.5 and IV.1, Vakil, Chapters 18 and 19, and Görtz-Wedhorn II, with details from the Stacks project. It uses Chapter 2, the Hilbert polynomial of P3 Ch. 13, the divisors of P3 Ch. 8 and Ch. 9, and the associated primes of P1 Ch. 2.
