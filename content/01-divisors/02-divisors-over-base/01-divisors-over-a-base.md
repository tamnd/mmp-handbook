## Divisors and linear equivalence over a base

### Statement

In this chapter $k$ is an algebraically closed field of characteristic 0, and a variety is an integral separated scheme of finite type over $k$. For an abelian group $A$ and a field $K = \mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{R}$, write $A_K = A \otimes_\mathbb{Z} K$. Since $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ are flat over $\mathbb{Z}$, an injective homomorphism $A \to B$ gives injective maps $A_\mathbb{Q} \to B_\mathbb{Q}$ and $A_\mathbb{R} \to B_\mathbb{R}$, and $A_\mathbb{R} = A_\mathbb{Q} \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}$.

Let $X$ be a normal variety. Write $\operatorname{Div}(X)$ for the group of Weil divisors, $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)$ for the group of Cartier divisors and $\operatorname{Pic}(X)$ for the Picard group. By Proposition P3.9.9 the map $D \mapsto [D]$ from Cartier divisors to Weil divisors is injective, so $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Div}(X)$, and $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)_K$ is a subspace of $\operatorname{Div}(X)_K$.

**Definition 1.2.1 ($\mathbb{Q}$- and $\mathbb{R}$-divisors).** Let $X$ be a normal variety. A *$\mathbb{Q}$-divisor* is an element of $\operatorname{Div}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$ and an *$\mathbb{R}$-divisor* is an element of $\operatorname{Div}(X)_\mathbb{R}$, that is, a finite sum $\sum d_i D_i$ of prime divisors with rational or real coefficients. An $\mathbb{R}$-divisor is *$\mathbb{R}$-Cartier* if it lies in the subspace $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)_\mathbb{R}$, that is, if it is a sum $\sum_j r_j H_j$ with $r_j \in \mathbb{R}$ and $H_j$ Cartier. A $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor $D$ is *$\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier* if $mD$ is a Cartier divisor for some integer $m \ge 1$, that is, if $D$ lies in $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$. The isomorphism $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)/\{\text{principal divisors}\} \cong \operatorname{Pic}(X)$ of Theorem P3.9.4, tensored with $\mathbb{R}$, gives the *class map* $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)_\mathbb{R} \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R}$, $D \mapsto [D]$, which sends a Cartier divisor $H$ to $\mathcal{O}_X(H) \otimes 1$.

These are the notions of Definition 1.1.6 and Definition P3.9.18. By the injectivity above, an $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier divisor determines its element of $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)_\mathbb{R}$, so its class $[D]$ is well defined. An element of $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R}$ is a finite sum $\sum r_i L_i$ of line bundles with real coefficients, and $\sum r_i L_i$ is written additively, so that $L + M$ means $L \otimes M$.

**Example 1.2.2 (smooth varieties).** Let $X$ be smooth. Then every Weil divisor is Cartier (Corollary P3.9.11), so every $\mathbb{R}$-divisor is $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier and every $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier. On $X = \mathbb{P}^2$ with a line $H$, the $\mathbb{R}$-divisor $\sqrt{2}\, H$ is $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier, and it is not a $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor, so the word $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier does not apply to it. Its class is $\sqrt{2} \, \mathcal{O}(1)$ in $\operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb{P}^2)_\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R} \, \mathcal{O}(1)$.

**Example 1.2.3 (two cones).** Let $S = \{xy = z^2\} \subset \mathbb{A}^3$, a normal surface, and $L = \{x = z = 0\}$, a line through the vertex. At the generic point of $L$ the function $y$ is a unit, and $x = z^2 y^{-1}$, so the maximal ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{S, L}$ is generated by $z$ and $\operatorname{ord}_L(x) = 2$. The function $x$ vanishes on $S$ only along $L$, so $\operatorname{div}(x) = 2L$, and $L$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier. It is not Cartier at the vertex $0$: its ideal $(x, z)$ in the local ring $\mathcal{O}_{S, 0}$ is not principal, since $x$ and $z$ have degree 1 for the grading of $k[x, y, z]/(xy - z^2)$ while $\mathfrak{m}(x, z)$ lies in degree at least 2, so the images of $x$ and $z$ in $(x, z)/\mathfrak{m}(x, z)$ are linearly independent. Let $X = \{xz = yw\} \subset \mathbb{A}^4$ and $D = \{x = y = 0\}$. By Counterexample 4.1.10, no multiple $mD$ with $m \ne 0$ is Cartier. So $D$ is not $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier, and Proposition 1.2.5 below shows that $cD$ is not $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier for any real $c \ne 0$.

**Lemma 1.2.4 (rational points of a rational subspace).** Let $W \subset V$ be vector spaces over $\mathbb{Q}$. Then $W \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R} \to V \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}$ is injective, and $(W \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}) \cap V = W$ inside $V \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}$.

*Proof.* Choose a basis $(w_\alpha)$ of $W$ and extend it by vectors $(v_\beta)$ to a basis of $V$. Then the vectors $w_\alpha \otimes 1$ and $v_\beta \otimes 1$ form a basis of $V \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}$, and $W \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}$ maps isomorphically onto the span of the $w_\alpha \otimes 1$. An element $x \in V$ is a finite combination of the basis vectors with rational coefficients, and it lies in the span of the $w_\alpha \otimes 1$ if and only if its coefficients at the $v_\beta$ vanish, that is, if and only if $x \in W$. $\square$

**Proposition 1.2.5 (rational and real Cartier divisors).** Let $X$ be a normal variety.

1. A $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor that is $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier.
2. The kernel of the class map $\operatorname{CaDiv}(X)_\mathbb{R} \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R}$ is the set of sums $\sum_i r_i \operatorname{div}(\varphi_i)$ with $r_i \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\varphi_i \in k(X)^*$, and the class map is surjective.

Lean: not yet in Lean.

*Proof.* For part 1 apply Lemma 1.2.4 to $W = \operatorname{CaDiv}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$ inside $V = \operatorname{Div}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$. Then $V \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R} = \operatorname{Div}(X)_\mathbb{R}$ and $W \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R} = \operatorname{CaDiv}(X)_\mathbb{R}$. A $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor $D$ that is $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier lies in $V \cap (W \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}) = W$. So $D = \frac1m H$ with $H$ Cartier and $m \ge 1$, and $mD = H$ is Cartier. For part 2, Theorem P3.9.4 gives an exact sequence $0 \to P \to \operatorname{CaDiv}(X) \to \operatorname{Pic}(X) \to 0$, where $P$ is the group of principal Cartier divisors $\operatorname{div}(\varphi)$. Tensoring with the flat module $\mathbb{R}$ keeps it exact, so the class map is surjective and its kernel is $P_\mathbb{R}$, the set of the sums $\sum_i r_i \operatorname{div}(\varphi_i)$. $\square$

By part 1, Example 1.2.3 is complete: if $cD = \sum_j r_j H_j$ with $c \ne 0$, then $D = \sum_j (r_j/c) H_j$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor that is $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier, so it would be $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier, which it is not.

Now let $f \colon X \to Z$ be a morphism of varieties. The pullback of line bundles is a homomorphism $f^* \colon \operatorname{Pic}(Z) \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)$, and it gives linear maps $f^* \colon \operatorname{Pic}(Z)_K \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)_K$.

**Definition 1.2.6 (linear equivalence over a base).** Let $f \colon X \to Z$ be a morphism of varieties with $X$ normal.

1. Two line bundles $L$ and $L'$ on $X$ are *linearly equivalent over $Z$*, written $L \sim_Z L'$, if $L \otimes L'^{-1} \cong f^* M$ for a line bundle $M$ on $Z$. Two Cartier divisors are linearly equivalent over $Z$ if their line bundles are.
2. Two elements $\lambda, \lambda'$ of $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R}$ are *$\mathbb{R}$-linearly equivalent over $Z$*, written $\lambda \sim_{\mathbb{R}, Z} \lambda'$, if $\lambda - \lambda'$ lies in the image of $f^* \colon \operatorname{Pic}(Z)_\mathbb{R} \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R}$. Two elements of $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$ are *$\mathbb{Q}$-linearly equivalent over $Z$*, written $\sim_{\mathbb{Q}, Z}$, if their difference lies in the image of $f^* \colon \operatorname{Pic}(Z)_\mathbb{Q} \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$.
3. Two $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier divisors $D$ and $D'$ are $\mathbb{R}$-linearly equivalent over $Z$ if $[D] \sim_{\mathbb{R}, Z} [D']$, and two $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisors are $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly equivalent over $Z$ if their classes in $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$ are.

When $Z$ is a point, $\operatorname{Pic}(Z) = 0$, and the subscript $Z$ is dropped: $D \sim_\mathbb{R} D'$ means $[D - D'] = 0$, which by Proposition 1.2.5 means $D - D' = \sum_i r_i \operatorname{div}(\varphi_i)$. In the same way $D \sim_\mathbb{Q} D'$ means that $m(D - D')$ is principal for some $m \ge 1$, because the kernel of $\operatorname{Pic}(X) \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$ is the torsion subgroup. If $B$ is a Cartier divisor on $Z$ whose support does not contain $f(X)$, then $f^*B$ is defined and $\mathcal{O}_X(f^* B) \cong f^* \mathcal{O}_Z(B)$ (Proposition P3.9.27), so $f^* B \sim_Z 0$. A divisor over $Z$ may be changed by pullbacks from $Z$: this is the freedom that the program over $Z$ needs, since a contraction $X \to Z$ cannot see line bundles that come from $Z$.

**Example 1.2.7 (projective space).** Let $X = \mathbb{P}^n$. The degree gives $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{P}^n) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ (Theorem P3.8.20), and every Weil divisor is Cartier, so $\operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb{P}^n)_\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}$ by the degree. An $\mathbb{R}$-divisor $\sum a_i D_i$ is $\sim_\mathbb{R} 0$ if and only if $\sum a_i \deg D_i = 0$. For example $\sqrt 2 \, (H_1 - H_2) \sim_\mathbb{R} 0$ for two hyperplanes, and $D_2 - 2H \sim 0$ for a conic $D_2$. Over $Z = \mathbb{P}^n$ with $f$ the identity, $f^*$ is surjective and every $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier divisor is $\sim_{\mathbb{R}, Z} 0$.

**Example 1.2.8 (a torsion point on an elliptic curve).** Let $E$ be an elliptic curve with origin $O$ and let $P \ne O$ be a point with $2P = O$ in the group law. By Proposition P5.1.13 the map $Q \mapsto [Q - O]$ is a bijection onto $\operatorname{Pic}^0(E)$ that respects the group law, so $2(P - O) \sim 0$ while $P - O \not\sim 0$. So $P \sim_\mathbb{Q} O$ and $P \not\sim O$. In $\operatorname{Pic}(E)_\mathbb{R}$ the class of $P - O$ is 0, so also $c(P - O) \sim_\mathbb{R} 0$ for every real $c$.

**Example 1.2.9 (a product over a factor).** Let $X = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ and $f = p_2 \colon X \to Z = \mathbb{P}^1$. By Corollary P3.9.11, $\operatorname{Pic}(X) = \mathbb{Z} F_1 \oplus \mathbb{Z} F_2$, where $F_1 = \{pt\} \times \mathbb{P}^1$ and $F_2 = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \{pt\}$ (Example P5.12.8), and $f^* \mathcal{O}(1) = \mathcal{O}(F_2)$. So $aF_1 + bF_2 \sim_Z a F_1$, and two divisors are $\mathbb{R}$-linearly equivalent over $Z$ if and only if they have the same coefficient at $F_1$, which is the degree on a fibre $F_2$ of $f$. The quotient $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R} / f^* \operatorname{Pic}(Z)_\mathbb{R}$ is the line $\mathbb{R} F_1$.

**Theorem 1.2.10 (real and rational equivalence over a base).** Let $f \colon X \to Z$ be a morphism of varieties with $X$ normal, and let $D$ and $D'$ be $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier $\mathbb{Q}$-divisors on $X$. If $D \sim_{\mathbb{R}, Z} D'$, then $D \sim_{\mathbb{Q}, Z} D'$. In particular, if $D \sim_\mathbb{R} D'$, then $m(D - D')$ is principal for some integer $m \ge 1$.

### Proof in words

A real linear equivalence is a solution of a system of linear equations with rational coefficients, in which the unknowns are the coefficients of the principal divisors and of the pullbacks. If a system with rational coefficients has a real solution, then it has a rational solution, and this is Lemma 1.2.4. The theorem applies the lemma to the image of $\operatorname{Pic}(Z)_\mathbb{Q}$ in $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$, and part 1 of Proposition 1.2.5 applies it to the Cartier divisors among the Weil divisors.

### Examples

**Example 1.2.11 (a real equivalence with irrational coefficients).** On $\mathbb{P}^1$ the divisor $D = \sqrt 2 \, [0] + (1 - \sqrt 2)[1] - [\infty]$ has degree 0, so $D \sim_\mathbb{R} 0$. Explicitly $D = \sqrt2 \operatorname{div}(x/(x-1)) + \operatorname{div}(x-1)$ in the coordinate $x$, where $\operatorname{div}(x - 1) = [1] - [\infty]$ and $\operatorname{div}(x/(x-1)) = [0] - [1]$. The rational divisor $[0] - [\infty]$ has the real expression $\sqrt2 \operatorname{div}(x/(x-1)) + \sqrt 2 \operatorname{div}(x - 1) + (1 - \sqrt2) \operatorname{div}(x)$, and the theorem replaces it by the rational one $\operatorname{div}(x)$.

**Example 1.2.12 (the torsion point over the curve itself).** In Example 1.2.8 let $f \colon E \to Z = \mathbb{P}^1$ be the morphism given by $|2O|$ (Proposition P5.1.13). Then $f^* \mathcal{O}(1) = \mathcal{O}_E(2O)$, and $P - O \sim_{\mathbb{Q}, Z} 0$ since its class is torsion. Also $O \sim_{\mathbb{Q}, Z} 0$, since $2O \sim f^*[\infty]$ for the point $\infty = f(O)$, so $\operatorname{Pic}(E)_\mathbb{Q}/f^* \operatorname{Pic}(Z)_\mathbb{Q}$ is the image of $\operatorname{Pic}^0(E)_\mathbb{Q}$. If $Q$ is a point such that $Q - O$ has infinite order in $\operatorname{Pic}^0(E)$, then $Q - O \not\sim_{\mathbb{Q}, Z} 0$: a relation $m(Q - O) \sim f^*(e[\infty])$ forces $e = 0$ by the degree, and then $m(Q - O) \sim 0$.

### Proof

Let $V = \operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$ and let $W \subset V$ be the image of $f^* \colon \operatorname{Pic}(Z)_\mathbb{Q} \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$. Then $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R} = V \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}$, and the image of $f^* \colon \operatorname{Pic}(Z)_\mathbb{R} \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R}$ is $W \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}$, embedded by Lemma 1.2.4. Since $D$ and $D'$ are $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier, their classes lie in $V$, and so does $x = [D] - [D']$. The hypothesis says $x \in W \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{R}$. By Lemma 1.2.4, $x \in W$, that is, $D \sim_{\mathbb{Q}, Z} D'$.

For the last claim take $Z$ a point, so $W = 0$ and $x = 0$ in $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$. Choose $m_0 \ge 1$ with $m_0(D - D')$ Cartier. The class of the line bundle $\mathcal{O}_X(m_0(D - D'))$ in $\operatorname{Pic}(X)$ maps to 0 in $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{Q}$, so it is torsion, and $\mathcal{O}_X(m m_0 (D - D')) \cong \mathcal{O}_X$ for some $m \ge 1$. By Theorem P3.9.4 the Cartier divisor $m m_0(D - D')$ is principal. $\square$

### Exercises

1. (A) Let $D = \sum a_i D_i$ be an $\mathbb{R}$-divisor on $\mathbb{P}^n$ with $D_i$ hypersurfaces of degrees $d_i$. Show that $D \sim_\mathbb{R} c H$ for a hyperplane $H$ exactly when $c = \sum a_i d_i$, and that $D \sim_\mathbb{Q} cH$ with $c \in \mathbb{Q}$ when all $a_i$ are rational.
2. (A) On the quadric cone $S$ of Example 1.2.3, show that $L \sim_\mathbb{Q} 0$ and that $\operatorname{div}(z) = L + L'$ for the line $L' = \{y = z = 0\}$. Deduce $L' \sim_\mathbb{Q} 0$ and $L \sim -L'$.
3. (A) Let $f \colon X \to Z$ be an isomorphism. Show that every $\mathbb{R}$-Cartier divisor on $X$ is $\sim_{\mathbb{R}, Z} 0$, and that every Cartier divisor is $\sim_Z 0$.
4. (B) On the elliptic curve of Example 1.2.8, let $P_1, P_2, P_3$ be the three points of order 2. Show that $P_1 + P_2 + P_3 - 3O \sim_\mathbb{Q} 0$ and decide whether it is $\sim 0$, using that $P_1 + P_2 + P_3 = O$ in the group law.
5. (B) Let $X = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ and $f$ the projection to the last two factors. Compute $\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb{R} / f^* \operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1)_\mathbb{R}$, and describe when two divisors $a_1 F_1 + a_2 F_2 + a_3 F_3$ are $\mathbb{R}$-linearly equivalent over the base. Here $F_i$ is the pullback of a point by the $i$-th projection.
6. (B) Let $D$ be a $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor on a normal variety and suppose $D = \sum_{i=1}^s r_i \operatorname{div}(\varphi_i)$ with real $r_i$. Show that there are rational $q_i$ with $D = \sum_{i=1}^s q_i \operatorname{div}(\varphi_i)$, with the same functions $\varphi_i$.

### Notes and history

$\mathbb{Q}$-divisors entered the minimal model program with the canonical divisor of a singular variety, which is often only $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier, and with boundaries with rational coefficients. $\mathbb{R}$-divisors became standard later, because the cones of divisors are real cones and their boundary points need real coefficients. KM98, Section 0.4, and Laz04, Section 1.3, give the definitions used here, and Deb01, Chapter 1, the same over a base. The rationality argument of Lemma 1.2.4 appears in many places, for example to show that a $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor that is $\mathbb{R}$-linearly trivial is $\mathbb{Q}$-linearly trivial.
