# Categories, functors and the Yoneda lemma

This volume gives the language in which the rest of the handbook is written. Algebraic geometry since Grothendieck states its results about maps more than about objects, and it constructs its objects by universal properties. The language for this is category theory. This chapter gives as much of it as the later volumes use, and no more.

Section 1.1 defines categories and functors and gives the examples that come back in every volume: sets, groups, modules over a ring, topological spaces, and the open sets of a space ordered by inclusion. Section 1.2 defines natural transformations and equivalences of categories and proves the Yoneda lemma. The lemma says that an object is determined, up to a unique isomorphism, by the maps into it. It is the reason a universal property defines an object. Section 1.3 is about adjoint functors, the most common source of universal properties. Section 1.4 defines limits and colimits, proves that a right adjoint preserves limits, and proves that filtered colimits of modules are exact.

The minimal model program uses this language in three places that a reader meets early. First, a scheme is determined by its functor of points (P3 Ch. 2), and this is the Yoneda lemma. The Hilbert scheme and the Hom scheme of P8 Ch. 1, which give the rational curves of bend-and-break and so the cone theorem of Volume 7, are defined as objects that represent a functor. Second, a sheaf is a functor on the category of open sets (Chapter 5), its stalk is a colimit, and the pairs $f^{-1} \dashv f_*$ of Chapter 5 and $f^* \dashv f_*$ of Chapter 6 are adjunctions. Third, every vanishing theorem of P7 and Volume 5, and every step of the proof of the base point free theorem in Volume 7, is a statement about derived functors, which Chapter 3 builds from the exactness properties that this chapter and Chapter 2 prove.

A word on size. Some categories, such as the category of all sets, have a class of objects that is not a set. The handbook does not discuss the foundations. A reader may fix a universe, or follow Mac Lane, Chapter I, and use classes. Every category in which a construction needs a set of objects, such as the index category of a limit, is small: its objects and its maps form sets.

The chapter follows Vakil, Chapter 1, with more detail from Mac Lane, Chapters I to V and IX. It assumes P1 Ch. 1: modules, exact sequences and the tensor product.
