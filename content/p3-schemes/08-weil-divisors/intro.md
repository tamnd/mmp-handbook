# Weil divisors

A rational function on a curve has finitely many zeros and poles, each with an order, and the formal sum of these points is its divisor. On a normal scheme the same construction works with the points replaced by prime divisors, the irreducible closed subsets of codimension one: the local ring along a prime divisor is a discrete valuation ring, and its valuation is the order of vanishing. The Weil divisors are the formal sums of prime divisors, the divisors of functions are the principal ones, and the class group is the quotient. It measures how far the scheme is from having unique factorization, and it is the first invariant that distinguishes the singular cones from the affine plane.

Section 8.1 defines prime divisors and the order of vanishing, proves that a rational function has finitely many zeros and poles, and proves that on a normal scheme a function without poles is regular. Section 8.2 defines Weil divisors and the class group, proves the exact sequence $1 \to \mathcal{O}(X)^* \to K^* \to \operatorname{Div}(X) \to \operatorname{Cl}(X) \to 0$, proves that a Noetherian domain is factorial if and only if it is normal with trivial class group, and proves the excision sequence and the invariance under $X \mapsto X \times \mathbb{A}^1$. Section 8.3 computes $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{P}^n) = \mathbb{Z}$ through the degree, the class groups of complements of hypersurfaces, of the cones $xy = z^n$, of the projective quadric cone and of the smooth quadric surface. Section 8.4 constructs the sheaf $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ of functions with poles bounded by $D$, proves that it depends only on the class of $D$, that it is invertible exactly where $D$ is locally principal, and identifies its sections with the complete linear system $|D|$.

In the minimal model program the varieties are normal, the canonical divisor $K_X$ is a Weil divisor, and the boundary of a pair is a $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor. A Weil divisor need not be Cartier: on the quadric cone the line $L$ is not locally principal at the vertex, while $2L$ is, and this is the model for the $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier condition of Chapter 9. The sheaves $\mathcal{O}_X(mK_X)$ define the canonical ring, and the insensitivity of $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ to closed sets of codimension two is the reason why flips preserve it.

The chapter follows Vakil, Chapter 14, Hartshorne, Section II.6, and Görtz-Wedhorn, Chapter 11. It uses Chapters 1 to 7 and the class groups and divisorial ideals of P1 Ch. 8 and Ch. 11.
