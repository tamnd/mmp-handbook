# Cartier divisors, line bundles and Q-Cartier divisors

A Weil divisor is a formal sum of subvarieties of codimension one. A Cartier divisor is a divisor given locally by one equation. On a smooth variety the two notions agree, but on a singular normal variety a Weil divisor need not have a local equation at a singular point, and the sheaf $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ is then not a line bundle. The Cartier divisors are exactly the divisors whose sheaves are line bundles, and their classes form the Picard group. A $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor is a Weil divisor with a Cartier multiple, and the least such multiple is its index.

Section 9.1 defines Cartier divisors by local equations, identifies the effective ones with the invertible ideal sheaves, constructs the line bundle $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ and proves that the Cartier class group of an integral scheme is its Picard group. Section 9.2 maps Cartier divisors to Weil divisors on a normal scheme, proves that the map is an isomorphism on a locally factorial scheme, computes $\operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb{P}^n)$, and shows on the cones $xy = z^n$ that $\operatorname{Pic}$ can be smaller than $\operatorname{Cl}$. Section 9.3 defines the $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier condition and the index, reduces both to the local class groups, and characterizes $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial schemes. Section 9.4 pulls back Cartier and $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisors along morphisms, compares the result with the pullback of line bundles, and proves the ramification formula on curves.

In the minimal model program the canonical divisor of a normal variety is a Weil divisor, and the program requires it to be $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier. Then $f^* K_Y$ is defined for a birational morphism $f$, the discrepancies are defined, and $K_X$ has rational intersection numbers with curves. The varieties of the program are $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, and the cone over a smooth quadric surface, which is not $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial, is the first example of what a flop has to avoid.

The chapter follows Vakil, Chapter 14, Hartshorne, Section II.6, and Görtz-Wedhorn, Chapter 11. It uses Chapters 1 to 8, P1 Ch. 8 and 9, and P2 Ch. 6 and 7.
