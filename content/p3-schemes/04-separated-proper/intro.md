# Separated and proper morphisms

A scheme can fail two properties that every space of classical geometry has. The affine line with a doubled origin has two copies of one point, so a sequence can tend to two limits. The affine line has no point at infinity, so a curve can run away to infinity. Separated morphisms exclude the first failure, and proper morphisms exclude both. In the analytic language they are the analogues of Hausdorff spaces and of compact spaces.

Section 4.1 defines the diagonal of a morphism and separated morphisms, proves that a morphism is separated when the image of its diagonal is closed, and gives a criterion with affine open sets: for a scheme over an affine base, the intersection of two affine open sets is affine and its ring is generated by the two rings. The doubled origin is not separated, and projective space is. Section 4.2 proves that separated morphisms are stable under composition and base change, that the graph of a morphism to a separated scheme is closed, and that two morphisms from a reduced scheme to a separated scheme that agree on a dense open set are equal. Section 4.3 defines universally closed and proper morphisms, proves that finite morphisms are proper, and proves that a reduced connected scheme that is proper over an algebraically closed field has only constant global functions. Section 4.4 proves that projective space is proper over every base, with the elimination argument, and proves the necessity of the valuative criteria: a separated morphism has at most one extension over a valuation ring, and a universally closed morphism has at least one.

In the minimal model program every contraction $\varphi \colon X \to Z$ is a proper morphism, and the cone theorem is stated for varieties that are projective over a base. The rigidity of Section 4.2 is used each time a rational map is shown to extend in at most one way, and the valuative criterion is the tool for proving that a family of varieties has a limit, as in the semistable reduction theorem of P8.

The chapter follows Vakil, Chapters 10 and 11, and Hartshorne, Section II.4, with Görtz-Wedhorn, Chapters 9 and 12. It uses Chapters 2 and 3 and the valuation rings and integral extensions of P1 Ch. 4 and Ch. 7.
