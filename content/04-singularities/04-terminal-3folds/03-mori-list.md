## Mori's list

### Statement

By Corollary 4.4.18, a terminal 3-fold point of index $r$ is the quotient of an isolated cDV point by $\mu_r$, acting freely outside the point. When the cover is smooth, the terminal lemma finishes the classification. When it is singular, the cover is a hypersurface in $\mathbb{C}^4$, and the action extends to $\mathbb{C}^4$. Mori's theorem lists the possible equations and weights. This section states the list with a reference, proves the formula for the index that the list uses, and works out the simplest cases.

**Definition 4.4.24 (hyperquotient).** Let $\mu_r$ act on $\mathbb{C}^4$, with coordinates $x, y, z, u$, by the weights $(b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4)$, as in Definition 4.4.2. Let $\varphi \in \mathbb{C}\{x, y, z, u\}$ be *semi-invariant* of weight $e$, that is $\varphi(\zeta \cdot p) = \zeta^e \varphi(p)$, and suppose that $\tilde X = \{\varphi = 0\}$ has an isolated singular point at the origin and that $\mu_r$ acts freely on $\tilde X \setminus 0$. The germ $X = \tilde X / \mu_r$ is a *hyperquotient*, written
$$X = \{\varphi = 0\} \subset \tfrac1r(b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4).$$

**Lemma 4.4.25 (the index of a hyperquotient).** In Definition 4.4.24, let $w = b_1 + b_2 + b_3 + b_4 - e$. Then $K_X$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier, and the index of $X$ at its point is the order of $w$ in $\mathbb{Z}/r$, that is $r / \gcd(r, w)$. In particular the index is $r$ if and only if $\gcd(w, r) = 1$.

**Theorem 4.4.26 (Mori's list).** Let $(X, P)$ be a terminal 3-fold germ of index $r \ge 2$. Then $(X, P)$ is analytically isomorphic to one of the following:

1. a cyclic quotient $\frac1r(a, -a, 1)$ with $\gcd(a, r) = 1$;
2. a hyperquotient $\{\varphi = 0\} \subset \frac1r(b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4)$ as in Definition 4.4.24, whose type, index, weights and equation are in the following table, with $\varphi$ semi-invariant of the given weight $e$ and $\{\varphi = 0\}$ an isolated singularity.

| type | $r$ | weights of $(x, y, z, u)$ | $\varphi$ | $e$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $cA/r$ | any $r \ge 2$ | $(a, -a, 0, 1)$, $\gcd(a, r) = 1$ | $xy + f(z, u^r)$ | $0$ |
| $cAx/4$ | $4$ | $(1, 3, 2, 1)$ | $x^2 + y^2 + f(z, u^2)$ | $2$ |
| $cAx/2$ | $2$ | $(1, 0, 1, 1)$ | $x^2 + y^2 + f(z, u)$, $f \in (z, u)^4$ | $0$ |
| $cD/2$ | $2$ | $(1, 1, 0, 1)$ | $u^2 + z^3 + xyz + f(x, y)$, or $u^2 + xyz + z^n + f(x, y)$ with $n \ge 4$, or $u^2 + y^2 z + z^n + f(x, y)$ with $n \ge 3$, where $f \in (x, y)^4$ | $0$ |
| $cD/3$ | $3$ | $(1, 2, 2, 0)$ | $u^2 + x^3 + y^3 + z^3$, or $u^2 + x^3 + y z^2 + f(x, y, z)$, or $u^2 + x^3 + y^3 + f(x, y, z)$, where $f \in (x, y, z)^4$ | $0$ |
| $cE/2$ | $2$ | $(0, 1, 1, 1)$ | $u^2 + x^3 + g(y, z) x + h(y, z)$, $g, h \in (y, z)^4$ | $0$ |

This is the main theorem of Mori (Mor85). Reid (Rei87, Section 6) explains the list and the proof, building on the general form of the terminal lemma in Rei87, Section 5. In each family the functions $f$, $g$, $h$ are only restricted by the conditions that $\varphi$ is semi-invariant of weight $e$ and that $\{\varphi = 0\}$ has an isolated singular point. The theorem is a list of candidates: it does not say that every member of each family is terminal. Mor85 and Rei87, Section 6, also treat that converse question, and this chapter does not use it. By Lemma 4.4.25 the index of every member of the table is $r$, as Exercise 2 checks.

**Theorem 4.4.27 (the general elephant).** Let $(X, P)$ be a terminal 3-fold germ. Then a general member $S$ of the linear system $|-K_X|$ near $P$ has a Du Val singularity at $P$.

This is Reid's theorem (Rei87, Section 6), proved there from Mori's list, case by case. The name comes from Reid's description of an anticanonical divisor as an "elephant".

### Proof in words

For the index, the canonical sheaf of the hypersurface $\{\varphi = 0\}$ is generated by the residue of $dx \wedge dy \wedge dz \wedge du / \varphi$, and $\mu_r$ multiplies this residue by $\zeta^w$. A power of the residue descends to a generator of $\mathcal{O}(mK_X)$ exactly when it is invariant, since the quotient map is étale outside the point.

For Mori's theorem, the index one cover is an isolated cDV point by Corollary 4.4.18, so it is a hypersurface $\{\varphi = 0\}$ in $\mathbb{C}^4$, and the action of $\mu_r$ lifts to $\mathbb{C}^4$ in semi-invariant coordinates. The terminal condition, tested on the toric divisors of the weights as in Section 1, gives a condition on the five numbers $b_1, \dots, b_4$ and $e$ of the same shape as Lemma 4.4.7, and Reid's general form of the terminal lemma forces the weights into a few patterns. Then the cDV type of $\varphi$, the semi-invariance and the free action force the equations of the table. The details are long, and they are in Mor85 and Rei87, Section 6.

### Examples

**Example 4.4.28 (the quotients inside the family $cA/r$).** Take $f(z, u^r) = z$ in the first row, so $\varphi = xy + z$, which is invariant since $x y$ and $z$ have weight 0. The hypersurface $\{xy + z = 0\}$ is smooth, and the projection $(x, y, z, u) \mapsto (x, y, u)$ is a $\mu_r$-equivariant isomorphism from it onto $\mathbb{C}^3$, with weights $(a, -a, 1)$. So the hyperquotient is $\frac1r(a, -a, 1)$, the first case of the theorem. Lemma 4.4.25 gives $w = a - a + 0 + 1 - 0 = 1$, so the index is $r$, as in Example 4.4.10.

**Example 4.4.29 (a point of type $cA/r$).** Let $\varphi = xy + z^2 + u^r$ with weights $(a, -a, 0, 1)$ and $\gcd(a, r) = 1$, so $f(z, u^r) = z^2 + u^r$. The partial derivatives $y$, $x$, $2z$ and $r u^{r-1}$ vanish together only at the origin, so $\tilde X = \{\varphi = 0\}$ has an isolated singular point. The section $u = 0$ is $\{xy + z^2 = 0\}$, so $\tilde X$ is of type $cA_1$. For $1 \le k \le r - 1$ the element $\zeta^k$ multiplies $x$, $y$ and $u$ by $\zeta^{ka}$, $\zeta^{-ka}$ and $\zeta^k$, all different from 1, and fixes $z$. So its fixed points in $\mathbb{C}^4$ form the $z$-axis, which meets $\tilde X$ where $z^2 = 0$, only at the origin. So $\mu_r$ acts freely on $\tilde X \setminus 0$, and the hyperquotient is defined. By Lemma 4.4.25, $w = 1$ and the index is $r$. So the point has the properties that Corollary 4.4.18 requires of a terminal point of index $r$: an isolated cDV cover, here of type $cA_1$, and a free action outside the point.

**Example 4.4.30 (the elephant of $\frac1r(1, -1, a)$).** Let $X = \frac1r(1, -1, a)$ with $\gcd(a, r) = 1$. The form $\omega = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz$ has weight $a$, so a section of $\mathcal{O}_X(-K_X)$ is $g \, \omega^{-1}$ with $g$ semi-invariant of weight $a$. The coordinate $z$ has weight $a$, and a general such $g$ is $c z + h$ with $c \ne 0$ and $h$ in the square of the maximal ideal, all of weight $a$. Then $z' = g$ is a semi-invariant coordinate of weight $a$, and the elephant $S = \{g = 0\}/\mu_r$ is the quotient of the plane $\{z' = 0\}$, with coordinates $x, y$, by $\mu_r$ with weights $(1, -1)$. This is $\frac1r(1, -1)$, the $A_{r-1}$ singularity, as in Example 4.3.21. So the general elephant is Du Val, as Theorem 4.4.27 says. For $\frac12(1,1,1)$ it is $A_1$.

**Counterexample 4.4.31 (an isolated cDV cover is not enough).** Let $\varphi = xy + zt$, with coordinates $x, y, z, t$, and let $\mu_2$ act by $-1$ on all four coordinates. Then $\varphi$ is invariant, $\tilde X = \{\varphi = 0\}$ is the ordinary double point, an isolated cDV point (Example 4.4.20), and $-1$ fixes only the origin, so the action on $\tilde X \setminus 0$ is free. By Lemma 4.4.25, $w = 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 - 0 \equiv 0 \pmod 2$, so $X = \tilde X / \mu_2$ has index 1. The coordinate ring of $\tilde X$ is $\bigoplus_k H^0(V, \mathcal{O}(k, k))$ with $V = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$, and $-1$ acts by $(-1)^k$ in degree $k$, so the invariant ring is $\bigoplus_k H^0(V, \mathcal{O}(2k, 2k))$, and $X$ is the cone over $V$ by $L = \mathcal{O}(2, 2)$. Since $K_V = \mathcal{O}(-2, -2) = -L$, Theorem 4.2.19 gives $a = -1 - (-1) = 0$. So $X$ is canonical of index 1 and not terminal. The necessary conditions of Corollary 4.4.18 hold, but the point is not terminal: the index one cover of $X$ is $X$ itself, not $\tilde X$, and $X$ is not cDV, by Theorem 4.4.17. The point is not on Mori's list, which is a list of points of index at least 2.

### Proof

*Proof of Lemma 4.4.25.* The hypersurface $\tilde X$ is normal, since it is Cohen-Macaulay with an isolated singular point, and its canonical sheaf is invertible, generated near 0 by the residue
$$\sigma = \operatorname{Res} \frac{dx \wedge dy \wedge dz \wedge du}{\varphi},$$
which equals $dx \wedge dy \wedge dz / (\partial \varphi / \partial u)$ where $\partial \varphi / \partial u \ne 0$, and similarly on the other charts. The element $\zeta$ multiplies $dx \wedge dy \wedge dz \wedge du$ by $\zeta^{b_1 + b_2 + b_3 + b_4}$ and $\varphi$ by $\zeta^e$, so it multiplies $\sigma$ by $\zeta^w$. The quotient map $\pi \colon \tilde X \to X$ is étale over $X \setminus P$, since the action is free outside the origin.

Suppose that $m w \equiv 0 \pmod r$. Then $\sigma^{\otimes m}$ is invariant, so it descends to a section of $\mathcal{O}_X(mK_X)$ over $X \setminus P$ that has no zero there, since $\pi$ is étale. So $\mathcal{O}_X(mK_X)$ and $\mathcal{O}_X$ agree outside the point $P$, which has codimension 3, and both are reflexive on the normal variety $X$, so $\mathcal{O}_X(mK_X) \cong \mathcal{O}_X$ and $mK_X$ is Cartier. In particular $r K_X$ is Cartier, and $K_X$ is $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier. Conversely, suppose that $\mathcal{O}_X(mK_X)$ has a generator $\tau$ near $P$. Then $\pi^* \tau$ is an invariant section of $\omega_{\tilde X}^{\otimes m}$ with no zero outside the origin, so $\pi^* \tau = g \, \sigma^{\otimes m}$ with $g$ a unit, since the origin has codimension 3 in the normal variety $\tilde X$. The function $g$ is semi-invariant of weight $-mw$, and $g(0) \ne 0$, so $g(0) = g(\zeta \cdot 0) = \zeta^{-mw} g(0)$ gives $m w \equiv 0$. So $mK_X$ is Cartier at $P$ if and only if $m w \equiv 0 \pmod r$, and the index is the order of $w$ in $\mathbb{Z}/r$. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 4.4.26.* This is Mor85, main theorem, and Rei87, Section 6. Only the start of the proof is in this chapter: by Corollary 4.4.18, the index one cover is an isolated cDV point, and if it is smooth the point is $\frac1r(1, -1, a)$, which after the change of generator $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{a'}$ with $a a' \equiv 1$ and a permutation of the coordinates is the form $\frac1r(a', -a', 1)$ of case 1. If it is singular, the cover is a hypersurface in $\mathbb{C}^4$ with embedding dimension 4, and the action of $\mu_r$ on the cotangent space $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ of the cover lifts to semi-invariant generators of the maximal ideal, which are coordinates $x, y, z, u$ of an ambient $\mathbb{C}^4$ on which $\mu_r$ acts diagonally. The equation of the cover can then be taken semi-invariant. The rest of the proof is the analysis of Mor85. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem 4.4.27.* This is Rei87, Section 6. The proof goes through the cases of Theorem 4.4.26. In the case of the cyclic quotients it is Example 4.4.30. $\square$

### Exercises

1. (A) Check the claims of Example 4.4.28: that the projection is an isomorphism onto $\mathbb{C}^3$, and that it is equivariant for the weights $(a, -a, 1)$.
2. (A) For each row of the table of Theorem 4.4.26, compute $w = b_1 + b_2 + b_3 + b_4 - e$ modulo $r$, and check that it is coprime to $r$. The values are $1$ for $cA/r$, $5 \equiv 1$ for $cAx/4$, $3 \equiv 1$ for $cAx/2$, $cD/2$ and $cE/2$, and $5 \equiv 2$ for $cD/3$.
3. (B) For the weights $(1, 1, 0, 1)$ of the row $cD/2$, show that the fixed points of $-1$ in $\mathbb{C}^4$ form the $z$-axis, and that it meets $\{\varphi = 0\}$ only at the origin for each of the three forms of $\varphi$. Show that $\varphi = u^2 + z^3 + xyz$ is semi-invariant of weight 0 but has non-isolated singularities along the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis, so that the term $f(x, y)$ is needed.
4. (B) Show that in Example 4.4.30 a general $g$ of weight $a$ has a nonzero coefficient of $z$, for every $r \ge 2$, by listing the monomials of weight $a$ of degree 1.
5. (B) In Counterexample 4.4.31, check that the invariant ring of $\mathbb{C}[x, y, z, t]/(xy + zt)$ under $-1$ is generated in degree 2, and that the blow-up of the vertex of $X$ is the total space of $\mathcal{O}_V(-2, -2)$, with exceptional divisor $V = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ and discrepancy 0.
6. (C) In Example 4.4.29, show that $u$ is a section of $\mathcal{O}_X(-K_X)$, and that the elephant $\{u = 0\}$ is the quotient of $\{xy + z^2 = 0\}$ by $\mu_r$ with weights $(a, -a, 0)$. Writing $x = s^2$, $y = -t^2$, $z = st$, show that this quotient is $\mathbb{C}^2 / G$ for a cyclic group $G \subset SL_2(\mathbb{C})$ of order $2r$, so that the elephant is the $A_{2r-1}$ singularity.

### Notes and history

Mori proved the classification of terminal 3-fold singularities in 1985 (Mor85), after Reid's reduction to the cDV case (Rei83b) and the terminal lemma for cyclic quotients (MS84). Reid's Young person's guide (Rei87, Sections 5 and 6) gives a readable account, with the general terminal lemma for hyperquotients and the general elephant theorem. The general elephant was the starting point of Reid's programme to study 3-folds through their anticanonical surfaces, and Kollár and Mori used it in the classification of 3-fold extremal neighbourhoods. KM98, Chapter 5, proves the cDV part of the theory and refers to Mori for the list. Later work determined the minimal discrepancy of each point of the list: a terminal 3-fold point of index $r$ has minimal discrepancy $\frac1r$, the value of Example 4.4.10 for the cyclic quotients.
