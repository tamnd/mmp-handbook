## The cone theorem and extremal contractions of surfaces

### Statement

Let $S$ be a smooth projective surface over the algebraically closed field $k$, with canonical divisor $K$ and the cones of Definition P5.12.1. Fix an ample divisor $H$ and the compact slice $\Sigma_H$ of Lemma P5.12.2. A *contraction* is a projective morphism $\varphi$ with $\varphi_* \mathcal{O} = \mathcal{O}$ (Definition P4.6.14), and the *contraction of an extremal ray* $R$ is a contraction $\varphi \colon S \to T$ onto a normal projective variety such that an integral curve $C$ is mapped to a point exactly when $[C] \in R$.

**Lemma P5.12.11 (curves of negative square).** Let $C$ be an integral curve with $C^2 < 0$. Then
$$\overline{NE}(S) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} [C] + \overline{NE}(S)_{C \geq 0},$$
the ray $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} [C]$ is extremal, and $C$ is the only integral curve whose class lies in it.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Lemma P5.12.12 (extremal rays and their square).** Let $R = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} x$ be an extremal ray of $\overline{NE}(S)$.

1. If $x^2 > 0$, then $\rho(S) = 1$. So if $\rho(S) \geq 2$, then $x^2 \leq 0$.
2. If $x^2 < 0$, then $R = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} [C]$ for a unique integral curve $C$, and $C^2 < 0$.
3. If $x^2 < 0$ and $K \cdot x < 0$, this curve is a $(-1)$-curve.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

The case $x^2 = 0$ is where curves must be produced from nothing, and there the only known proof uses Mori's deformation theory of rational curves. It is cited.

**Theorem P5.12.13 (Mori's theorem on extremal rays).** Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety of dimension $n$ over an algebraically closed field of any characteristic, and $R$ a $K_X$-negative extremal ray of $\overline{NE}(X)$. Then $R$ contains the class of a rational curve $C$ with $0 < -K_X \cdot C \leq n + 1$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P5.12.14 (the cone theorem for surfaces).** Let $\{R_i\}_{i \in I}$ be the set of $K$-negative extremal rays of $\overline{NE}(S)$.

1. Each $R_i$ is spanned by the class of a rational curve $C_i$ with $0 < -K \cdot C_i \leq 3$, and
$$\overline{NE}(S) = \overline{NE}(S)_{K \geq 0} + \sum_{i \in I} \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} [C_i].$$
2. For every $\varepsilon > 0$ only finitely many $i$ have $(K + \varepsilon H) \cdot C_i < 0$, and for these finitely many $i$,
$$\overline{NE}(S) = \overline{NE}(S)_{K + \varepsilon H \geq 0} + \sum_{(K + \varepsilon H) \cdot C_i < 0} \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} [C_i].$$
3. The rays $R_i$ accumulate only at $K^\perp$: a limit in $\Sigma_H$ of normalized generators of infinitely many distinct $R_i$ lies on $K^\perp$.
4. $K$ is nef if and only if $I$ is empty.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P5.12.15 (the $K$-negative extremal rays of a surface).** Let $R$ be a $K$-negative extremal ray of $\overline{NE}(S)$. Then $R = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C]$ for an integral curve $C \cong \mathbb{P}^1$, the contraction $\varphi_R$ of $R$ exists, and exactly one of the following holds.

1. ($C^2 < 0$.) $C$ is a $(-1)$-curve and the only integral curve with class in $R$. The contraction $\varphi_R \colon S \to S'$ is the blow-down of $C$ to a point of a smooth projective surface $S'$, and $\rho(S') = \rho(S) - 1$.
2. ($C^2 = 0$.) $\rho(S) = 2$, and $\varphi_R \colon S \to B$ is a morphism onto a smooth projective curve with $\varphi_{R*} \mathcal{O}_S = \mathcal{O}_B$, all of whose scheme-theoretic fibres are isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^1$, with $-K \cdot F = 2$ for a fibre $F$. The integral curves with class in $R$ are exactly the fibres.
3. ($C^2 > 0$.) $\rho(S) = 1$, $-K$ is ample, $S \cong \mathbb{P}^2$, and $\varphi_R$ is the map to a point. Here $C$ may be taken to be a line, with $-K \cdot C = 3$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

### Proof in words

A curve of negative square meets every other curve nonnegatively, so it cuts the cone into its own ray and the half where it is nonnegative; this makes its ray extremal. Conversely, an extremal ray of negative square is not nef, so some curve is negative on it, and extremality forces the ray to be the ray of that curve. A ray of positive square sits inside the open positive cone, which lies in $\overline{NE}(S)$, and so it cannot be extremal unless the space is a line. Mori's theorem supplies a rational curve in each $K$-negative extremal ray. The cone theorem follows from Minkowski's theorem that a compact convex set is the convex hull of its extreme points, and the finiteness from the bound $-K \cdot C_i \leq 3$: rays with $(K + \varepsilon H) \cdot C_i < 0$ have $H \cdot C_i < 3/\varepsilon$, and there are finitely many lattice points in a bounded region.

For the classification, the genus formula makes the curve of a $K$-negative ray a $(-1)$-curve if its square is negative, and a smooth rational curve with $K \cdot C = -2$ if its square is $0$. In the first case Castelnuovo's theorem contracts it. In the second, Riemann-Roch makes $|nC|$ grow, extremality forces its moving part into the ray, and two members of square $0$ without common component are disjoint, so a pencil gives a morphism to $\mathbb{P}^1$ whose Stein factorization is the fibration. Every fibre component lies in the ray, so the fibres are multiples $mG$ of smooth rational curves, and the Euler characteristic of the fibre, which is $m$ for $F = mG$ and is at most $1$ at the generic fibre, forces $m = 1$. A divisor orthogonal to the fibre becomes effective after adding fibres, and its components are vertical, hence fibres; this gives $\rho = 2$. In the third case $\rho = 1$, and $-K$ is ample by Kleiman's criterion; the identification with $\mathbb{P}^2$ is cited.

### Examples

**Example P5.12.16 (the Hirzebruch surfaces).** By Example P5.12.8, $\overline{NE}(F_n) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C_-] + \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[F]$ with $C_-^2 = -n$, $F^2 = 0$, $K \cdot C_- = n - 2$ and $K \cdot F = -2$. Both rays are extremal: a two-dimensional cone has its two boundary rays as extremal rays. For $n \geq 2$ only the fibre ray is $K$-negative, and the cone theorem reads $\overline{NE}(F_n) = \overline{NE}(F_n)_{K \geq 0} + \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[F]$, where $\overline{NE}(F_n)_{K \geq 0}$ is the cone of the classes $\alpha C_- + \beta F$ with $\alpha, \beta \geq 0$ and $\alpha(n - 2) \geq 2\beta$; for $n = 2$ it is the ray of $C_-$. The ray of $F$ is of type 2 in Theorem P5.12.15, and its contraction is the projection $F_n \to \mathbb{P}^1$, with fibres $\mathbb{P}^1$ by Theorem P3.11.25. The ray of $C_-$ is extremal and spanned by a curve of square $-n$, but it is not $K$-negative and Theorem P5.12.15 says nothing about it: for $n = 2$ it is a $(-2)$-curve, and contracting it gives a quadric cone, which is singular. For $n = 1$ both rays are $K$-negative: $C_-$ is a $(-1)$-curve, of type 1, contracting to $\mathbb{P}^2$, and $F$ is of type 2. Here $\overline{NE}(F_1)_{K \geq 0} = \{0\}$, because $K \cdot (\alpha C_- + \beta F) = -\alpha - 2\beta$.

**Example P5.12.17 (the plane blown up at two points).** Let $S = \operatorname{Bl}_{q_1, q_2} \mathbb{P}^2$, with $\operatorname{Num}(S) = \mathbb{Z}\ell \oplus \mathbb{Z}E_1 \oplus \mathbb{Z}E_2$ and $K = -3\ell + E_1 + E_2$ (Example P5.6.8). The strict transform $L_{12}$ of the line through $q_1, q_2$ has class $\ell - E_1 - E_2$, and $E_1, E_2, L_{12}$ are $(-1)$-curves. An integral curve $C$ other than these three has class $a\ell - b_1E_1 - b_2E_2$ with $b_i = C \cdot E_i \geq 0$ and $a - b_1 - b_2 = C \cdot L_{12} \geq 0$ by Proposition P5.5.5, so $a \geq b_i$ and
$$a\ell - b_1 E_1 - b_2 E_2 = a L_{12} + (a - b_1) E_1 + (a - b_2) E_2.$$
Hence $\overline{NE}(S) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[E_1] + \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[E_2] + \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[L_{12}]$, and each of the three rays is extremal by Lemma P5.12.11 and $K$-negative, with $K \cdot E_i = K \cdot L_{12} = -1$. There are no other extremal rays, since every class of the cone is a nonnegative combination of these three. The class $\ell - E_1 = L_{12} + E_2$ of the pencil of lines through $q_1$ has square $0$ and $K \cdot (\ell - E_1) = -2$, but it is the sum of two non-proportional classes of the cone, so it does not span an extremal ray, in accordance with Theorem P5.12.15, which requires $\rho = 2$ for rays of square $0$. Since $-K \cdot E_i = -K \cdot L_{12} = 1 > 0$ on the three generators, $-K$ is positive on $\overline{NE}(S) \setminus \{0\}$ and ample by Theorem P5.12.6. Contracting $E_1$ or $E_2$ gives $F_1$, and contracting $L_{12}$ gives a surface with $\rho = 2$, described in Example P5.12.26.

**Example P5.12.18 (the projective plane and a surface with infinitely many rays).** On $\mathbb{P}^2$, $N^1 = \mathbb{R}\ell$, $\overline{NE} = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\ell$ and $K = -3\ell$. The only ray is $K$-negative with $\ell^2 = 1 > 0$, of type 3, and $-K \cdot \ell = 3$ attains the bound of Theorem P5.12.13 for $n = 2$. At the other extreme, let $S$ be the blow-up of $\mathbb{P}^2$ at nine general points. Chapter 10 shows that $S$ contains infinitely many $(-1)$-curves $C_n$. Each spans a $K$-negative extremal ray by Lemma P5.12.11, with $K \cdot C_n = -1$, and since there are finitely many integral classes of bounded $H$-degree, $H \cdot C_n \to \infty$. The normalized classes $[C_n] / (H \cdot C_n)$ therefore satisfy $K \cdot [C_n] / (H \cdot C_n) \to 0$, so their limit points in the compact slice $\Sigma_H$ lie on $K^\perp$, as Theorem P5.12.14 (3) predicts. The cone $\overline{NE}(S)$ is not polyhedral, and the infinitely many rays are what part (2) allows, finitely many on each side $K + \varepsilon H < 0$.

**Example P5.12.19 (a ruled surface over an elliptic curve).** Let $E$ be an elliptic curve and $S = E \times \mathbb{P}^1$, with fibre classes $f = \{x\} \times \mathbb{P}^1$ and $s = E \times \{y\}$, so $f^2 = s^2 = 0$, $f \cdot s = 1$, and $K = p_1^* K_E + p_2^* K_{\mathbb{P}^1} \sim -2s$ by Example P5.5.19 and $K_E \sim 0$. The classes $f$ and $s$ are classes of integral curves of square $0$, hence nef by Lemma P5.12.4. An integral curve $C$ of class $\alpha s + \beta f$, other than a fibre of either projection, has $\alpha = C \cdot f \geq 0$ and $\beta = C \cdot s \geq 0$. Step 5 of the proof of Theorem P5.12.15 below uses only that the fibres of a morphism onto a curve are integral with $f^2 = 0$ and $K \cdot f = -2$, so it applies to $p_1$ and gives $\rho(S) = 2$; the Gram matrix of $s, f$ has determinant $-1$, so $s, f$ span $N^1(S)$, and $\overline{NE}(S) = \operatorname{Nef}(S) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} s + \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} f$. Then $K \cdot f = -2$ and $K \cdot s = 0$: the ray of $f$ is $K$-negative of type 2, contracting to $E$ by $p_1$, while the ray of $s$ lies on $K^\perp$ and is the face $\overline{NE}(S)_{K \geq 0}$. No ray is of type 1 or 3, so $S$ contains no $(-1)$-curve, consistently with Example P5.11.22.

### Proof

*Lemma P5.12.11.* For every integral curve $C' \neq C$, $C \cdot C' \geq 0$ by Proposition P5.5.5, so $[C'] \in \overline{NE}(S)_{C \geq 0}$, and $NE(S) \subseteq \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C] + \overline{NE}(S)_{C \geq 0}$. The right side is closed: if $a_n [C] + y_n \to z$ with $a_n \geq 0$ and $y_n \in \overline{NE}(S)_{C \geq 0}$, then $H \cdot (a_n[C])$ and $H \cdot y_n$ are nonnegative with bounded sum, so by Lemma P5.12.2 (3) both sequences are bounded, and a subsequence converges to a decomposition of $z$. So the right side is a closed cone between $NE(S)$ and $\overline{NE}(S)$, and equals $\overline{NE}(S)$.

Let $u, v \in \overline{NE}(S)$ with $u + v = t[C]$, $t \geq 0$. Write $u = a[C] + u'$ and $v = b[C] + v'$ with $a, b \geq 0$ and $u', v' \in \overline{NE}(S)_{C \geq 0}$. Then $u' + v' = s[C]$ with $s = t - a - b$. If $s > 0$, then $0 \leq C \cdot (u' + v') = sC^2 < 0$, which is impossible. If $s < 0$, then $-[C] = |s|^{-1}(u' + v') \in \overline{NE}(S)$, and $[C] \neq 0$ (as $H \cdot C > 0$) contradicts Lemma P5.12.2 (3). So $s = 0$, $u' = -v' \in \overline{NE}(S) \cap (-\overline{NE}(S)) = \{0\}$, and $u = a[C]$, $v = b[C]$ lie in the ray. Finally, an integral $C' \neq C$ with $[C'] = \lambda [C]$, $\lambda > 0$, would have $C \cdot C' = \lambda C^2 < 0$, contradicting Proposition P5.5.5.

*Lemma P5.12.12.* (1) By Lemma P5.12.3, $x$ lies in the open set $\mathcal{P}(S) \subseteq \overline{NE}(S)$. If $\rho(S) \geq 2$, choose $y$ not proportional to $x$. For small $\varepsilon > 0$ both $x \pm \varepsilon y$ lie in $\mathcal{P}(S)$, and $x = \frac{1}{2}(x + \varepsilon y) + \frac{1}{2}(x - \varepsilon y)$. Extremality puts $x + \varepsilon y$ in $R$, so $y$ is proportional to $x$, a contradiction.

(2) By Lemma P5.12.4, $x$ is not nef, so some integral curve $C$ has $x \cdot C < 0$. An integral curve with $C^2 \geq 0$ is nef by Lemma P5.12.4, and $x \in \overline{NE}(S)$, so $C^2 < 0$. By Lemma P5.12.11, $x = a[C] + y$ with $a \geq 0$ and $y \in \overline{NE}(S)_{C \geq 0}$, and $a > 0$, since for $a = 0$ the class $x = y$ would have $x \cdot C \geq 0$. By extremality $y \in R$, say $y = \lambda x$ with $\lambda \geq 0$; then $0 \leq C \cdot y = \lambda \, C \cdot x$ forces $\lambda = 0$. So $x = a[C]$ and $R = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C]$. Uniqueness is Lemma P5.12.11.

(3) $C^2 < 0$ and $K \cdot C < 0$, so $C$ is a $(-1)$-curve by Corollary P5.5.15.

*Theorem P5.12.13.* This is Mori's theorem, from *Threefolds whose canonical bundles are not numerically effective* (Annals of Mathematics 116, 1982), Section 1; see Kollár and Mori, Theorem 1.24, for the cone theorem in this form. The proof constructs rational curves by deforming a curve $C$ with $K \cdot C < 0$ through a fixed point, which is possible after reduction to characteristic $p$ and composition with a high power of the Frobenius, and breaks the deformations into rational components by the bend-and-break lemma. It is not reproved here. For surfaces, rays of negative square do not need it, by Lemma P5.12.12 (2) and (3), and the ray of $\mathbb{P}^2$ does not need it either; it is used only for rays of square $0$.

*Theorem P5.12.14.* (1) The first claim is Theorem P5.12.13 with $n = 2$. Let $V$ denote the right side of the displayed equation, a subcone of $\overline{NE}(S)$. The slice $\Sigma_H$ is a compact convex set, and by Minkowski's theorem it is the convex hull of its extreme points: every point of $\Sigma_H$ is a convex combination of finitely many extreme points (Rockafellar, *Convex analysis*, Corollary 18.5.1). A point $y \in \Sigma_H$ is extreme exactly when $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} y$ is an extremal ray of $\overline{NE}(S)$. Indeed, if $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} y$ is extremal and $y = tu + (1 - t)v$ with $u, v \in \Sigma_H$ and $0 < t < 1$, then $tu$ and $(1 - t)v$ lie in the ray, and normalizing by $H$ gives $u = v = y$. Conversely, if $y$ is extreme in $\Sigma_H$ and $y = u + v$ with $u, v \in \overline{NE}(S) \setminus \{0\}$, then $y = (H \cdot u) \frac{u}{H \cdot u} + (H \cdot v) \frac{v}{H \cdot v}$ is a convex combination of points of $\Sigma_H$, so both normalized points are $y$, and $u, v \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} y$. So every $z \in \overline{NE}(S) \setminus \{0\}$ is a finite sum of generators of extremal rays. Each extremal ray $R$ either has $K \cdot R \geq 0$, so $R \subseteq \overline{NE}(S)_{K \geq 0}$, or is some $R_i = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C_i]$. Hence $z \in V$.

(2) If $(K + \varepsilon H) \cdot C_i < 0$, then $\varepsilon H \cdot C_i < -K \cdot C_i \leq 3$, so $H \cdot C_i < 3/\varepsilon$, and by Lemma P5.12.2 (3) the class $[C_i]$ lies in the bounded set $\{ y \in \overline{NE}(S) : |y| \leq 3c/\varepsilon \}$. The classes $[C_i]$ lie in the lattice $\operatorname{Num}(S) \subseteq N^1(S)$, which has finitely many points in a bounded set, and distinct rays have distinct generators $[C_i]$. So there are finitely many such $i$. In the decomposition $z = z_0 + \sum a_i [C_i]$ of (1), the term $z_0 \in \overline{NE}(S)_{K \geq 0}$ satisfies $(K + \varepsilon H) \cdot z_0 \geq 0$, as do the $[C_i]$ with $(K + \varepsilon H) \cdot C_i \geq 0$, and their sum lies in $\overline{NE}(S)_{K + \varepsilon H \geq 0}$. The remaining terms belong to the finite list.

(3) Let $y_n = [C_{i_n}]/(H \cdot C_{i_n}) \in \Sigma_H$ for distinct $i_n$, converging to $y$. Then $K \cdot y_n < 0$ gives $K \cdot y \leq 0$. If $K \cdot y < 0$, choose $\varepsilon > 0$ with $(K + \varepsilon H) \cdot y = K \cdot y + \varepsilon < 0$; then $(K + \varepsilon H) \cdot y_n < 0$ for all large $n$, so infinitely many distinct $i$ violate (2). So $K \cdot y = 0$.

(4) If $K$ is nef, no ray is $K$-negative. If $K$ is not nef, some $z \in \overline{NE}(S)$ has $K \cdot z < 0$, so $z \notin \overline{NE}(S)_{K \geq 0}$, and the decomposition of (1) contains some $C_i$.

*Theorem P5.12.15.* Let $R = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} x$ with $K \cdot x < 0$. The three cases $x^2 < 0$, $x^2 = 0$ and $x^2 > 0$ are exclusive, and the square of any curve with class in $R$ has the sign of $x^2$.

*Case $x^2 < 0$.* By Lemma P5.12.12, $R = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C]$ for a $(-1)$-curve $C$, the only integral curve with class in $R$. By Castelnuovo's theorem (Theorem P0.8.32, and Chapter 7) there are a smooth projective surface $S'$, a point $p \in S'$ and an isomorphism $S \cong \operatorname{Bl}_p S'$ sending $C$ to the exceptional curve. The blow-down $\varphi \colon S \to S'$ is projective, $\varphi_* \mathcal{O}_S = \mathcal{O}_{S'}$ by Theorem P5.6.2, it contracts exactly $C$ and is an isomorphism elsewhere, and $\rho(S') = \rho(S) - 1$ by Theorem P5.6.3 (4).

*Case $x^2 > 0$.* By Lemma P5.12.12 (1), $\rho(S) = 1$, so $N^1(S) = \mathbb{R}[H]$ and $\overline{NE}(S) = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[H] = R$. Then $K \cdot H < 0$, so $-K = \lambda [H]$ in $N^1(S)$ with $\lambda > 0$, and $-K$ is positive on $\overline{NE}(S) \setminus \{0\}$, hence ample by Theorem P5.12.6. A smooth projective surface with $\rho = 1$ and $-K$ ample is isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^2$, and this is cited. It follows from the classification: $h^0(nK) = 0$ for $n \geq 1$, since $H \cdot nK < 0$ (Lemma P5.5.20), so $\kappa(S) = -\infty$; $S$ contains no $(-1)$-curve, since its contraction would have $\rho = 0$ by Theorem P5.6.3, so $S$ is its own minimal model; and by Theorem P5.11.26 (1) it is $\mathbb{P}^2$ or a geometrically ruled surface, which has $\rho = 2$. Kollár and Mori, Theorem 1.28, and Mori 1982, Chapter 2, give the statement directly. The structure map to a point contracts every curve, and every class lies in $R$.

*Case $x^2 = 0$.* Here $\rho(S) \geq 2$, since for $\rho(S) = 1$ every nonzero class is a multiple of $[H]$ and has nonzero square. By Theorem P5.12.13, $R = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C]$ with $C$ an integral rational curve, so $C^2 = 0$ and $K \cdot C < 0$. The genus formula gives $2p_a(C) - 2 = C^2 + K \cdot C < 0$, so $p_a(C) = 0$, $K \cdot C = -2$, and $C \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ by Corollary P5.5.15. The construction of $\varphi_R$ takes five steps.

Step 1, a pencil. For $n \geq 1$, Riemann-Roch (Theorem P5.5.13) gives
$$h^0(nC) + h^0(K - nC) \geq \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) + \tfrac{1}{2} nC \cdot (nC - K) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) + n.$$
For large $n$, $H \cdot (K - nC) < 0$, so $K - nC$ is not effective by Lemma P5.5.20, and $h^0(nC) \geq \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) + n \geq 2$. Fix such an $n$ and write $|nC| = |M| + Z$ with $Z$ the fixed part and $|M|$ without fixed components, $\dim |M| \geq 1$. Then $n[C] = [M] + [Z]$ with $[M], [Z] \in NE(S)$, and extremality, applied repeatedly to the decompositions into components, puts the class of every component of $M$ and of $Z$ in $R$. So $M^2 = 0$, and $M \neq 0$ has $H \cdot M > 0$. Let $M_1 \in |M|$. For each of the finitely many components $D$ of $M_1$, the members of $|M|$ containing $D$ form a proper linear subspace, since $D$ is not a fixed component, and $k$ is infinite, so some $M_2 \in |M|$ has no component in common with $M_1$. Then $M_1 \cdot M_2 = M^2 = 0$, and $M_1 \cap M_2 = \emptyset$ by Proposition P5.5.5. The sections $s_1, s_2$ of $\mathcal{O}_S(M)$ defining $M_1$ and $M_2$ are linearly independent and have no common zero, so they define a surjective morphism $g = (s_1 : s_2) \colon S \to \mathbb{P}^1$ with $g^* \mathcal{O}(1) \cong \mathcal{O}_S(M)$ (Theorem P3.6.21).

Step 2, the fibration. Let $g = h \circ \varphi$ be the Stein factorization (Theorem P4.6.15), with $\varphi \colon S \to B$ a contraction and $h \colon B \to \mathbb{P}^1$ finite. Then $B$ is a projective curve, integral since $S$ is, and normal since $\mathcal{O}_B = \varphi_* \mathcal{O}_S$ and $S$ is normal: a rational function on an open $W \subseteq B$ integral over $\mathcal{O}_B(W)$ pulls back to a rational function on $\varphi^{-1}(W)$ integral over $\mathcal{O}_S(\varphi^{-1}W)$, hence regular. So $B$ is a smooth projective curve. Since $S$ is integral and $\varphi$ is dominant, $\varphi$ is flat by Theorem P3.13.4. For a closed point $b \in B$ write $F_b = \varphi^* b$, an effective divisor whose support is the fibre, connected by Theorem P4.6.7.

Step 3, all fibres are numerically equal and lie in $R$. For a divisor $D$, Definition P5.5.4 gives $D \cdot F_b = \chi(\mathcal{O}_{F_b}) - \chi(\mathcal{O}_{F_b}(-D))$. The sheaves $\mathcal{O}_S$ and $\mathcal{O}_S(-D)$ are flat over $B$, so both Euler characteristics are independent of $b$ by Theorem P4.5.13, and $F_b \equiv F_{b'}$ for all $b, b'$. A general fibre of $g$ is a member of $|M|$, and it equals $\sum_{b \in h^{-1}(t)} e_b F_b$ with positive $e_b$, so $[M]$ is a positive multiple of $[F_b]$, and $[F_b] \in R$. By extremality every component $G$ of every fibre has $[G] \in R$, so $G^2 = 0$, $K \cdot G < 0$, and two components $G \neq G'$ have $G \cdot G' = 0$, hence are disjoint by Proposition P5.5.5. A fibre is connected, so it has a single component: $F_b = m_b G_b$ with $G_b$ integral. As for $C$, the genus formula gives $G_b \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ and $K \cdot G_b = -2$. Since $K \cdot F_b = -2 m_b$ is independent of $b$, $m_b = m$ is constant.

Step 4, the fibres are reduced. By Riemann-Roch, $\chi(\mathcal{O}_{F_b}) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) - \chi(\mathcal{O}_S(-F_b)) = -\frac{1}{2} F_b \cdot (F_b + K) = -\frac{1}{2}(0 - 2m) = m$. By Theorem P4.5.13 this is also $\chi$ of the generic fibre $S_\eta$, a projective curve over $k(B)$. By Corollary P4.5.2 (2), $H^0(S_\eta, \mathcal{O}_{S_\eta}) \cong (\varphi_* \mathcal{O}_S)_\eta = k(B)$, so $\chi(\mathcal{O}_{S_\eta}) = 1 - h^1(\mathcal{O}_{S_\eta}) \leq 1$. Hence $m = 1$, and every fibre $F_b = G_b$ is a reduced curve isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^1$, with $-K \cdot F_b = 2$. By Hartshorne, Theorem III.10.2, $\varphi$ is smooth.

Step 5, $\rho(S) = 2$ and the curves in $R$. Let $F$ be a fibre. Let $D$ be a divisor with $D \cdot F = 0$. Riemann-Roch for $D + jF$ gives
$$h^0(D + jF) + h^0(K - D - jF) \geq \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) + \tfrac{1}{2}(D + jF) \cdot (D + jF - K) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) + \tfrac{1}{2} D \cdot (D - K) + j,$$
using $F^2 = D \cdot F = 0$ and $K \cdot F = -2$. For large $j$, $K - D - jF$ has negative $H$-degree and is not effective, so $D + jF \sim A$ with $A$ effective. Then $A \cdot F = 0$. Every integral curve $\Gamma$ has $\Gamma \cdot F \geq 0$, choosing a fibre in the class of $F$ different from $\Gamma$, so every component $\Gamma$ of $A$ has $\Gamma \cdot F = 0$. If $\varphi(\Gamma) = B$, then $\Gamma$ meets every fibre, and a fibre $F_b \neq \Gamma$ gives $\Gamma \cdot F_b > 0$ by Proposition P5.5.5. So $\varphi(\Gamma)$ is a point $b$, $\Gamma \subseteq F_b$, and $\Gamma = F_b$. Hence $A$ is a sum of fibres, and $D \equiv A - jF \equiv cF$ for an integer $c$. So the kernel of $\operatorname{Num}(S) \to \mathbb{Z}$, $D \mapsto D \cdot F$, has rank at most $1$, and $\rho(S) \leq 2$; with $\rho(S) \geq 2$ this gives $\rho(S) = 2$. The same argument shows that an integral curve with class in $R$, which has $\Gamma \cdot F = 0$, is a fibre, and conversely every fibre has class $[F] \in R$. So $\varphi$ contracts exactly the curves with class in $R$, and $\varphi = \varphi_R$.

### Lean

Mathlib has convex cones (`ConvexCone`) and the extreme points of a set (`Set.extremePoints`), and the Krein-Milman theorem, of which Minkowski's theorem in the proof of Theorem P5.12.14 is the finite-dimensional case. It has no cone of curves, no extremal contractions and no deformation theory of rational curves, so neither Mori's theorem nor the classification can be stated. The handbook adds no `MinimalModel` node here.

### Notes and history

Mori proved Theorem P5.12.13 and the cone theorem for smooth projective varieties of any dimension in 1982, and classified the extremal contractions of smooth threefolds in the same paper; Theorem P5.12.15 is the surface part, Kollár and Mori, Theorem 1.28. The literature state is theorem. For surfaces there are proofs of the cone theorem that avoid bend-and-break and use Riemann-Roch on the boundary of the cone, and Mori's original paper treats the two-dimensional case separately. The contraction of Case 1 is Castelnuovo's theorem of the 1890s, and the conic bundles of Case 2 are the geometrically ruled surfaces, which by Tsen's theorem have a section and are projectivized rank $2$ bundles over $B$ (Beauville, Chapter III; Hartshorne, Section V.2). In the minimal model program, Theorem P5.12.14 is the surface case of the cone theorem of Volume 2, and the three types of Theorem P5.12.15 are the divisorial contractions and the two kinds of Mori fibre space; the small contractions, which need flips, do not occur on surfaces.
