## Ruled surfaces and the Hirzebruch surfaces

### Statement

In this chapter $k$ is algebraically closed, a surface is a smooth projective surface over $k$, and the notation of Chapters 5 and 6 is in force. Let $C$ be a smooth projective curve of genus $g$. For a locally free sheaf $\mathcal{E}$ of rank $2$ on $C$ write $\deg \mathcal{E} = \deg \det \mathcal{E}$, where $\det \mathcal{E} = \wedge^2 \mathcal{E}$. If $0 \to \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N} \to 0$ is exact with $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ invertible, then $\det \mathcal{E} \cong \mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{N}$, so $\deg \mathcal{E} = \deg \mathcal{M} + \deg \mathcal{N}$, and $\deg (\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}) = \deg \mathcal{E} + 2 \deg \mathcal{L}$ for invertible $\mathcal{L}$. Let $X = \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$ be the projective bundle of Definition P3.6.33, with $\pi \colon X \to C$ and $\mathcal{O}_X(1)$. Write $h$ for the class of $\mathcal{O}_X(1)$, $X_y = \pi^{-1}(y)$ for the scheme-theoretic fibre over a closed point $y$, so that $X_y = \pi^* y$ as a divisor, and $f$ for the class of a fibre.

**Definition P5.9.1 (geometrically ruled surface).** A *geometrically ruled surface* over $C$ is a surface $X$ with a morphism $\pi \colon X \to C$ such that the fibre $X_y$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^1_k$ for every closed point $y \in C$, and $\pi$ has a section. A *section* is a morphism $\sigma \colon C \to X$ with $\pi \sigma = \mathrm{id}_C$; its image $D = \sigma(C)$ is a curve isomorphic to $C$, and the word section is also used for $D$.

**Lemma P5.9.2 (finite morphisms with one-point fibres).** Let $Y$ be a reduced scheme of finite type over $k$ and $\varphi \colon X \to Y$ a finite morphism such that for every closed point $y \in Y$ the fibre $X_y = X \times_Y \operatorname{Spec} \kappa(y)$ is isomorphic to $\operatorname{Spec} \kappa(y)$. Then $\varphi$ is an isomorphism.

**Proposition P5.9.3 (the projective bundle over a curve).** Let $\mathcal{E}$ be locally free of rank $2$ on $C$ and $X = \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$.

1. $X$ is a surface, $\pi$ is flat, and $X_y \cong \mathbb{P}^1_k$ with $\mathcal{O}_X(1)|_{X_y} \cong \mathcal{O}(1)$ for every closed point $y$. The sheaf $\pi_* \mathcal{O}_X(n)$ is $\operatorname{Sym}^n \mathcal{E}$ for $n \geq 0$, $R^1 \pi_* \mathcal{O}_X = 0$, and $h^i(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^i(\mathcal{O}_C)$ for every $i$. So $q(X) = g$, $p_g(X) = 0$ and $\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = 1 - g$.
2. There is an exact sequence $0 \to \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N} \to 0$ with $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ invertible.
3. Sections $\sigma$ of $\pi$ correspond to surjections $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N}$ onto invertible sheaves, up to isomorphism of $\mathcal{N}$. If $\sigma$ corresponds to $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N}$ with kernel $\mathcal{M}$ and $D = \sigma(C)$, then $\sigma^* \mathcal{O}_X(1) \cong \mathcal{N}$ and $\mathcal{O}_X(D) \cong \mathcal{O}_X(1) \otimes \pi^* \mathcal{M}^{-1}$. In particular $X$ is a geometrically ruled surface over $C$.

**Theorem P5.9.4 (Picard group, intersection form and canonical class).** Let $X = \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$ as above.

1. $\operatorname{Pic}(X) = \mathbb{Z} h \oplus \pi^* \operatorname{Pic}(C)$, and $\pi^* \colon \operatorname{Pic}(C) \to \operatorname{Pic}(X)$ is injective.
2. For invertible $\mathcal{M}$ on $C$, $\pi^* \mathcal{M} \equiv (\deg \mathcal{M}) f$. The intersection numbers are $f^2 = 0$, $h \cdot f = 1$ and $h^2 = \deg \mathcal{E}$, and $\operatorname{Num}(X) = \mathbb{Z} h \oplus \mathbb{Z} f$ with a unimodular form. If a section $D$ corresponds to $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N}$ with kernel $\mathcal{M}$, then $D^2 = \deg \mathcal{N} - \deg \mathcal{M} = 2 \deg \mathcal{N} - \deg \mathcal{E}$.

**Definition P5.9.6 (normalized bundle and the invariant $e$).** A locally free sheaf $\mathcal{E}$ of rank $2$ on $C$ is *normalized* if $H^0(C, \mathcal{E}) \neq 0$ and $H^0(C, \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}) = 0$ for every invertible $\mathcal{L}$ with $\deg \mathcal{L} < 0$. For a normalized $\mathcal{E}$ put $e = -\deg \mathcal{E}$.

**Proposition P5.9.7 (normalized bundles).**

1. For every locally free $\mathcal{E}$ of rank $2$ there is an invertible $\mathcal{L}$ with $\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}$ normalized. If $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{E}'$ are normalized and $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}) \cong \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}')$ over $C$, then $\deg \mathcal{E} = \deg \mathcal{E}'$. 4. $e \geq -2g$. If $e > 2g - 2$, then $\mathcal{E}$ is decomposable.

**Proposition P5.9.9 (the Hirzebruch surfaces).** Let $F_n = \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(n))$ over $\mathbb{P}^1$, $n \geq 0$, with the curves $C_-$, $C_+$ and $F$ of Definition P3.11.24.

1. Every geometrically ruled surface over $\mathbb{P}^1$ is isomorphic over $\mathbb{P}^1$ to exactly one $F_n$. The bundle $\mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(-n)$ is a normalization of $\mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(n)$, so $e(F_n) = n$, and $C_0 = C_-$.
2. $\operatorname{Pic}(F_n) = \operatorname{Num}(F_n) = \mathbb{Z} C_- \oplus \mathbb{Z} F$ with $C_-^2 = -n$, $C_- \cdot F = 1$, $F^2 = 0$. The class $h$ of $\mathcal{O}(1)$ is $C_+ \sim C_- + nF$, with $C_+^2 = n$, and $K \sim -2C_- - (n + 2)F$, with $K^2 = 8$.
3. $F_n \cong F_m$ as varieties over $k$ only if $n = m$.
4. $-K$ is ample if and only if $n \leq 1$. For every $n$, $-K \cdot C_- = 2 - n$.

For a closed point $c \in \mathbb{P}^1$ write $F_c$ for the fibre of $F_n$ over $c$. Use the charts $V_0$ and $V_1$ of Example P3.6.37, glued by $s = 1/t$ and $[a' : b'] = [a : t^n b]$. **Proposition P5.9.10 (elementary transformations).** Let $n \geq 0$, let $\lambda$ be a linear form vanishing at $c \in \mathbb{P}^1$, let $x = C_- \cap F_c$ in $F_n$ and $x' = C_+ \cap F_c$ in $F_{n+1}$.

1. The formulas $([a : b], t) \mapsto ([\lambda(t) a : b], t)$ on $V_0$ and $([a' : b'], s) \mapsto ([\lambda'(s) a' : b'], s)$ on $V_1$ define a birational map $\varepsilon \colon F_n \dashrightarrow F_{n+1}$ over $\mathbb{P}^1$, which is a morphism on $F_n \setminus \{x\}$, an isomorphism from $F_n \setminus F_c$ onto $F_{n+1} \setminus F_c$, and maps $F_c \setminus \{x\}$ to the point $x'$.
2. There is an isomorphism $\operatorname{Bl}_x F_n \cong \operatorname{Bl}_{x'} F_{n+1}$ over $\mathbb{P}^1$ that extends $\varepsilon$. It maps the exceptional curve over $x$ to the strict transform of the fibre of $F_{n+1}$ over $c$, the strict transform of the fibre $F_c$ of $F_n$ to the exceptional curve over $x'$, and the strict transform of $C_-$ to the strict transform of $C_-$.
3. Let $m \geq 0$. For every closed point $y \in F_m \setminus C_-$ there is an automorphism of $F_m$ over $\mathbb{P}^1$ that preserves $C_-$ and maps $y$ to a point of $C_+$. So for $m \geq 1$, blowing up a point of $F_m \setminus C_-$ and contracting the strict transform of its fibre gives $F_{m-1}$, and blowing up a point of $C_-$ and contracting the strict transform of its fibre gives $F_{m+1}$.

The birational map $\varepsilon$, and more generally the passage from a ruled surface $X$ to the ruled surface obtained by blowing up a point $x$ and contracting the strict transform of the fibre through $x$, is the *elementary transformation* of $X$ at $x$.

### Proof in words

A projective bundle over a curve is glued from products $U \times \mathbb{P}^1$, so its fibres are lines and the cohomology of its structure sheaf is that of the curve. A line bundle on $X$ has a degree on the fibres, and after twisting by $\mathcal{O}(-d)$ it is trivial on every fibre and so comes from the base; this gives the Picard group. The canonical class is then pinned down by adjunction twice: on a fibre it has degree $-2$, and on a section it restricts to the canonical class of $C$. Conversely a fibration by lines with a divisor of degree $1$ on the fibres embeds itself, fibre by fibre, as the projective bundle of the push-forward of that divisor.

A rank $2$ bundle is determined by $X$ only up to twist, and normalizing it fixes the twist: the normalized $\mathcal{E}$ has a section, but no twist by a negative line bundle has one. The section is the section $C_0$ of $X$ of smallest self-intersection $-e$. For $e \geq 0$ every other irreducible curve meets $C_0$ nonnegatively, which bounds its class and gives the ample cone. Over $\mathbb{P}^1$ Grothendieck's theorem makes every bundle split, so every ruled surface is some $F_n$, and $n$ is read off from the negative curve.

The elementary transformation is a single explicit formula. Multiplying the fibre coordinate $a$ by the equation $\lambda(t)$ of a fibre changes the twist $t^n$ of the gluing into $t^{n+1}$. In local coordinates near $x$ the formula is $(r, t) \mapsto t/r$, which is undefined only at $x$ and becomes a morphism after one blow-up, so the two blow-ups are identified by the universal property.

### Examples

**Example P5.9.11 (the surfaces $F_0$ and $F_1$).** For $n = 0$, $F_0 = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ (Example P3.11.27), $C_-$ and $F$ are the two rulings, $K \sim -2C_- - 2F$, $K^2 = 8$, and $-K$ is ample by Proposition P5.9.8, as $b = 2 > a e = 0$. Every class $a C_- + b F$ with $a, b \geq 0$ is effective, and the integral curves of class $a C_- + b F$ with $a, b > 0$ are the curves of bidegree $(a, b)$ of Example P5.5.17. For $n = 1$, Theorem P3.11.26 identifies $F_1$ with $\operatorname{Bl}_p \mathbb{P}^2$, with $C_- = E$, $C_+ = \ell$ the pullback of a line, and $F = \ell - E$. Then Proposition P5.9.9 gives $K \sim -2E - 3(\ell - E) = -3\ell + E$, which is the formula of Theorem P5.6.3, and $-K \cdot E = 1$, $-K \cdot (\ell - E) = 2$, $(-K)^2 = 9 - 1 = 8$.

**Example P5.9.12 (decomposable bundles over an elliptic curve).** Let $C$ be an elliptic curve, so $\omega_C \cong \mathcal{O}_C$, and $\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{O}_C \oplus \mathcal{L}$ with $\deg \mathcal{L} = -e \leq 0$. By Theorem P5.9.4, $K \equiv -2h + \deg \mathcal{E} \cdot f = -2C_0 - e f$, and $K^2 = 4C_0^2 + 4e \, C_0 \cdot f = -4e + 4e = 0 = 8(1 - g)$. The section $C_0$ corresponds to the quotient $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{L}$ with kernel $\mathcal{O}_C$, and the second summand gives the section $C_1$ with quotient $\mathcal{O}_C$ and kernel $\mathcal{L}$, of self-intersection $0 - (-e) = e$ by Theorem P5.9.4. By part 3 of Proposition P5.9.3, $C_1 \sim h - \pi^* \mathcal{L}$, so $C_1 \equiv C_0 + e f$ and $C_0 \cdot C_1 = -e + e = 0$: the two sections are disjoint. For $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{O}_C$ the surface is $C \times \mathbb{P}^1$. For $e > 0$, $-K \cdot C_0 = -2e + e = -e < 0$, so $-K$ is not nef, although $-K$ is effective: by part 4 of Theorem P4.4.14, $h^0(-K) = h^0(\operatorname{Sym}^2 \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{-1}) = h^0(\mathcal{L}^{-1}) + h^0(\mathcal{O}_C) + h^0(\mathcal{L}) \geq 1 + e$, and every member of $|-K|$ contains $C_0$.

**Example P5.9.13 (the indecomposable bundle of degree $1$ on an elliptic curve).** Let $C$ be an elliptic curve and $P \in C$ a closed point. By Serre duality and Riemann-Roch, $h^1(\mathcal{O}_C(-P)) = h^0(\mathcal{O}_C(P)) = 1$, so there is a nonsplit extension $0 \to \mathcal{O}_C \to \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{O}_C(P) \to 0$ (extensions of $\mathcal{O}_C(P)$ by $\mathcal{O}_C$ are classified by $H^1(\mathcal{O}_C(-P))$, Hartshorne, Exercise III.6.1 and Proposition III.6.7). Then $\mathcal{E}$ is normalized. Indeed $H^0(\mathcal{E}) \supseteq H^0(\mathcal{O}_C) \neq 0$. Let $\deg \mathcal{L} < 0$. The sequence $0 \to \mathcal{L} \to \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L} \to \mathcal{L}(P) \to 0$ has $H^0(\mathcal{L}) = 0$. If $\deg \mathcal{L} \leq -2$, also $H^0(\mathcal{L}(P)) = 0$. If $\deg \mathcal{L} = -1$ and $\mathcal{L}(P) \not\cong \mathcal{O}_C$, then $H^0(\mathcal{L}(P)) = 0$ as well. If $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{O}_C(-P)$, a nonzero section of $\mathcal{E}(-P)$ maps to a nonzero constant in $H^0(\mathcal{O}_C)$, which gives a splitting $\mathcal{O}_C(P) \to \mathcal{E}$ of $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{O}_C(P)$, a contradiction. So $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}) = 0$ in all cases, $\mathcal{E}$ is normalized, and $e = -1$. By part 3 of Proposition P5.9.7, $\mathcal{E}$ is indecomposable. On $X = \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$ the section $C_0$ has $C_0^2 = 1$, and $K \equiv -2C_0 + f$, with $K^2 = 4 - 4 = 0$. The value $e = -1$ lies strictly between the bound $-2g = -2$ of Proposition P5.9.7 and $2g - 2 = 0$, and it equals Nagata's bound $-g$ quoted in the Notes.

**Example P5.9.14 (the quadric and the plane blown up in two points).** Apply Proposition P5.9.10 with $n = 0$: for $x \in F_0 = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ on $C_-$, $\operatorname{Bl}_x F_0 \cong \operatorname{Bl}_{x'} F_1$ with $x' \notin C_-$. By Theorem P3.11.26, $F_1 = \operatorname{Bl}_{p_1} \mathbb{P}^2$ with $C_- = E_1$, and $x'$ lies over a point $p_2 \neq p_1$ of $\mathbb{P}^2$. So $\operatorname{Bl}_x(\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1) \cong \operatorname{Bl}_{p_1, p_2} \mathbb{P}^2$, the isomorphism of Example P5.7.17, obtained here from the elementary transformation. In the basis $\ell, E_1, E_2$ of Theorem P5.6.3 the fibre of $F_1$ through $x'$ is the line through $p_1$ and $p_2$, with strict transform $\ell - E_1 - E_2$, and the exceptional curve over $x'$ is $E_2$. Proposition P5.9.10 says that $E_2$ is the strict transform of the fibre $F_c$ of $F_0$ through $x$ and $\ell - E_1 - E_2$ is the exceptional curve $E_x$. The strict transform of $C_-$ is the strict transform $E_1$ of the negative section of $F_1$. So the pullbacks of the two rulings of $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ are $F_c + E_x \mapsto E_2 + (\ell - E_1 - E_2) = \ell - E_1$ and $C_- + E_x \mapsto E_1 + (\ell - E_1 - E_2) = \ell - E_2$, the two pencils of lines through $p_1$ and through $p_2$. Both have square $0$ and product $1$, as they should.

### Proof

*Proof of Lemma P5.9.2.* As $\varphi$ is finite, $\mathcal{A} = \varphi_* \mathcal{O}_X$ is a coherent $\mathcal{O}_Y$-algebra and $X = \operatorname{Spec}_Y \mathcal{A}$. Let $\mathcal{Q}$ be the cokernel of $\mathcal{O}_Y \to \mathcal{A}$, a coherent sheaf. For a closed point $y$, the fibre $X_y$ is $\operatorname{Spec}(\mathcal{A}_y \otimes \kappa(y))$, so $\mathcal{A}_y \otimes \kappa(y) = \kappa(y)$ is spanned by the image of $1$, and $\mathcal{A}_y = \mathcal{O}_{Y,y} \cdot 1 + \mathfrak{m}_y \mathcal{A}_y$. By Nakayama's lemma $\mathcal{A}_y = \mathcal{O}_{Y,y} \cdot 1$, so $\mathcal{Q}_y = 0$. The support of $\mathcal{Q}$ is closed and contains no closed point, so it is empty, since every nonempty closed subset of $Y$ contains a closed point. So $\mathcal{O}_Y \to \mathcal{A}$ is surjective with kernel an ideal $\mathcal{I}$, and $\varphi$ is the closed immersion of $V(\mathcal{I})$. Its image contains every closed point, and $Y$ is of finite type over $k$, so the closed points are dense in every closed subset and $V(\mathcal{I}) = Y$ as sets. So every section of $\mathcal{I}$ is nilpotent, and $\mathcal{I} = 0$ because $Y$ is reduced. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition P5.9.3.* (1) On an open $U \subseteq C$ on which $\mathcal{E}$ is free, $\pi^{-1}(U) \cong \mathbb{P}^1_U$ with $\mathcal{O}_X(1)$ corresponding to $\mathcal{O}(1)$ (part 3 of Proposition P3.6.35). So $\pi$ is flat and smooth of relative dimension $1$, $X$ is smooth of dimension $2$ over $k$, and $X_y \cong \mathbb{P}^1_k$ with $\mathcal{O}_X(1)|_{X_y} \cong \mathcal{O}(1)$. It is integral, since it is irreducible (two nonempty opens $\pi^{-1}(U)$, $\pi^{-1}(U')$ meet in $\pi^{-1}(U \cap U') \neq \emptyset$) and reduced. For projectivity, let $\mathcal{A}$ be an ample invertible sheaf on $C$. By Theorem P3.6.23, $\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{A}^m$ is globally generated for large $m$, so there is a surjection $\mathcal{O}_C^{N+1} \to \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{A}^m$ and hence a surjection $\operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{O}_C^{N+1}) \to \operatorname{Sym}(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{A}^m)$ of graded algebras. Over every affine open of $C$ part 4 of Proposition P3.6.4 makes the induced morphism a closed immersion, so $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}) \cong \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{A}^m)$ (part 2 of Proposition P3.6.35) is a closed subscheme of $\mathbb{P}^N_C = \mathbb{P}^N \times C$. As $C$ is projective, the Segre embedding (Theorem P3.10.20) makes $X$ projective. The statements on $\pi_*$ and $R^1 \pi_*$ and the equality $h^i(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^i(\mathcal{O}_C)$ are part 1 of Theorem P4.4.14 and Corollary P4.4.15. So $q(X) = h^1(\mathcal{O}_C) = g$, $h^2(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^2(\mathcal{O}_C) = 0$, hence $p_g(X) = h^0(\omega_X) = h^2(\mathcal{O}_X) = 0$ by Serre duality (Theorem P4.7.21), and $\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = 1 - g$.

(2) Let $\mathcal{A}$ be ample. For large $m$, $\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{A}^m$ is globally generated and nonzero, so it has a nonzero section, that is, a nonzero map $s \colon \mathcal{A}^{-m} \to \mathcal{E}$. It is injective, since $\mathcal{E}$ is torsion free and $C$ is integral. Let $\mathcal{T}$ be the torsion subsheaf of $\mathcal{E}/s(\mathcal{A}^{-m})$ and $\mathcal{M} \subseteq \mathcal{E}$ the preimage of $\mathcal{T}$. Then $\mathcal{N} = \mathcal{E}/\mathcal{M}$ is torsion free of rank $1$, hence invertible, as a finitely generated torsion free module over a discrete valuation ring is free (Proposition P5.1.1). Then $\mathcal{M}$ is locally free of rank $1$, as the kernel of a surjection of locally free sheaves.

(3) The correspondence is part 1 of Proposition P3.6.35, and it sends $\sigma$ to the pullback $\mathcal{E} = \sigma^* \pi^* \mathcal{E} \to \sigma^* \mathcal{O}_X(1)$ of the universal quotient of Theorem P3.6.34, so $\sigma^* \mathcal{O}_X(1) \cong \mathcal{N}$. Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the kernel and $u \colon \pi^* \mathcal{M} \to \pi^* \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{O}_X(1)$ the composite, a section of $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{O}_X(1) \otimes \pi^* \mathcal{M}^{-1}$. On the fibre $X_y = \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}(y))$, with $\mathcal{E}(y) = \mathcal{E} \otimes \kappa(y)$, the restriction of $u$ is the image $v$ of a generator of $\mathcal{M}(y)$, a nonzero vector of $\mathcal{E}(y) = H^0(X_y, \mathcal{O}(1))$, that is, a nonzero linear form on the line $X_y$. It vanishes at exactly one point, simply, and that point is the quotient of $\mathcal{E}(y)$ killing $v$, which is $\mathcal{E}(y) \to \mathcal{N}(y)$, that is, $\sigma(y)$. So $u \neq 0$, its divisor $Z$ is effective with $\mathcal{O}_X(Z) \cong \mathcal{L}$, it contains no fibre, and $Z \cap X_y = \{\sigma(y)\}$ for every closed $y$. So $Z$ and $D$ have the same closed points and $Z = m D$ for some $m \geq 1$, as $D$ is an integral curve. By Theorem P5.5.6, $Z \cdot X_y = \deg(\mathcal{L}|_{X_y}) = \deg \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}(1) = 1$, and $D \cdot X_y = \deg(\mathcal{O}_X(X_y)|_D) = \deg \sigma^* \pi^* \mathcal{O}_C(y) = 1$, as $\mathcal{O}_X(X_y) = \pi^* \mathcal{O}_C(y)$. So $m = 1$ and $\mathcal{O}_X(D) \cong \mathcal{L}$. A section exists by (2), so $X$ is geometrically ruled. $\square$

*Proof of Theorem P5.9.4.* (1) Let $\mathcal{L}$ be invertible on $X$. By Proposition P5.9.3, $\pi$ is projective and flat, so by Theorem P4.5.13 the function $y \mapsto \chi(X_y, \mathcal{L}_y)$ is locally constant on $C$, hence constant, and so is the degree $d = \chi(\mathcal{L}_y) - 1$ of $\mathcal{L}$ on the closed fibres. Put $\mathcal{L}' = \mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{O}_X(-d)$. On every closed fibre $\mathcal{L}'_y \cong \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}$, so $h^0(\mathcal{L}'_y) = 1$ and $h^1(\mathcal{L}'_y) = 0$. By Theorem P4.5.13 the sets where $h^1 \geq 1$ and where $h^0 \geq 2$ are closed, and they contain no closed point, so they are empty, as every nonempty closed subset of $C$ has a closed point. As $\chi$ is constant, $h^0 = 1$ and $h^1 = 0$ at every point of $C$, including the generic point. By Corollary P4.5.15, $R^1 \pi_* \mathcal{L}' = 0$, and by Grauert's theorem P4.5.16, $\mathcal{P} = \pi_* \mathcal{L}'$ is invertible with $\mathcal{P} \otimes \kappa(y) \cong H^0(X_y, \mathcal{L}'_y)$. The natural map $\pi^* \mathcal{P} \to \mathcal{L}'$ restricts on each closed fibre to the evaluation $H^0(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}) \otimes \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1} \to \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}$, which is surjective. By Nakayama's lemma its cokernel has no closed point in its support, so it vanishes, and a surjection of invertible sheaves is an isomorphism. So $\mathcal{L} \cong \mathcal{O}_X(d) \otimes \pi^* \mathcal{P}$. If $\mathcal{O}_X(d) \otimes \pi^* \mathcal{P} \cong \mathcal{O}_X$, then $d = 0$ by restriction to a fibre, and $\mathcal{P} \cong \pi_* \pi^* \mathcal{P} \cong \pi_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_C$ by the projection formula (Proposition P4.4.10) and Proposition P5.9.3. So the sum is direct and $\pi^*$ is injective.

(2) Since $\mathcal{O}_X(X_y) = \pi^* \mathcal{O}_C(y)$ and $\pi^* \mathcal{O}_C(y)|_{X_{y'}}$ is trivial, $X_y \cdot X_{y'} = 0$ for all closed $y, y'$, by Theorem P5.5.6; in particular $f^2 = 0$, and $h \cdot f = \deg \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}(1) = 1$. By (2) of Proposition P5.9.3 there is a section $D$, corresponding to $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N}$ with kernel $\mathcal{M}$, and $D \sim h - \pi^* \mathcal{M}$ by part 3 of the same proposition. Identifying $D$ with $C$ by $\pi$, the restriction of $\pi^* \mathcal{P}$ to $D$ is $\mathcal{P}$ and the restriction of $\mathcal{O}_X(1)$ is $\mathcal{N}$. Let $\deg \mathcal{P} = 0$. Then $\pi^* \mathcal{P} \cdot X_y = 0$ and $\pi^* \mathcal{P} \cdot D = \deg \mathcal{P} = 0$, and $h = D + \pi^* \mathcal{M}$ while $\pi^* \mathcal{M}$ is a combination of fibres, so $\pi^* \mathcal{P} \equiv 0$ by (1). Every $\mathcal{M}$ of degree $m$ is $\mathcal{O}_C(m y) \otimes \mathcal{P}$ with $\deg \mathcal{P} = 0$, so $\pi^* \mathcal{M} \equiv m f$. Now $D^2 = \deg \mathcal{O}_X(D)|_D = \deg(\mathcal{N} \otimes \mathcal{M}^{-1}) = \deg \mathcal{N} - \deg \mathcal{M}$, and $\deg \mathcal{E} = \deg \mathcal{M} + \deg \mathcal{N}$ gives $D^2 = 2 \deg \mathcal{N} - \deg \mathcal{E}$. Then $h^2 = (D + \deg \mathcal{M} \cdot f)^2 = D^2 + 2 \deg \mathcal{M} = \deg \mathcal{N} + \deg \mathcal{M} = \deg \mathcal{E}$. By (1) every class is $a h + \pi^* \mathcal{M} \equiv a h + b f$, and $h$, $f$ are independent in $\operatorname{Num}(X)$, since $(a h + b f) \cdot f = a$ and $(a h + b f) \cdot h = a \deg \mathcal{E} + b$. The Gram matrix in the basis $h, f$ has determinant $-1$, so the form is unimodular.

(3) By (1), $\omega_X \cong \mathcal{O}_X(a) \otimes \pi^* \mathcal{P}$ for some $a$ and $\mathcal{P}$. A fibre $X_y \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ has $X_y^2 = 0$, so the genus formula (Theorem P5.5.14) gives $-2 = K_X \cdot X_y = a$. Let $D$ be a section as in (2). By the adjunction formula, part 1 of Theorem P5.5.14,
$$\omega_C \cong (\omega_X \otimes \mathcal{O}_X(D))|_D \cong (\mathcal{O}_X(-1) \otimes \pi^*(\mathcal{P} \otimes \mathcal{M}^{-1}))|_D \cong \mathcal{N}^{-1} \otimes \mathcal{P} \otimes \mathcal{M}^{-1},$$
so $\mathcal{P} \cong \omega_C \otimes \mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{N} \cong \omega_C \otimes \det \mathcal{E}$, which has degree $2g - 2 + \deg \mathcal{E}$. By (2), $K_X \equiv -2h + (2g - 2 + \deg \mathcal{E}) f$, $K_X \cdot f = -2$, and $K_X^2 = 4 \deg \mathcal{E} - 4(2g - 2 + \deg \mathcal{E}) = 8(1 - g)$. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition P5.9.5.* (1) The morphism $\pi$ is surjective, as its closed fibres are nonempty and its image is closed, and $X$ is integral, so $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}$ is a torsion free module over the discrete valuation ring $\mathcal{O}_{C, \pi(x)}$, and $\pi$ is flat by Proposition P3.13.4. It is projective, as the composite of the graph $X \to X \times C$, a closed immersion, with a closed immersion $X \times C \to \mathbb{P}^N \times C$. Let $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{O}_X(D)$. By Theorem P4.5.13, $\deg \mathcal{L}|_{X_y} = \chi(\mathcal{L}_y) - 1$ is constant on $C$, and it equals $D \cdot X_y = 1$ at one point by Theorem P5.5.6, so $\mathcal{L}_y \cong \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}(1)$ on every closed fibre, with $h^0 = 2$ and $h^1 = 0$. As in the proof of Theorem P5.9.4, semicontinuity extends this to all points of $C$, and Corollary P4.5.15 and Theorem P4.5.16 show that $\mathcal{E} = \pi_* \mathcal{L}$ is locally free of rank $2$ with $\mathcal{E}(y) \cong H^0(X_y, \mathcal{L}_y)$. The map $\pi^* \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{L}$ is surjective on every closed fibre, since $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}(1)$ is globally generated, so it is surjective. By Theorem P3.6.34 it defines a morphism $\varphi \colon X \to \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$ over $C$ with $\varphi^* \mathcal{O}(1) \cong \mathcal{L}$. On the fibre over $y$, $\varphi$ is the morphism $X_y \to \mathbb{P}(H^0(\mathcal{L}_y))$ given by the complete linear system $|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}(1)|$, which is an isomorphism $\mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^1$. So for every closed point $z$ of $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$ over $y$, $\varphi^{-1}(z) = \varphi_y^{-1}(z)$ is one reduced point. The morphism $\varphi$ is projective, as $X \to \mathbb{P}^N_C \times_C \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$ is a closed immersion, and it has finite fibres, so it is finite by Corollary P4.6.17. By Lemma P5.9.2, $\varphi$ is an isomorphism. If $X$ is geometrically ruled with a section $\sigma$, take $D = \sigma(C)$, with $D \cdot X_y = \deg \sigma^* \mathcal{O}_C(y) = 1$.

(2) If $\mathcal{E}' \cong \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}$, this is part 2 of Proposition P3.6.35. Conversely, let $\psi \colon \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}') \to \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$ be an isomorphism over $C$, with projections $\pi'$ and $\pi = \pi' \psi^{-1}$. The sheaf $\psi^* \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1)$ has degree $1$ on the fibres, so by Theorem P5.9.4 it is $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}')}(1) \otimes \pi'^* \mathcal{L}$ for an invertible $\mathcal{L}$. Applying $\pi'_*$ and using the projection formula and Proposition P5.9.3,
$$\mathcal{E} \cong \pi_* \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1) \cong \pi'_* \psi^* \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1) \cong \pi'_* \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}')}(1) \otimes \mathcal{L} \cong \mathcal{E}' \otimes \mathcal{L}.$$
So $\mathcal{E}' \cong \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{-1}$. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition P5.9.7.* (1) A nonzero section of $\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}$ is an injection $\mathcal{L}^{-1} \to \mathcal{E}$, since $\mathcal{E}$ is torsion free. Fix a sequence $0 \to \mathcal{M}_0 \to \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N}_0 \to 0$ as in Proposition P5.9.3. For an invertible subsheaf $\mathcal{K} \subseteq \mathcal{E}$, either $\mathcal{K} \subseteq \mathcal{M}_0$ or $\mathcal{K} \to \mathcal{N}_0$ is nonzero, and a nonzero map of invertible sheaves on $C$ is a nonzero section of $\mathcal{K}^{-1} \otimes \mathcal{M}_0$ or $\mathcal{K}^{-1} \otimes \mathcal{N}_0$, so $\deg \mathcal{K} \leq \max(\deg \mathcal{M}_0, \deg \mathcal{N}_0)$. Let $\mu$ be the largest degree of an invertible subsheaf and $\mathcal{K}$ one of degree $\mu$. Then $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{K}^{-1}) \neq 0$, and if $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{K}^{-1} \otimes \mathcal{L}) \neq 0$ with $\deg \mathcal{L} < 0$, then $\mathcal{K} \otimes \mathcal{L}^{-1}$ is an invertible subsheaf of degree $\mu - \deg \mathcal{L} > \mu$, which is impossible. So $\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{K}^{-1}$ is normalized. If $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{E}'$ are normalized with $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}) \cong \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}')$, then $\mathcal{E}' \cong \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}$ by Proposition P5.9.5. As $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}) \neq 0$, $\deg \mathcal{L} \geq 0$, and as $H^0(\mathcal{E}' \otimes \mathcal{L}^{-1}) \neq 0$, $\deg \mathcal{L} \leq 0$. So $\deg \mathcal{L} = 0$ and $\deg \mathcal{E}' = \deg \mathcal{E} + 2 \deg \mathcal{L} = \deg \mathcal{E}$.

(2) A nonzero section is an injection $\mathcal{O}_C \to \mathcal{E}$. As in the proof of part 2 of Proposition P5.9.3 its saturation $\mathcal{K} \supseteq \mathcal{O}_C$ is an invertible subsheaf with invertible quotient. If $\mathcal{K} \neq \mathcal{O}_C$, then $\mathcal{K}/\mathcal{O}_C$ is a nonzero sheaf of finite length and $\deg \mathcal{K} > 0$, so $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{K}^{-1}) \neq 0$ with $\deg \mathcal{K}^{-1} < 0$, which is impossible. So $0 \to \mathcal{O}_C \to \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N} \to 0$ with $\mathcal{N} \cong \det \mathcal{E}$ invertible of degree $-e$. The section $C_0$ of the quotient $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{N}$ has $\mathcal{O}_X(C_0) \cong \mathcal{O}_X(1)$ by Proposition P5.9.3 and $C_0^2 = \deg \mathcal{N} - 0 = -e$ by Theorem P5.9.4.

(3) Let $\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{L}_1 \oplus \mathcal{L}_2$ be normalized, with $H^0(\mathcal{L}_1) \neq 0$, so $\deg \mathcal{L}_1 \geq 0$. Then $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}_1^{-1}) \supseteq H^0(\mathcal{O}_C) \neq 0$, so $\deg \mathcal{L}_1 \leq 0$. So $\deg \mathcal{L}_1 = 0$ and $\mathcal{L}_1 \cong \mathcal{O}_C$, as an invertible sheaf of degree $0$ with a nonzero section is trivial. If $\deg \mathcal{L}_2 > 0$, then $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}_2^{-1}) \neq 0$, again impossible, so $\deg \mathcal{L}_2 \leq 0$, and $e = -\deg \mathcal{L}_2$. Conversely, for $\deg \mathcal{L} \leq 0$ and $\deg \mathcal{L}' < 0$, $H^0(\mathcal{L}') \oplus H^0(\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{L}') = 0$, while $H^0(\mathcal{O}_C \oplus \mathcal{L}) \neq 0$.

(4) Let $\mathcal{L}$ have degree $-1$. Then $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}) = 0$, so $\chi(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}) \leq 0$. From $0 \to \mathcal{L} \to \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L} \to \mathcal{N} \otimes \mathcal{L} \to 0$, the additivity of $\chi$ (Proposition P4.3.2) and the Riemann-Roch theorem P5.1.10,
$$\chi(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{L}) = (-1 + 1 - g) + (-e - 1 + 1 - g) = -e - 2g,$$
so $e \geq -2g$. Tensoring the sequence of (2) with $\mathcal{N}^{-1}$ gives $0 \to \mathcal{N}^{-1} \to \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{N}^{-1} \to \mathcal{O}_C \to 0$. By Serre duality (Theorem P4.7.21), $h^1(\mathcal{N}^{-1}) = h^0(\omega_C \otimes \mathcal{N})$, and $\deg(\omega_C \otimes \mathcal{N}) = 2g - 2 - e < 0$ if $e > 2g - 2$. Then $H^0(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{N}^{-1}) \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_C)$ is surjective, and a section mapping to $1$ is a map $\mathcal{N} \to \mathcal{E}$ splitting the quotient, so $\mathcal{E} \cong \mathcal{O}_C \oplus \mathcal{N}$. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition P5.9.8.* By Proposition P5.9.7 and Theorem P5.9.4, $C_0 \equiv h$, so $\operatorname{Num}(X) = \mathbb{Z} C_0 \oplus \mathbb{Z} f$ with the stated numbers. (1) Let $Y \equiv a C_0 + b f$ be integral, not $C_0$ and not a fibre. The fibres are integral, so $Y$ is contained in no fibre, $\pi(Y) = C$, and $Y$ meets every fibre in a finite nonempty set. So $a = Y \cdot f > 0$ by Proposition P5.5.5. As $Y \neq C_0$, $0 \leq Y \cdot C_0 = -ae + b$.

(2) For $Y$ as in (1), $Y^2 = -a^2 e + 2ab \geq -a^2 e + 2a^2 e = a^2 e$, which is positive if $e > 0$. Fibres have square $0$ and $C_0^2 = -e < 0$.

(3) If $D$ is ample, then $D \cdot f = a > 0$ and $D \cdot C_0 = b - ae > 0$ by Lemma P5.5.20. Conversely let $a > 0$ and $b > ae$. Then $D \cdot f = a > 0$, $D \cdot C_0 = b - ae > 0$, $D^2 = a(2b - ae) > 0$ since $2b > 2ae \geq ae$, and for $Y \equiv a' C_0 + b' f$ as in (1), $D \cdot Y = a(b' - a' e) + a' b > 0$, since $b' - a' e \geq 0$, $a' > 0$ and $b > 0$. By the Nakai-Moishezon criterion (Theorem P5.12.5), $D$ is ample. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition P5.9.9.* (1) By Proposition P5.9.5 a geometrically ruled surface over $\mathbb{P}^1$ is $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})$, and by Grothendieck's theorem P5.4.10, $\mathcal{E} \cong \mathcal{O}(a_1) \oplus \mathcal{O}(a_2)$ with $a_1 \geq a_2$. Twisting by $\mathcal{O}(-a_1)$ gives $\mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(-n)$ with $n = a_1 - a_2 \geq 0$, which is normalized by part 3 of Proposition P5.9.7, with $e = n$; twisting by $\mathcal{O}(n)$ gives $\mathcal{O}(n) \oplus \mathcal{O} \cong \mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(n)$. So $X \cong F_n$, and $n = e$ is determined by $X$ by part 1 of Proposition P5.9.7. For $F_n$ itself, $\mathcal{E} = \mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(n)$ and the normalized bundle is $\mathcal{E}(-n)$, whose section $C_0$ is the section of the quotient $\mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(-n) \to \mathcal{O}(-n)$, which is the quotient $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{O}$ of $\mathcal{E}$ after the twist. This is the section $b = 0$ of Example P3.6.37, that is, $C_-$.

(2) The section $C_-$ corresponds to $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{O}$ with kernel $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{O}(n)$, so $C_-^2 = 0 - n = -n$ and $\mathcal{O}(C_-) \cong \mathcal{O}(1) \otimes \pi^* \mathcal{O}(-n)$ by Proposition P5.9.3 and Theorem P5.9.4. So $h \sim C_- + nF \sim C_+$, using part 3 of Theorem P3.11.25. As $\operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb{P}^1) = \mathbb{Z}$, Theorem P5.9.4 gives $\operatorname{Pic}(F_n) = \mathbb{Z} h \oplus \mathbb{Z} F = \mathbb{Z} C_- \oplus \mathbb{Z} F$, which maps isomorphically onto $\operatorname{Num}(F_n)$. Then $C_+^2 = -n + 2n = n$, and by part 3 of Theorem P5.9.4 with $g = 0$, $\deg \mathcal{E} = n$, $K \sim -2h + (n - 2)F = -2C_- - (n + 2)F$, with $K^2 = 4(-n) + 4(n + 2) = 8$.

(3) Intersection numbers of integral curves are preserved by isomorphisms of surfaces, so the smallest self-intersection $\mu(X)$ of an integral curve on $X$ is an invariant. On $F_n$ with $n \geq 1$, $\mu = -n$ by part 2 of Proposition P5.9.8. On $F_0$, every integral curve other than $C_0$ and the fibres has $Y^2 = 2ab \geq 0$, so $\mu = 0$. So $n = -\mu(F_n)$.

(4) $-K \equiv 2C_- + (n + 2)F$, and by part 3 of Proposition P5.9.8 it is ample if and only if $n + 2 > 2n$, that is, $n \leq 1$. Finally $-K \cdot C_- = -2n + n + 2 = 2 - n$. $\square$

*Proof of Proposition P5.9.10.* (1) On $V_0 \cap V_1$ the point $([a : b], t)$ of $F_n$ is $([a : t^n b], s)$ in the chart $V_1$, and the formula on $V_1$ sends it to $([\lambda'(s) a : t^n b], s)$. A point of $F_c \setminus \{x\}$ has $b \neq 0$ and maps to $[0 : b] = [0 : 1]$ over $c$, which is $x' = C_+ \cap F_c$.

It pulls back to $(v, rv) = (v)$ on $B$, to the unit ideal on $A$, whose image $\operatorname{Spec} k[r_1, t]$ misses $x'$, and to $(tw, t) = (t)$ on $\beta^{-1}(\operatorname{Spec} k[w, t]) = \operatorname{Spec} k[w, t]$, where $\varepsilon(w, t) = (tw, t)$; away from $F_c$ it is the unit ideal. The composites $\delta \gamma$ and $\gamma \delta$ are the identity on the dense open complements of the fibres over $c$, and the blow-ups are integral and separated, so both composites are the identity. In chart $B$, $\gamma$ is $(r, v) \mapsto (w_1, t) = (v, rv)$, which is the chart $t = w_1 \cdot r$ of $\operatorname{Bl}_{x'}$ with coordinates $(w_1, r)$, and it maps $E_x = V(r)$ onto the strict transform $V(r)$ of the fibre $t = 0$ through $x'$. The strict transform of $C_- = V(r)$ in chart $A$ is $V(u)$, which maps to $V(r_1) = C_-$ of $F_{n+1}$.

(3) For a constant $\alpha \in k$, the formulas $([a : b], t) \mapsto ([a + \alpha b : b], t)$ on $V_0$ and $([a' : b'], s) \mapsto ([a' + \alpha s^m b' : b'], s)$ on $V_1$ agree on the overlap, as $a + \alpha b = a' + \alpha s^m (t^m b)$ with $a' = a$, $b' = t^m b$. Mathlib has the Proj construction of a graded ring (`AlgebraicGeometry.Proj`), but neither the projective bundle of a locally free sheaf nor the intersection pairing on a surface, so none of the statements of this section can be stated there yet. The handbook adds no `MinimalModel` node here.

### Notes and history

Ruled surfaces, the surfaces swept out by a one-parameter family of lines, were studied by Corrado Segre in 1886 and 1889, who found the invariant $e$ as the minimal degree of a directrix curve. Hirzebruch introduced the surfaces $F_n$ in 1951 as the complex manifolds $\Sigma_n$ and showed that $\Sigma_n$ and $\Sigma_m$ are diffeomorphic exactly when $n \equiv m \pmod 2$, while they are biholomorphic only for $n = m$. The treatment by normalized bundles is Hartshorne, Section V.2, and Proposition P5.9.7 is his Proposition V.2.8. The bound $e \geq -2g$ is weak: Nagata proved in 1970 that $e \geq -g$ for every ruled surface of genus $g$, and Example P5.9.13 shows that the bound is attained for $g = 1$. By Atiyah's classification of vector bundles on an elliptic curve (1957), the indecomposable normalized bundles of rank $2$ on an elliptic curve have $e = 0$ or $e = -1$, so the ruled surfaces over an elliptic curve are those of Examples P5.9.12 and P5.9.13 and the one of the nonsplit extension of $\mathcal{O}_C$ by $\mathcal{O}_C$. Elementary transformations were used systematically by Nagata and Maruyama to classify ruled surfaces up to birational equivalence over the base. The literature state is theorem. In the language of Volume 2, $\pi \colon \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}) \to C$ is the contraction of the extremal ray spanned by the class $f$, with $K \cdot f = -2$, and so it is a Mori fibre space over a curve; the elementary transformation of Proposition P5.9.10 is the simplest example of a Sarkisov link between two Mori fibre spaces.
