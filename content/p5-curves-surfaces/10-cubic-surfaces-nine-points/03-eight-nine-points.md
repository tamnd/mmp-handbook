## The plane blown up in eight and nine points

### Statement

The notation of Section 10.1 and Section 10.2 is in force: $X_r$ is the blow-up of $r$ distinct points of $\mathbb{P}^2$, $\operatorname{Pic}(X_r) = I^{1,r}$ with $K = k_r = -3\ell + \sum E_i$, and an exceptional class is a class with $D^2 = K \cdot D = -1$. Since the form on $I^{1,r}$ is unimodular, a numerically trivial class is $0$, so $\operatorname{Pic}(X_r) = \operatorname{Num}(X_r)$, and $N^1(X_r) = \operatorname{Pic}(X_r) \otimes \mathbb{R} \cong \mathbb{R}^{r+1}$. A class $x$ is *nef* if $x \cdot C \geq 0$ for every curve $C$ (Definition P5.8.22). The class $\ell$ is nef: $\ell \cdot E_i = 0$, and for any other integral curve $C$ a line not containing $\pi(C)$ pulls back to a member of $|\ell|$ not containing $C$. For $r = 9$ the canonical class satisfies $K^2 = 0$.

**Proposition P5.10.17 (exceptional classes for $r \leq 9$).** Let $r \leq 9$ and $P_1, \dots, P_r$ be any distinct points.

1. Every exceptional class $D = a\ell - \sum_i b_i E_i$ has $a \geq 0$, and $a = 0$ if and only if $D = E_i$ for some $i$.
2. Every exceptional class is effective.
3. For $r = 7$ there are exactly $56$ exceptional classes: $7$, $21$, $21$, $7$ with $a = 0, 1, 2, 3$, the last ones being $3\ell - 2E_i - \sum_{j \neq i} E_j$. For $r = 8$ there are exactly $240$, with $8, 28, 56, 56, 56, 28, 8$ classes for $a = 0, 1, \dots, 6$:
$$E_i, \quad \ell - E_i - E_j, \quad 2\ell - \textstyle\sum_5 E, \quad 3\ell - 2E_i - \sum_6 E, \quad 4\ell - 2\sum_3 E - \sum_5 E, \quad 5\ell - 2\sum_6 E - \sum_2 E, \quad 6\ell - 3E_i - 2\sum_{j \neq i} E_j,$$
where $\sum_m E$ is a sum of $m$ distinct $E_j$, the index sets in each class being disjoint and covering at most $\{1, \dots, 8\}$. The map $D \mapsto D + k_8$ is a bijection from the exceptional classes onto the $240$ roots of $I^{1,8}$.
4. For $r = 9$ and $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, the classes
$$D_n = E_9 - n(E_1 - E_2) - n^2 k_9 = 3n^2 \ell - (n^2 + n) E_1 - (n^2 - n) E_2 - n^2 (E_3 + \dots + E_8) - (n^2 - 1) E_9$$
are pairwise distinct exceptional classes. So $X_9$ has infinitely many exceptional classes, and $D_1 = 3\ell - 2E_1 - E_3 - \dots - E_8$.

**Proposition P5.10.18 (the del Pezzo surface of degree one).** Let $X = X_8$ and assume that $-K$ is ample, which holds for points in general position by Theorem P5.9.26.

1. Each exceptional class contains exactly one effective divisor, and it is a $(-1)$-curve. So $X$ contains exactly $240$ $(-1)$-curves.
2. $h^0(-K) = 2$, and every member of $|-K|$ is an integral curve of arithmetic genus $1$.
3. Two distinct members of $|-K|$ meet in exactly one point $x_0$, transversally, and $x_0$ is the only base point of $|-K|$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

So $|-K|$ is a pencil of curves of arithmetic genus $1$ through one point, and blowing up $x_0$ separates them. The rest of the section studies the resulting surface, starting from a pencil of plane cubics.

**Definition P5.10.19 (a cubic pencil with nine distinct base points).** Let $F_0$ and $F_\infty$ be cubic forms whose curves $C_0$ and $C_\infty$ have no common component, and let $C_{[s:t]} = V(sF_0 + tF_\infty)$ for $[s : t] \in \mathbb{P}^1$ be the *pencil* they span. Its *base points* are the points of $C_0 \cap C_\infty$, which lie on every member. The pencil *satisfies (H)* if it has nine distinct base points $P_1, \dots, P_9$ and every member is integral.

**Lemma P5.10.20 (pencils with nine distinct base points).** Let the pencil $C_t$ have nine distinct base points $P_1, \dots, P_9$.

1. Two distinct members have no common component and meet exactly in $P_1, \dots, P_9$, transversally. Every member is smooth at every $P_i$, and for each line $T$ through $P_i$ exactly one member is tangent to $T$ at $P_i$.
2. Every member is integral if and only if no three of the $P_i$ lie on a line and no six lie on a conic.
3. If every member is integral, the cubic forms vanishing at $P_1, \dots, P_9$ are exactly the forms $sF_0 + tF_\infty$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P5.10.21 (the rational elliptic surface).** Let the pencil satisfy (H) and let $X = X_9$ be the blow-up of its base points.

1. $h^0(-K) = 2$, the members of $|-K|$ are the strict transforms $\tilde C_t$, any two of them are disjoint, and $|-K|$ is base point free.
2. The morphism $f \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ defined by $|-K|$ has the fibres $f^{-1}(t) = \tilde C_t \cong C_t$, integral curves of arithmetic genus $1$. The fibre class is $F = -K$, with $F^2 = K \cdot F = 0$.
3. Each $E_i$ is a section of $f$: the restriction $f|_{E_i} \colon E_i \to \mathbb{P}^1$ is an isomorphism, which sends a tangent direction $T$ at $P_i$ to the member tangent to $T$.
4. The $t$ with $C_t$ singular form a closed subset of $\mathbb{P}^1$. So if one member is smooth, all but finitely many fibres of $f$ are smooth curves of genus $1$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

A *section* of $f$ is an integral curve $S \subseteq X$ such that $f|_S \colon S \to \mathbb{P}^1$ is an isomorphism.

**Theorem P5.10.22 (the $(-1)$-curves are the sections).** In the situation of Theorem P5.10.21:

1. An integral curve $S \subseteq X$ is a $(-1)$-curve if and only if $F \cdot S = 1$, if and only if it is a section of $f$.
2. Each exceptional class contains exactly one effective divisor, and it is a section. So the sections, the $(-1)$-curves and the exceptional classes of $X$ correspond to each other.
3. The quotient $k_9^\perp / \mathbb{Z} k_9$ is a free abelian group of rank $8$, its form is negative definite, even and unimodular, and it is isometric to $k_8^\perp \subseteq I^{1,8}$. The map $D \mapsto [D - E_9]$ is a bijection from the exceptional classes onto $k_9^\perp / \mathbb{Z} k_9$. In particular $X$ contains infinitely many $(-1)$-curves, among them the curves in the classes $D_n$ of Proposition P5.10.17.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P5.10.23 (the nef cone is not polyhedral).** In the situation of Theorem P5.10.21, the nef cone $\operatorname{Nef}(X) \subseteq N^1(X) \cong \mathbb{R}^{10}$ is a closed convex cone with nonempty interior that is not generated by finitely many vectors. The rays of the classes $D_n$ converge to the ray of $F = -K$: $D_n / (3n^2) \to -\frac{1}{3} K$ as $|n| \to \infty$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

### Proof in words

Riemann-Roch gives $\chi(D) = 1$ for every exceptional class, and $K - D$ is not effective because it has negative degree on lines, so exceptional classes are effective for $r \leq 9$. When $r \leq 8$ the form on $K^\perp$ is negative definite and there are finitely many of them; for $r = 9$, $K^2 = 0$ and $K$ itself lies in $K^\perp$, so adding multiples of $K$ to an exceptional class and correcting by a vector of $K^\perp$ produces infinitely many.

For a cubic pencil satisfying (H), the anticanonical system of $X_9$ consists of the strict transforms of the cubics of the pencil, which are disjoint after blowing up the nine base points. So $-K$ is the fibre class of a fibration $f \colon X_9 \to \mathbb{P}^1$ in curves of genus $1$. A $(-1)$-curve $C$ has $F \cdot C = -K \cdot C = 1$, so it is a section, and conversely. Every exceptional class is effective and the fibres are integral, so its effective representative is a section plus fibres, and $D^2 = -1$ rules out the fibres. The sections then correspond to the vectors of the $E_8$ lattice $K^\perp / \mathbb{Z} K$, which is the Mordell-Weil group of the generic fibre. Every $(-1)$-curve cuts out a face of maximal dimension of the nef cone, and infinitely many distinct such faces are impossible for a polyhedral cone.

### Examples

**Example P5.10.24 (nine points on a smooth cubic, and an explicit pencil).** Let $C$ be a smooth plane cubic with a flex $O$, used as the origin of the group law of Example P5.1.17, and $P_1, \dots, P_9 \in C$ distinct. Then there is a cubic $C' \neq C$ with $C \cap C' = \{P_1, \dots, P_9\}$ if and only if $P_1 + \dots + P_9 = 0$ in the group, and in that case the pencil spanned by $C$ and $C'$ satisfies (H) if and only if no three of the $P_i$ have sum $0$. Indeed, $3O \sim H|_C$, so the divisor $P_1 + \dots + P_9$ is linearly equivalent to $3H|_C \sim 9O$ exactly when $\sum (P_i - O) \sim 0$, that is, when $\sum P_i = 0$ under the isomorphism $C \cong \operatorname{Pic}^0(C)$ of Proposition P5.1.13. Since $H^1(\mathbb{P}^2, \mathcal{O}) = 0$ by Theorem P4.2.1, restriction $H^0(\mathbb{P}^2, \mathcal{O}(3)) \to H^0(C, \mathcal{O}_C(3))$ is surjective, so a divisor of $|3H|_C|$ is cut out by a cubic, which is not a multiple of the equation of $C$. In the same way, using $H^1(\mathbb{P}^2, \mathcal{O}(-1)) = 0$, six points of $C$ lie on a conic if and only if their sum is $0$, and three points are collinear if and only if their sum is $0$ (Example P5.1.17). If $\sum_{i=1}^9 P_i = 0$, six of the points have sum $0$ exactly when the other three do. By part 2 of Lemma P5.10.20 this gives the criterion. For an explicit case let $k$ have characteristic $0$, and let $C$ be $y^2 z = x^3 + 17 z^3$ with the flex $O = [0 : 1 : 0]$, whose tangent line $z = 0$ meets $C$ only at $O$. The affine points
$$(-1, 4), \ (2, 5), \ (2, -5), \ (4, 9), \ (8, 23), \ (43, -282), \ (52, 375), \ (52, -375), \ \left(-\tfrac{967}{1936}, \tfrac{349933}{85184}\right)$$
lie on $C$, the ninth being minus the sum of the first eight. An exact computation over $\mathbb{Q}$ shows that no three of them have sum $0$, and, as an independent check, that no three are collinear and no six lie on a conic, and that the cubic forms vanishing at the nine points form a $2$-dimensional space over $\mathbb{Q}$, spanned by $y^2 z - x^3 - 17 z^3$ and a second form with rational coefficients. So these nine points are the base points of a pencil satisfying (H).

**Example P5.10.25 (the section $D_1$).** In the situation of Theorem P5.10.21, the class $D_1 = 3\ell - 2E_1 - E_3 - \dots - E_8$ is a section $S$ by Theorem P5.10.22. It is not an $E_i$, so it is the strict transform of the integral plane curve $N = \pi(S)$, and by Proposition P5.6.4 the degree of $N$ is $S \cdot \ell = 3$ and its multiplicity at $P_i$ is $S \cdot E_i$. So $N$ is the unique cubic with a double point at $P_1$ that passes through $P_3, \dots, P_8$, and it misses $P_2$ and $P_9$. On a smooth fibre $\tilde C_t \cong C_t$, use $P_9$ as the origin of the group law. The curve $S$ meets $\tilde C_t$ in one point $x_t$, since $S \cdot F = 1$, and $E_i$ meets it in the point over $P_i$. So the cubic $N$ cuts on $C_t$ the divisor $x_t + 2P_1 + P_3 + \dots + P_8$, the cubic $C_0$ cuts $P_1 + \dots + P_9$, and the two divisors are linearly equivalent. Hence $x_t + P_1 \sim P_2 + P_9$, that is, $x_t = P_2 - P_1$ in the group $(C_t, P_9)$. Under the bijection of Theorem P5.10.22, $D_1$ goes to $[D_1 - E_9] = [E_2 - E_1]$, and the section $E_i$ goes to $[E_i - E_9]$: the lattice difference matches the difference of points on every smooth fibre. Shioda's theory of Mordell-Weil lattices, cited in the Notes, makes this into a group isomorphism.

**Example P5.10.26 (a nef anticanonical class that is not semiample).** Let $C$ be a smooth plane cubic with a flex $O$ as origin, let $R \in C$ be a point of infinite order, and choose distinct points $P_1, \dots, P_9 \in C$ with $\sum P_i = R$: choose $P_1, \dots, P_8$ freely and $P_9 = R - (P_1 + \dots + P_8)$, which differs from $P_1, \dots, P_8$ for all but finitely many choices of $P_8$. Let $X = X_9$. Since $C$ is smooth through all nine points, its strict transform $\tilde C \cong C$ has class $3\ell - \sum E_i = -K$. It is integral with $\tilde C^2 = 0$, so $-K$ is nef. The restriction $N = \mathcal{O}_X(-K)|_{\tilde C}$ is, on $C$, the sheaf $\mathcal{O}_C(3H - \sum P_i) \cong \mathcal{O}_C(9O - \sum P_i)$, whose class in $\operatorname{Pic}^0(C) \cong C$ is $-R$. So $N^{\otimes m}$ corresponds to $-mR \neq 0$ and has no nonzero section for $m \geq 1$, since a nonzero section of a degree $0$ invertible sheaf on an integral curve has no zeros and trivializes it. From the exact sequences
$$0 \to \mathcal{O}_X(-(m-1)K) \to \mathcal{O}_X(-mK) \to N^{\otimes m} \to 0$$
and induction, $h^0(-mK) = 1$ for all $m \geq 0$. So $m\tilde C$ is the only member of $|-mK|$, every $|-mK|$ with $m \geq 1$ has base locus $\tilde C$, and $-K$ is nef but not semiample. Such points exist over $k = \overline{\mathbb{Q}}$: on $y^2 = x^3 + 17$ the point $R = (-1, 4)$ has $2R = (\frac{137}{64}, -\frac{2651}{512})$, which has non-integral coordinates, so $R$ has infinite order by the Nagell-Lutz theorem (Silverman, Corollary VIII.7.2). Over $\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$ every point of $C$ is torsion, because $C(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p)$ is the union of the finite groups $C(\mathbb{F}_{p^n})$, and the construction fails: then $-mK$ moves in a pencil for $m$ the order of $R$, the Halphen pencil of index $m$.

**Example P5.10.27 (the Hesse pencil).** Let the characteristic of $k$ be different from $3$, $\omega$ a primitive cube root of $1$, and consider the pencil spanned by $x^3 + y^3 + z^3$ and $xyz$. Its base points are the nine points $[0 : 1 : -\zeta]$, $[1 : 0 : -\zeta]$, $[1 : -\zeta : 0]$ with $\zeta^3 = 1$, which are distinct. Four members are triangles: $xyz$, and for $\lambda^3 = 1$,
$$x^3 + y^3 + z^3 - 3\lambda xyz = \prod_{\epsilon^3 = 1} (x + \epsilon \lambda y + \epsilon^2 z),$$
which follows from the case $\lambda = 1$ by substituting $\lambda y$ for $y$. So the pencil does not satisfy (H). Each of the $12$ lines contains exactly three base points: it meets another member in $3$ points, which are base points, and the three lines of a triangle carry all nine. Their strict transforms on $X_9$ have classes $\ell - E_a - E_b - E_c$, square $-2$ and $K \cdot C = 0$, so they are $(-2)$-curves. The Fermat member $x^3 + y^3 + z^3$ is smooth, hence integral, so part 3 of Lemma P5.10.20 holds with $C_0$ the Fermat cubic, and the proofs of parts 1 and 3 of Theorem P5.10.21 and of the first sentence of part 2 go through: $f \colon X_9 \to \mathbb{P}^1$ is a fibration whose general fibre is a smooth cubic and which has four fibres that are triangles of $(-2)$-curves. The proof of Theorem P5.10.22 breaks down because a vertical curve need not be a whole fibre, and its conclusion fails. Let $L$ be one of the four lines among the $12$ that pass through $P_1$, containing the base points $P_1, P_b, P_c$. The class $\ell - E_b - E_c$ is exceptional, but $L$ is the only line through $P_b$ and $P_c$, so the only effective divisor in the class is $\tilde L + E_1$, which is not a $(-1)$-curve. By Oguiso and Shioda the Mordell-Weil group of this fibration is $(\mathbb{Z}/3)^2$, so its sections are exactly $E_1, \dots, E_9$.

### Proof

*Proposition P5.10.17.* The conditions are $\sum b_i = 3a - 1$ and $\sum b_i^2 = a^2 + 1$, so as in the proof of Theorem P5.10.5
$$\sum_i b_i (b_i - 1) = (a - 1)(a - 2), \qquad (3a - 1)^2 \leq r (a^2 + 1)$$
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

(1) If $a = -c$ with $c \geq 1$, then $(3c + 1)^2 \leq 9(c^2 + 1)$ gives $6c \leq 8$, so $c = 1$, and then $\sum b_i = -4$ while $\sum b_i^2 = 2$, which is impossible since $|\sum b_i| \leq \sum |b_i| \leq \sum b_i^2$ for integers. If $a = 0$, then $\sum b_i^2 = 1$ and $\sum b_i = -1$, so $D = E_i$.

(2) By part 3 of Theorem P5.6.3, applied $r$ times, $\chi(\mathcal{O}_{X_r}) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2}) = 1$. Theorem P5.5.13 gives $h^0(D) + h^0(K - D) \geq 1 + \frac{1}{2} D \cdot (D - K) = 1 + \frac{1}{2}(-1 + 1) = 1$. If $K - D$ were effective, then $\ell \cdot (K - D) \geq 0$ because $\ell$ is nef, but $\ell \cdot (K - D) = -3 - a < 0$ by part 1. So $h^0(D) \geq 1$.

(3) The values of $b(b - 1)$ on integers are $0$ for $b \in \{0, 1\}$, $2$ for $b \in \{-1, 2\}$, $6$ for $b \in \{-2, 3\}$, $12$ for $b \in \{-3, 4\}$ and $20$ for $b \in \{-4, 5\}$. For $r = 7$ the inequality is $a^2 - 3a - 3 \leq 0$, so $a \leq 3$. For $a = 0, 1, 2$ the argument of Theorem P5.10.5 gives $E_i$, $\ell - E_i - E_j$ and $2\ell - \sum_5 E$. For $a = 3$ one $b_i$ lies in $\{-1, 2\}$ and the others in $\{0, 1\}$, with sum $8$; the value $-1$ would need the six others to sum to $9$, so $b_i = 2$ and the six others are $1$. This gives $7 + 21 + 21 + 7 = 56$. For $r = 8$ the inequality is $(a + 1)(a - 7) \leq 0$, so $a \leq 7$, and $a = 7$ would force equality in Cauchy-Schwarz, that is, all $b_i = 20/8$, which is not an integer. Let $N = (a - 1)(a - 2)$ and $\Sigma = 3a - 1$, so $\sum_i b_i(b_i - 1) = N$ and $\sum_i b_i = \Sigma$. An entry with $b(b - 1) = 0, 2, 6, 12, 20$ is at most $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ respectively, so a distribution of $N$ into the values $b_i(b_i - 1)$ bounds $\sum b_i$ by the sum of these maxima, and the following cases exhaust $0 \leq a \leq 6$.

- $a = 0$: $N = 2$, $\Sigma = -1$. One entry is $-1$ and the others $0$, since an entry $2$ would leave the others summing to $-3$. This gives $8$ classes.
- $a = 1, 2$: all entries in $\{0, 1\}$ with sum $2$ or $5$: $28$ and $56$ classes.
- $a = 3$: $N = 2$, $\Sigma = 8$. One entry in $\{-1, 2\}$, the rest in $\{0, 1\}$. The entry $-1$ would need the other $7$ to sum to $9$. So one entry is $2$, and six of the other seven are $1$: $8 \cdot 7 = 56$ classes.
- $a = 4$: $N = 6$, $\Sigma = 11$. Either one entry is in $\{-2, 3\}$ and the rest in $\{0, 1\}$, with sum at most $3 + 7 = 10$, or three entries are in $\{-1, 2\}$ and five in $\{0, 1\}$, with sum $11$ only if the three are $2$ and the five are $1$. This gives $\binom{8}{3} = 56$ classes.
- $a = 5$: $N = 12$, $\Sigma = 14$. The possible distributions of $12$ are $12$, $6 + 6$, $6 + 2 + 2 + 2$ and six times $2$, with maximal sums $4 + 7 = 11$, $3 + 3 + 6 = 12$, $3 + 6 + 4 = 13$ and $12 + 2 = 14$. So six entries are $2$ and two are $1$: $\binom{8}{2} = 28$ classes.
- $a = 6$: $N = 20$, $\Sigma = 17$. The distributions of $20$ into at most $8$ parts from $\{2, 6, 12, 20\}$ are $20$, $12 + 6 + 2$, $12 + 2 \cdot 4$, $6 \cdot 3 + 2$, $6 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 4$ and $6 + 2 \cdot 7$, with maximal sums $5 + 7$, $4 + 3 + 2 + 5$, $4 + 8 + 3$, $9 + 2 + 4$, $6 + 8 + 2$ and $3 + 14$, that is $12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 17$. So one entry is $3$ and the other seven are $2$: $8$ classes.

The total is $8 + 28 + 56 + 56 + 56 + 28 + 8 = 240$. For $r = 8$, $k_8^2 = 1$. If $D$ is exceptional, then $(D + k_8)^2 = -1 - 2 + 1 = -2$ and $(D + k_8) \cdot k_8 = -1 + 1 = 0$, so $D + k_8$ is a root, and if $\alpha$ is a root, then $(\alpha - k_8)^2 = -2 + 1 = -1$ and $(\alpha - k_8) \cdot k_8 = -1$. So $D \mapsto D + k_8$ is a bijection onto the roots, and there are $240$ roots. A computer enumeration of the integer solutions confirms the counts for $r = 7$ and $r = 8$.

(4) Let $v = -n(E_1 - E_2)$ and $c = -n^2$, so $D_n = E_9 + v + c k_9$, with $v \cdot k_9 = 0$, $v^2 = -2n^2$, $E_9 \cdot v = 0$, $E_9 \cdot k_9 = -1$ and $k_9^2 = 0$. Then
$$D_n^2 = -1 + v^2 + 2c \, (E_9 \cdot k_9) = -1 - 2n^2 + 2n^2 = -1, \qquad D_n \cdot k_9 = E_9 \cdot k_9 = -1 .$$
Expanding $-n^2 k_9 = 3n^2 \ell - n^2 \sum E_i$ gives the displayed formula. The coefficients of $\ell$ and $E_1$ determine $n$, so the classes are distinct. $\square$

*Proposition P5.10.18.* (1) By Proposition P5.10.17 an exceptional class $D$ has an effective member $D' = \sum m_j C_j$. By Lemma P5.5.20, $-K \cdot C_j \geq 1$ for every component, and $\sum m_j (-K \cdot C_j) = -K \cdot D = 1$. So $D' = C$ is an integral curve with $C^2 = -1$, which by Example P5.5.11 is the only member of $|D|$, and it is a $(-1)$-curve. The class of a $(-1)$-curve is exceptional, so there are $240$ of them by part 3 of Proposition P5.10.17.

(2) By Theorem P5.5.13, $h^0(-K) + h^0(2K) \geq 1 + \frac{1}{2}(-K) \cdot (-2K) = 1 + K^2 = 2$, and $h^0(2K) = 0$ because $\ell \cdot 2K = -6 < 0$ and $\ell$ is nef. So $h^0(-K) \geq 2$. Every member $C$ of $|-K|$ is integral by the argument of part 1, since $(-K) \cdot (-K) = 1$ and every component $C_j$ has $-K \cdot C_j \geq 1$, and $2p_a(C) - 2 = C \cdot (C + K) = 0$ by Theorem P5.5.14, so $p_a(C) = 1$. Fix such a $C$. The sequence $0 \to \mathcal{O}_X \to \mathcal{O}_X(-K) \to \mathcal{O}_C(-K) \to 0$ gives $h^0(-K) \leq 1 + h^0(C, \mathcal{L})$ for $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{O}_C(-K)$, of degree $-K \cdot C = 1$. By Theorem P5.5.14, $\omega^\circ_C \cong \mathcal{O}_C(K + C) \cong \mathcal{O}_C$, and Theorem P5.3.10 gives $h^0(\mathcal{L}) - h^0(\mathcal{L}^{-1}) = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1$. The sheaf $\mathcal{L}^{-1}$ has negative degree on the integral curve $C$, so it has no nonzero section, and $h^0(\mathcal{L}) = 1$. Hence $h^0(-K) = 2$.

(3) Two distinct members $C$ and $C'$ are integral, so they have no common component, and $C \cdot C' = K^2 = 1$. By Proposition P5.5.5 they meet in exactly one point $x_0$, transversally. Every member is the zero locus of a combination of the two sections defining $C$ and $C'$, both of which vanish at $x_0$, so every member contains $x_0$, and no other point lies on both $C$ and $C'$. $\square$

*Lemma P5.10.20.* (1) A common component of two distinct members would be a common component of all members, since their equations span the pencil, and in particular of $C_0$ and $C_\infty$. For distinct members $C_s, C_t$, Bézout's theorem (Example P5.5.8) gives $9 = \sum_P (C_s \cdot C_t)_P$, and each base point contributes at least $1$. So there are no other common points and every local term is $1$, which by Proposition P5.5.5 means that both members are smooth at $P_i$ with distinct tangent lines. Choose affine coordinates centred at $P_i$ and write $f_0$ and $f_\infty$ for the dehomogenized equations. Their linear parts $\lambda_0$ and $\lambda_\infty$ are linearly independent, since the tangent lines of $C_0$ and $C_\infty$ differ, and the tangent line of $C_{[s:t]}$ at $P_i$ is $V(s\lambda_0 + t\lambda_\infty)$. The map $[s : t] \mapsto [s\lambda_0 + t\lambda_\infty]$ is a bijection from $\mathbb{P}^1$ onto the lines through $P_i$.

(2) Suppose a member is $L + Q$ with $L$ a line. Choose a different member $C'$, which does not contain $L$ or any component of $Q$. By Bézout, $L \cap C'$ has at most $3$ points and $Q \cap C'$ at most $6$, and these contain all nine base points. So exactly three base points lie on $L$ and six on $Q$. Conversely, if three base points lie on a line $L$, choose $R \in L$ not a base point and a member $C_t$ through $R$, which exists because vanishing at $R$ is one linear condition on $[s : t]$. Then $C_t$ meets $L$ in at least $4$ points, so it contains $L$ by Bézout, and $C_t$ is not integral. If six base points lie on a conic $Q$ and no three are collinear, then $Q$ is integral, and the member through a further point of $Q$ meets $Q$ in at least $7 > 6$ points, so it contains $Q$ and is not integral.

(3) Let $G$ be a cubic form vanishing at the nine points. The member $C_0$ is integral by assumption. Choose a point $R \in C_0$ that is not a base point, so $F_0(R) = 0$ and $F_\infty(R) \neq 0$, and put $G' = G - (G(R)/F_\infty(R)) F_\infty$. If $G' \neq 0$, its curve passes through the nine base points and $R$, ten points of $C_0$, so by Bézout it has a common component with the integral curve $C_0$, hence contains $C_0$, and $G'$ is a multiple of $F_0$ by degree. So $G$ lies in the span of $F_0$ and $F_\infty$. $\square$

*Theorem P5.10.21.* (1) As in Section 10.1, the members of $|-K|$ are the divisors $\pi^* C - \sum_i E_i$ for cubics $C$ through the nine points, and by part 3 of Lemma P5.10.20 these cubics are the members $C_t$. By part 1 of Lemma P5.10.20 each $C_t$ is smooth at every $P_i$, so $\pi^* C_t - \sum E_i = \tilde C_t$ by Proposition P5.6.4, and $h^0(-K) = 2$. Two distinct strict transforms have no common component and $\tilde C_s \cdot \tilde C_t = K^2 = 9 - 9 = 0$, so they are disjoint by Proposition P5.5.5. Every point of $X$ lies on some member, since vanishing at a point is one linear condition, and two members have no common point, so $|-K|$ has no base point.

(2) By Theorem P3.6.21 the two sections define a morphism $f \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^1$ with $f^* \mathcal{O}(1) \cong \mathcal{O}_X(-K)$, whose fibre over a point is the zero divisor of the corresponding section, that is, a member $\tilde C_t$. By part 5 of Proposition P5.6.4, applied at each $P_i$, $\pi$ restricts to an isomorphism $\tilde C_t \to C_t$, and $p_a(C_t) = 1$ for a plane cubic. The fibre class is $F = -K$, and $F^2 = K^2 = 0$, $K \cdot F = -K^2 = 0$.

(3) The restriction $f|_{E_i}$ has degree $F \cdot E_i = 3\ell \cdot E_i - \sum_j E_j \cdot E_i = 1$, so it is a finite morphism of degree $1$ between smooth projective curves, hence an isomorphism. The point of $E_i$ given by $T$ lies on $\tilde C_t$ exactly when $T$ is the tangent line of $C_t$ at $P_i$, by Proposition P5.6.4.

(4) The set $Z = \{(x, t) \in \mathbb{P}^2 \times \mathbb{P}^1 : x \text{ is a singular point of } C_t\}$ is closed, defined by the vanishing of the three partial derivatives of $sF_0 + tF_\infty$, which for a cubic also implies the vanishing of the form by Euler's formula when the characteristic is not $3$, and in general by adding the equation $sF_0 + tF_\infty = 0$. Its image in $\mathbb{P}^1$ is closed because $\mathbb{P}^2$ is proper. A proper closed subset of $\mathbb{P}^1$ is finite. $\square$

*Theorem P5.10.22.* (1) Let $S$ be an integral curve with $F \cdot S = 1$. Then $f|_S$ is not constant, so it is a finite surjective morphism $S \to \mathbb{P}^1$ of degree $\deg (f^* \mathcal{O}(1))|_S = F \cdot S = 1$, hence birational. By Proposition P5.1.2 its inverse rational map extends to a morphism $g \colon \mathbb{P}^1 \to S$. The composites $f \circ g$ and $g \circ f|_S$ are the identity on dense open subsets of reduced separated schemes, hence everywhere, so $f|_S$ is an isomorphism and $S$ is a section. A section $S \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ has $p_a(S) = 0$ and $F \cdot S = \deg \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}(1) = 1$, so $K \cdot S = -1$ and the genus formula gives $S^2 = -2 + 1 = -1$: it is a $(-1)$-curve. A $(-1)$-curve $C$ has $F \cdot C = -K \cdot C = 1$.

(2) Let $D$ be exceptional, with an effective member $D' = \sum m_j C_j$ by Proposition P5.10.17. The class $F$ is base point free, so $F \cdot C_j \geq 0$ for each $j$, and $\sum m_j F \cdot C_j = F \cdot D = -K \cdot D = 1$. So exactly one component $S$ has $F \cdot S = 1$, with multiplicity $1$, and the others have $F \cdot C_j = 0$. A curve with $F \cdot C_j = 0$ is mapped by $f$ to a point, so it lies in a fibre, which is integral, and $C_j$ is that fibre. So $D' = S + mF$ with $m \geq 0$ and $S$ a section by part 1. Then $-1 = D^2 = S^2 + 2m \, S \cdot F + m^2 F^2 = -1 + 2m$, so $m = 0$ and $D' = S$. By Example P5.5.11, $S$ is the only member of $|D|$.

(3) Write $k = k_9$. For $x = a\ell - \sum b_i E_i$, $x^2 + x \cdot k = a(a - 3) - \sum b_i(b_i - 1)$ is even, so $x^2$ is even for $x \in k^\perp$. Identify $I^{1,8}$ with the classes of $I^{1,9}$ without $E_9$. For such $x$, $x \cdot k_9 = x \cdot k_8$, so $k_8^\perp \subseteq k^\perp$. The induced map $k_8^\perp \to k^\perp / \mathbb{Z} k$ is injective, because $k$ has coefficient $1$ at $E_9$, and surjective, because for $y \in k^\perp$ with coefficient $-b_9$ at $E_9$, $y + b_9 k$ has no $E_9$ term and still lies in $k^\perp$. Since $k \cdot k^\perp = 0$, the form descends to $k^\perp / \mathbb{Z} k$, and the map is an isometry. The lattice $k_8^\perp$ has rank $8$, is negative definite by the Cauchy-Schwarz argument of Proposition P5.10.11 with $r = 8$, where $x^2 \leq a^2 (1 - 9/8)$, and is unimodular: since $k_8^2 = 1$, every $x \in I^{1,8}$ is $(x - (x \cdot k_8) k_8) + (x \cdot k_8) k_8$, so $I^{1,8} = k_8^\perp \oplus \mathbb{Z} k_8$ and $\det(k_8^\perp) = \pm \det(I^{1,8}) = \pm 1$. The roots $\ell - E_1 - E_2 - E_3, E_1 - E_2, \dots, E_7 - E_8$ lie in $k_8^\perp$, and their Gram matrix is the negative of the Cartan matrix of $E_8$, of determinant $1$, so they span a sublattice of index $1$, that is, a basis. For an exceptional class $D$, $(D - E_9) \cdot k = -1 + 1 = 0$. Given $v \in k^\perp$, the class $D = E_9 + v + ck$ has $D \cdot k = -1$ and $D^2 = -1 + v^2 + 2E_9 \cdot v - 2c$, so $D$ is exceptional for $c = \frac{1}{2} v^2 + E_9 \cdot v$, an integer since $v^2$ is even. So the map is surjective. If $D$ and $D' = D + mk$ are both exceptional, then $-1 = D'^2 = D^2 + 2m \, D \cdot k = -1 - 2m$, so $m = 0$, and the map is injective. Since $k^\perp / \mathbb{Z} k \cong \mathbb{Z}^8$ is infinite, so is the set of $(-1)$-curves, by part 2. $\square$

*Theorem P5.10.23.* The nef cone is an intersection of closed half-spaces, so it is a closed convex cone. The classes $\ell$ and $\ell - E_i$ are nef: for $\ell - E_i$, for every integral curve $C$ other than $E_i$ some member $\tilde M + \sum_{j \neq i, \, P_j \in M} E_j$ of $|\ell - E_i|$ does not contain $C$, where $M$ is a line through $P_i$ chosen not to be a component of $\pi(C)$ and, when $C = E_j$, not through $P_j$, while $(\ell - E_i) \cdot E_i = 1$. These ten classes form a basis of $N^1(X)$, since $E_i = \ell - (\ell - E_i)$, so $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$ has nonempty interior.

For a $(-1)$-curve $C$ consider the linear map $p_C(x) = x + (x \cdot C) C$, which is the identity on the hyperplane $C^\perp$ and satisfies $p_C(x) \cdot C = x \cdot C - x \cdot C = 0$. If $x$ is nef, then for every integral curve $C' \neq C$, $p_C(x) \cdot C' = x \cdot C' + (x \cdot C)(C \cdot C') \geq 0$, and $p_C(x) \cdot C = 0$, so $p_C(x)$ is nef. So $p_C$ maps $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$ into $\operatorname{Nef}(X) \cap C^\perp$, and since $p_C$ maps $N^1(X)$ onto $C^\perp$ and $\operatorname{Nef}(X)$ spans $N^1(X)$, the cone $\operatorname{Nef}(X) \cap C^\perp$ spans the hyperplane $C^\perp$. For distinct $(-1)$-curves $C \neq C'$ the hyperplanes differ: $C^\perp = C'^\perp$ would give $C' = \lambda C$ in $N^1(X)$, since the form is nondegenerate, then $\lambda^2 = 1$ from the squares and $\lambda = 1$ from $F \cdot C = F \cdot C' = 1$, so $C$ and $C'$ would have the same class, against part 2 of Theorem P5.10.22.

Suppose $\sigma = \operatorname{Nef}(X)$ were generated by finitely many vectors. By Fulton, *Introduction to toric varieties*, Section 1.2, its dual cone $\sigma^\vee$ is then generated by finitely many linear forms $u_1, \dots, u_N$, and $\sigma = (\sigma^\vee)^\vee = \{x : u_j(x) \geq 0 \text{ for all } j\}$. For a $(-1)$-curve $C$, the form $u = (\,\cdot\, C)$ lies in $\sigma^\vee$, so $u = \sum_j c_j u_j$ with $c_j \geq 0$. On $\sigma \cap C^\perp$ the sum $\sum_j c_j u_j$ vanishes with nonnegative terms, so every $u_j$ with $c_j > 0$ vanishes on $\sigma \cap C^\perp$, hence on its span $C^\perp$, and $\ker u_j = C^\perp$ for some $j$. So the hyperplanes $C^\perp$ would be among the finitely many hyperplanes $\ker u_j$, which contradicts part 3 of Theorem P5.10.22 and the previous paragraph. For the last statement, $D_n / (3n^2) = \ell - \frac{n^2 + n}{3n^2} E_1 - \frac{n^2 - n}{3n^2} E_2 - \frac{1}{3}(E_3 + \dots + E_8) - \frac{n^2 - 1}{3n^2} E_9$, which tends to $\ell - \frac{1}{3} \sum E_i = -\frac{1}{3} K$. $\square$

1. (A) Check directly that $D_2 = 12\ell - 6E_1 - 2E_2 - 4(E_3 + \dots + E_8) - 3E_9$ satisfies $D_2^2 = K \cdot D_2 = -1$, and show that $D_n \cdot D_m = (n - m)^2 - 1$ for all $n, m$.

3. (B) For $r = 8$, show that $D \mapsto -2K - D$ permutes the $240$ exceptional classes and changes the coefficient $a$ of $\ell$ into $6 - a$, in accordance with the counts of Proposition P5.10.17.
4. (B) In the situation of Theorem P5.10.21, show that every integral curve $C$ with $C^2 < 0$ is a $(-1)$-curve. So $X$ contains no $(-2)$-curves, in contrast with Example P5.10.27.
5. (B) Let $X = X_8$ with $-K$ ample, and let $\sigma \colon Y \to X$ be the blow-up of the base point $x_0$ of $|-K|$ (Proposition P5.10.18), with exceptional curve $E$. Show that $|-\sigma^* K - E| = |-K_Y|$ is a base point free pencil whose members are the strict transforms of the members of $|-K|$, that it defines a morphism $Y \to \mathbb{P}^1$ with integral fibres of arithmetic genus $1$, and that $E$ is a section.
6. (C) In the situation of Theorem P5.10.21, let $S_1, S_2, \dots$ be pairwise distinct $(-1)$-curves. Writing $S_n = E_9 + v_n + c_n K$ with $v_n \in k_8^\perp$, show that $c_n = \frac{1}{2} v_n^2$ and that the rays $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} S_n$ converge to the ray of $-K$. Deduce that $-K$ spans the only accumulation ray of the rays of $(-1)$-curves.

### Notes and history

The pencil of cubics through eight points and its ninth base point are classical, and Halphen studied the pencils of curves of degree $3m$ with nine $m$-fold base points, which are the case of torsion in Example P5.10.26. Nagata proved in *On rational surfaces II* (1960) that the blow-up of $r \geq 9$ points in general position has infinitely many $(-1)$-curves, by the Cremona action on the classes, and his counterexample to Hilbert's fourteenth problem (1959) rests on the related statement that for $r = s^2 \geq 16$ very general points every plane curve of degree $d$ with multiplicities $m_i$ at the points satisfies $d > \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}} \sum m_i$. The Nagata conjecture asserts the same inequality for all $r \geq 10$ very general points. It is open for every $r \geq 10$ that is not a perfect square. The stronger Segre-Harbourne-Gimigliano-Hirschowitz conjecture, which predicts the dimension of every linear system $|d\ell - \sum m_i E_i|$ for very general points, is also open. The theorems of this section are proved, and the literature state is theorem for them; the Nagata conjecture and the SHGH conjecture are open. Shioda's theory of Mordell-Weil lattices (1990) identifies the group of sections of a rational elliptic surface with integral fibres with the $E_8$ lattice, compatibly with Theorem P5.10.22 and Example P5.10.25, and Oguiso and Shioda (1991) classified the Mordell-Weil lattices of all rational elliptic surfaces, including the group $(\mathbb{Z}/3)^2$ of the Hesse pencil. Example P5.10.26 is a variant of Zariski's example of a nef divisor that is not semiample (Lazarsfeld, *Positivity I*, Section 2.3). Hu and Keel defined Mori dream spaces in 2000; their nef cones are polyhedral, so by Theorem P5.10.23 the surface $X_9$ is not a Mori dream space. The cone theorem, Theorem P5.12.14, describes the $K$-negative part of the cone of curves, and on $X_9$, as in Example P5.12.18, that part is spanned by infinitely many $(-1)$-curves whose rays accumulate only at the ray of $-K$ on the hyperplane $K^\perp$.
