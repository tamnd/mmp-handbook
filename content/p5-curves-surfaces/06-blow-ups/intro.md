# Blow-ups of surfaces

The blow-up of a point $p$ on a smooth surface $S$ replaces $p$ by a projective line $E$, the line of tangent directions of $S$ at $p$, and changes nothing else. It is the basic birational operation in dimension $2$. Chapter 7 proves that every birational morphism of smooth projective surfaces is a composite of blow-ups of points. So the numerical invariants of a blow-up, computed in this chapter, are the numerical invariants of every birational morphism of smooth surfaces.

Section 6.1 computes the blow-up $\pi \colon \tilde S \to S$ of a point in terms of Chapter 5. The surface $\tilde S$ is smooth and projective, $\operatorname{Pic}(\tilde S) = \pi^* \operatorname{Pic}(S) \oplus \mathbb{Z} E$ is an orthogonal sum with $E^2 = -1$, and $K_{\tilde S} = \pi^* K_S + E$, so $K^2$ drops by $1$ while $\chi(\mathcal{O})$ and all the $h^i(\mathcal{O})$ stay the same. A curve $C$ with multiplicity $m$ at $p$ has total transform $\tilde C + m E$, and the strict transform has $\tilde C^2 = C^2 - m^2$ and $p_a(\tilde C) = p_a(C) - m(m - 1)/2$. Linear systems on $\tilde S$ are linear systems on $S$ with an assigned multiplicity at $p$. Section 6.2 uses the drop of the arithmetic genus to resolve the singularities of a curve on a surface by blowing up points, and then to make its total transform a curve with simple normal crossings. The same bookkeeping gives the $\delta$-invariant of a plane curve singularity as a sum over its infinitely near points, and Noether's formula for intersection multiplicities.

In the minimal model program, the exceptional curve $E$ is the model of a $K$-negative extremal curve: $K_{\tilde S} \cdot E = -1$ and $E^2 = -1$, and Chapter 7 shows that every curve with these two numbers is the exceptional curve of a blow-up. The formula $K_{\tilde S} = \pi^* K_S + E$, with coefficient $1$, is the first discrepancy computation, and the embedded resolution of Section 6.2 is the surface case of the log resolutions on which the definitions of Volume 4 rest.

The chapter follows Hartshorne, Sections V.3 and V.4, Beauville, Chapter II, and the second chapter of Reid's *Chapters on algebraic surfaces*.
