## Castelnuovo's contractibility criterion

### Statement

In this chapter $k$ is algebraically closed, $X$, $Y$ and $S$ denote smooth projective surfaces over $k$ unless stated otherwise, and the notation of Chapters 5 and 6 is in force. For an integral curve $E$ on $X$, $\mathcal{I}_E = \mathcal{O}_X(-E)$ is its ideal sheaf and $nE$ is the closed subscheme defined by $\mathcal{I}_E^n$. For a closed point $y \in Y$, $\mathcal{I}_y$ is its ideal sheaf and $\mathfrak{m}_y \subseteq \mathcal{O}_{Y,y}$ its maximal ideal.

**Definition P5.7.1 ($(-1)$-curves and contractions).** A *$(-1)$-curve* on $X$ is an integral curve $E \cong \mathbb{P}^1_k$ with $E^2 = -1$. A *contraction* of an integral curve $E \subseteq X$ is a morphism $\psi \colon X \to Y$ to a smooth projective surface $Y$ together with a closed point $y \in Y$ and an isomorphism $X \cong \operatorname{Bl}_y Y$ over $Y$ that carries $E$ onto the exceptional curve. Then $\psi$ is also called the *blow-down* of $E$, and $E$ is *contractible*.

By the genus formula (Theorem P5.5.14), a $(-1)$-curve has $K_X \cdot E = -2 - E^2 = -1$. By part 2 of Corollary P5.5.15, an integral curve with $E^2 < 0$ and $K_X \cdot E < 0$ is a $(-1)$-curve. By Theorem P5.6.2 the exceptional curve of the blow-up of a point is a $(-1)$-curve, so a contractible curve is a $(-1)$-curve. The theorem of this section is the converse. Its proof uses two lemmas, one about morphisms with $\psi_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_Y$ and one about the infinitesimal neighbourhoods of a $(-1)$-curve.

**Lemma P5.7.2 (factoring through a contraction).** Let $\psi \colon X' \to Y'$ be a projective morphism of Noetherian schemes with $\psi_* \mathcal{O}_{X'} = \mathcal{O}_{Y'}$, and let $f \colon X' \to Z$ be a morphism of schemes such that $f(\psi^{-1}(y))$ is a single point for every $y \in Y'$. Then there is exactly one morphism $h \colon Y' \to Z$ with $f = h \circ \psi$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Lemma P5.7.3 (the formal neighbourhood of a $(-1)$-curve).** Let $E$ be a $(-1)$-curve on $X$ and $\mathcal{I} = \mathcal{I}_E$.

1. $\mathcal{I}^n / \mathcal{I}^{n+1} \cong \mathcal{O}_E(n)$ for $n \geq 0$, and the restriction maps $H^0(\mathcal{O}_{(n+1)E}) \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$ and $H^0(\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^{n+1}) \to H^0(\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^n)$ are surjective.
2. Let $A = \varprojlim_n H^0(nE, \mathcal{O}_{nE})$. Choose $u, v \in \varprojlim_n H^0(\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^n) \subseteq A$ whose images in $H^0(\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^2) \cong H^0(\mathcal{O}_E(1))$ form a basis. Then there is a unique ring homomorphism $\alpha \colon k[[U, V]] \to A$ with $U \mapsto u$, $V \mapsto v$ that is compatible with the maps to all $H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$, and it is an isomorphism. It induces isomorphisms $k[[U, V]]/(U, V)^n \cong H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$ for every $n \geq 1$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P5.7.4 (Castelnuovo's contractibility criterion).** Let $E$ be a $(-1)$-curve on a smooth projective surface $X$.

1. There are a smooth projective surface $Y$, a closed point $y_0 \in Y$ and a projective morphism $\psi \colon X \to Y$ with $\psi_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_Y$ and $\psi^{-1}(y_0) = E$, which restricts to an isomorphism $X \setminus E \to Y \setminus \{y_0\}$.
2. $\mathcal{I}_{y_0} \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{I}_E$. The morphism $X \to \operatorname{Bl}_{y_0} Y$ over $Y$ given by the universal property of the blow-up (Theorem P3.11.9) is an isomorphism, and it carries $E$ onto the exceptional curve. So $\psi$ is a contraction of $E$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Corollary P5.7.5 (the contraction of a $(-1)$-curve).** Let $\psi \colon X \to Y$ and $y_0$ be as in Theorem P5.7.4.

1. (*Universal property.*) For every morphism of schemes $f \colon X \to Z$ such that $f(E)$ is a point, there is exactly one morphism $h \colon Y \to Z$ with $f = h \circ \psi$. If $\psi' \colon X \to Y'$ is a second contraction of $E$, there is exactly one isomorphism $u \colon Y \to Y'$ with $\psi' = u \circ \psi$.
2. $\operatorname{Pic}(X) = \psi^* \operatorname{Pic}(Y) \oplus \mathbb{Z} E$, an orthogonal sum with $\psi^* D \cdot \psi^* D' = D \cdot D'$. $\psi^*$ identifies $\operatorname{Pic}(Y)$ with $\{ D \in \operatorname{Pic}(X) : D \cdot E = 0 \}$, and $\operatorname{Num}(X) = \psi^* \operatorname{Num}(Y) \oplus \mathbb{Z} E$.
3. $K_X = \psi^* K_Y + E$, $K_X^2 = K_Y^2 - 1$, and $h^i(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^i(\mathcal{O}_Y)$ for every $i$.
4. A smooth projective surface is the blow-up of a smooth projective surface at a point if and only if it contains a $(-1)$-curve, if and only if it contains an integral curve $C$ with $C^2 < 0$ and $K \cdot C < 0$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

### Proof in words

To contract $E$ one needs a line bundle that is trivial on $E$ and ample away from it. Take $H$ very ample with $H^1(H) = 0$ and put $a = H \cdot E > 0$. Since $E^2 = -1$, the bundle $M = H + aE$ has degree $0$ on $E$, and the sequences $0 \to \mathcal{O}(H + (i-1)E) \to \mathcal{O}(H + iE) \to \mathcal{O}_E(a - i) \to 0$ show inductively that $H^1(H + iE) = 0$ for $0 \leq i \leq a$, so that the sections of $M$ restrict onto the constants on $E$. Then $|M|$ is base point free, it is constant on $E$, and the sections $s e^a$, with $s \in H^0(H)$ and $e$ the equation of $E$, separate the points of $X \setminus E$ from each other and from $E$. The Stein factorization of the resulting morphism is a contraction $\psi \colon X \to Y$ whose only positive-dimensional fibre is $E$.

The image point $y_0$ is smooth because its completed local ring is computed by formal functions: $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Y, y_0} = \varprojlim H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$. The successive quotients $\mathcal{I}^n / \mathcal{I}^{n+1}$ are $\mathcal{O}_E(n)$, because the conormal bundle of $E$ has degree $-E^2 = 1$, and they have no $H^1$. So the formal neighbourhood of $E$ looks exactly like that of the exceptional curve of the blow-up of $\mathbb{A}^2$: two sections of $\mathcal{O}_E(1)$ lift to formal functions $u, v$, and the monomials of degree $n$ in $u, v$ are a basis of $H^0(\mathcal{O}_E(n))$. This gives $k[[u, v]]$, a regular ring. Two local parameters at $y_0$ pull back to sections of $\mathcal{I}_E$ that generate $\mathcal{I}_E / \mathcal{I}_E^2 = \mathcal{O}_E(1)$, hence generate $\mathcal{I}_E$, and the universal property of the blow-up finishes the proof.

### Examples

**Example P5.7.6 (the plane blown up at a point).** Let $\pi \colon X = \operatorname{Bl}_p \mathbb{P}^2 \to \mathbb{P}^2$, with $\operatorname{Pic}(X) = \mathbb{Z} \ell \oplus \mathbb{Z} E$ as in Example P5.6.7. Every divisor on $X$ is $H = \alpha \ell - \beta E$, and $H \cdot E = \beta$. If $H$ is very ample, then $\beta = H \cdot E > 0$ by Lemma P5.5.20, and the divisor of the proof of Theorem P5.7.4 is
$$M = H + \beta E = \alpha \ell = \pi^* \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2}(\alpha).$$
By part 4 of Theorem P5.6.2, $H^0(X, M) = \pi^* H^0(\mathbb{P}^2, \mathcal{O}(\alpha))$, so the morphism $\varphi_{|M|}$ is $v_\alpha \circ \pi$, with $v_\alpha$ the Veronese embedding of degree $\alpha$ (Theorem P3.10.19). Its image is $v_\alpha(\mathbb{P}^2) \cong \mathbb{P}^2$, the finite part of the Stein factorization is the identity, and the construction returns $\psi = \pi$ and $Y = \mathbb{P}^2$, as Corollary P5.7.5 predicts. The ample classes are those with $\alpha > \beta > 0$, by Lemma P5.5.20 applied to $E$ and to the fibre class $\ell - E$; for instance $2\ell - E$ is the class of the conics through $p$, and with it $M = 2\ell$.

**Example P5.7.7 (the line through two blown-up points).** Let $p_1 = [1 : 0 : 0]$ and $p_2 = [0 : 1 : 0]$, and let $S_2 \to \mathbb{P}^2$ be the blow-up of both, with $\operatorname{Pic}(S_2) = \mathbb{Z}\ell \oplus \mathbb{Z} E_1 \oplus \mathbb{Z} E_2$ as in Example P5.6.8. The strict transform $L$ of the line $V(z) = \overline{p_1 p_2}$ has class $\ell - E_1 - E_2$ and is a $(-1)$-curve. Let $\psi \colon S_2 \to Y$ be its contraction (Theorem P5.7.4). The claim is that $Y \cong \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$.

The projection from $p_i$ extends to a morphism $\operatorname{Bl}_{p_i} \mathbb{P}^2 \to \mathbb{P}^1$ whose fibres are the strict transforms of the lines through $p_i$ (Theorem P3.11.17). Composing with the blow-down of the other point gives morphisms $\rho_1, \rho_2 \colon S_2 \to \mathbb{P}^1$, equal to $[y : z]$ and $[x : z]$ on $S_2 \setminus (E_1 \cup E_2 \cup L)$, with fibres of class $F_i = \ell - E_i$. Let $\rho = (\rho_1, \rho_2) \colon S_2 \to \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$. On the open set $z \neq 0$, which is $\mathbb{A}^2$ with coordinates $x, y$, $\rho(x, y) = ([y : 1], [x : 1])$ is an isomorphism onto $\mathbb{A}^1 \times \mathbb{A}^1$, so $\rho$ is birational.

An integral curve $C$ is contracted by $\rho_i$ if and only if $C \cdot F_i = 0$: if $\rho_i(C)$ is a point, a fibre over another point is disjoint from $C$, and if $\rho_i(C) = \mathbb{P}^1$, every fibre meets $C$ in a finite nonempty set, so $C \cdot F_i > 0$ by Proposition P5.5.5. So $C$ is contracted by $\rho$ if and only if $C \cdot (\ell - E_1) = C \cdot (\ell - E_2) = 0$. The curves $E_i$ have $E_i \cdot (\ell - E_i) = 1$. Any other $C$ is the strict transform of a plane curve of degree $d \geq 1$ with multiplicities $m_1, m_2$ at the two points, of class $d\ell - m_1 E_1 - m_2 E_2$ (Proposition P5.6.4), and the conditions say $m_1 = m_2 = d$. If $C \neq L$, then $C \cdot L = d - m_1 - m_2 = -d < 0$ contradicts Proposition P5.5.5. So $L$ is the only curve contracted by $\rho$, and $\rho(L) = ([1 : 0], [1 : 0])$.

By Corollary P5.7.5, $\rho = h \circ \psi$ for a morphism $h \colon Y \to \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$, birational because $\psi$ and $\rho$ are. If $h$ contracted an integral curve $C'$, the closure $C$ of $\psi^{-1}(C' \setminus \{y_0\})$ would be an integral curve different from $L$ contracted by $\rho$. So every fibre of $h$ is finite: a fibre of positive dimension contains an integral curve. The morphism $h$ is projective, as $Y$ is projective, and $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ is normal, so $h$ is an isomorphism by Corollary P4.6.10. Thus
$$\operatorname{Bl}_{p_1, p_2} \mathbb{P}^2 \cong \operatorname{Bl}_o (\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1), \qquad o = ([1 : 0], [1 : 0]),$$
with $L$ as the exceptional curve. The curve $E_1$ maps isomorphically by $\rho_1$ and to the point $\rho_2(p_1) = [1 : 0]$ by $\rho_2$, so $\psi(E_1) = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \{[1 : 0]\}$ is the fibre of the second projection through $o$; similarly $\psi(E_2)$ is the fibre of the first projection through $o$. So, as in Example P5.6.9, $E_1$ and $E_2$ are the strict transforms of the two fibres of the projections through the blown-up point $o$, two disjoint $(-1)$-curves, and $L$ is its exceptional curve. Section 7.2 proves the converse direction, that the projection of the quadric from a point is the blow-up of two points (Example P5.7.17).

**Example P5.7.8 (a $(-2)$-curve contracts to a singular point).** On the Hirzebruch surface $F_2$, the negative section $C_-$ is disjoint from the positive section $C_+ \sim C_- + 2F$ (Theorem P3.11.25), and $C_- \cdot F = 1$, so $C_-^2 = C_- \cdot C_+ - 2 C_- \cdot F = -2$. By Example P3.11.29, $F_2 \setminus C_+$ is the blow-up of the vertex of the cone $Q_0 = V(xy - z^2) \subseteq \mathbb{A}^3$, and $C_-$ is its exceptional curve. So $C_- \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ is contracted by a birational morphism to a point at which $\dim \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 3$ (Example P3.7.18): the image point is singular. In the proof of Theorem P5.7.4 the self-intersection enters only through $\mathcal{I}^n / \mathcal{I}^{n+1} \cong \mathcal{O}_E(-n E^2)$. For $C_-$ these quotients are $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}(2n)$, with $2n + 1$ sections, while $\mathfrak{m}^n / \mathfrak{m}^{n+1}$ of a regular local ring of dimension $2$ has dimension $n + 1$. Exercise 3 of Section 7.2 shows that no birational morphism from $F_2$ onto a smooth surface contracts $C_-$.

### Proof

*Lemma P5.7.2.* By Theorem P4.6.7 every fibre of $\psi$ is nonempty, so $\psi$ is surjective, and $\psi$ is closed because it is projective, hence proper. Define $h$ on points by $\{h(y)\} = f(\psi^{-1}(y))$. Then $f = h \circ \psi$ as maps of sets, so for every subset $T \subseteq Z$, $f^{-1}(T) = \psi^{-1}(h^{-1}(T))$ and, $\psi$ being surjective, $h^{-1}(T) = \psi(f^{-1}(T))$. If $T$ is closed, $f^{-1}(T)$ is closed and so is its image under the closed map $\psi$. So $h$ is continuous, and for $W \subseteq Z$ open, $\psi^{-1}(h^{-1}(W)) = f^{-1}(W)$. Define $h^\# \colon \mathcal{O}_Z(W) \to \mathcal{O}_{Y'}(h^{-1}(W))$ as the composite
$$\mathcal{O}_Z(W) \xrightarrow{f^\#} \mathcal{O}_{X'}(f^{-1}(W)) = \mathcal{O}_{X'}(\psi^{-1}(h^{-1}(W))) = (\psi_* \mathcal{O}_{X'})(h^{-1}(W)) \xrightarrow{(\psi^\#)^{-1}} \mathcal{O}_{Y'}(h^{-1}(W)).$$
It is a map of sheaves of rings, compatible with restrictions, and $\psi^\# \circ h^\# = f^\#$ by construction. For $y \in Y'$ with $z = h(y)$, and $s \in \mathfrak{m}_{Z, z}$, choose $x \in \psi^{-1}(y)$. The image of $h^\#(s)$ in $\mathcal{O}_{X', x}$ is $f^\#(s)$, which lies in $\mathfrak{m}_{X', x}$ since $f$ is a morphism of locally ringed spaces with $f(x) = z$. If $h^\#(s)$ were a unit of $\mathcal{O}_{Y', y}$, its image in $\mathcal{O}_{X', x}$ would be a unit. So $h^\#$ is local, $(h, h^\#)$ is a morphism of schemes, and $f = h \circ \psi$. If $h' \circ \psi = f$, then $h'$ agrees with $h$ on points because $\psi$ is surjective, and $\psi^\# \circ h'^\# = f^\# = \psi^\# \circ h^\#$ with $\psi^\# \colon \mathcal{O}_{Y'} \to \psi_* \mathcal{O}_{X'}$ an isomorphism, so $h' = h$. $\square$

*Lemma P5.7.3.* (1) The line bundle $\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^2 = \mathcal{O}_X(-E)|_E$ has degree $-E^2 = 1$ on $E \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ (Theorem P5.5.6), so it is $\mathcal{O}_E(1)$ by Corollary P3.9.11. As $\mathcal{I}$ is invertible, $\mathcal{I}^n / \mathcal{I}^{n+1} = \mathcal{I}^n \otimes \mathcal{O}_E \cong (\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^2)^{\otimes n} \cong \mathcal{O}_E(n)$, and the multiplication $\mathcal{I}^a/\mathcal{I}^{a+1} \otimes \mathcal{I}^b/\mathcal{I}^{b+1} \to \mathcal{I}^{a+b}/\mathcal{I}^{a+b+1}$ is the isomorphism $\mathcal{O}_E(a) \otimes \mathcal{O}_E(b) \cong \mathcal{O}_E(a + b)$. For $n \geq 0$ there are exact sequences
$$0 \to \mathcal{O}_E(n) \to \mathcal{O}_{(n+1)E} \to \mathcal{O}_{nE} \to 0, \qquad 0 \to \mathcal{O}_E(n) \to \mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^{n+1} \to \mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^n \to 0,$$
the second for $n \geq 1$. Since $H^1(\mathbb{P}^1, \mathcal{O}(n)) = 0$ for $n \geq 0$ (Theorem P4.2.1), the maps on $H^0$ are surjective. The sequences also show that $H^0(\mathcal{O}_{(n+1)E})$ is finite dimensional for all $n$, by induction.

(2) Write $R = k[[U, V]]$ and $\mathfrak{n} = (U, V)$, and let $u_n, v_n \in H^0(\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^n) \subseteq H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$ be the components of $u$ and $v$. A product of $j$ sections of $\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{I}^n$ is a section of $\mathcal{I}^j/\mathcal{I}^n$, which is $0$ for $j \geq n$. So $\alpha_n(\sum c_{ab} U^a V^b) = \sum_{a + b < n} c_{ab} u_n^a v_n^b$ is a ring homomorphism $R \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$ that kills $\mathfrak{n}^n$, the $\alpha_n$ are compatible with the restriction maps, and $\alpha = \varprojlim \alpha_n$ is the homomorphism of the statement. Any homomorphism compatible with the maps to all $H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$ that sends $U$ to $u$ and $V$ to $v$ has components that kill $\mathfrak{n}^n$, since $u^a v^b$ has component $0$ in $H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$ for $a + b \geq n$, and that agree with $\alpha_n$ on polynomials, hence on $R = k[U, V] + \mathfrak{n}^n$. So $\alpha$ is unique.

Let $\bar\alpha_n \colon R/\mathfrak{n}^n \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE})$ be the induced map. It is an isomorphism for $n = 1$, as $H^0(\mathcal{O}_E) = k$. Consider the commutative diagram with exact rows
$$\begin{array}{ccccccccc} 0 & \to & \mathfrak{n}^n / \mathfrak{n}^{n+1} & \to & R/\mathfrak{n}^{n+1} & \to & R/\mathfrak{n}^n & \to & 0 \\ & & \downarrow & & \downarrow \bar\alpha_{n+1} & & \downarrow \bar\alpha_n & & \\ 0 & \to & H^0(\mathcal{O}_E(n)) & \to & H^0(\mathcal{O}_{(n+1)E}) & \to & H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE}) & \to & 0, \end{array}$$
whose lower row is exact by part 1. The left vertical map sends $U^a V^b$, $a + b = n$, to $\bar u^a \bar v^b$, where $\bar u, \bar v \in H^0(\mathcal{O}_E(1))$ are the images of $u, v$, a basis by assumption. After a linear change of the homogeneous coordinates of $E = \mathbb{P}^1$, $\bar u$ and $\bar v$ are the two coordinates, and by Theorem P4.2.1 the monomials of degree $n$ in them are a basis of $H^0(\mathcal{O}_E(n))$. So the left map is an isomorphism, and by the five lemma and induction every $\bar\alpha_n$ is an isomorphism. Passing to the limit, $\alpha \colon R = \varprojlim R/\mathfrak{n}^n \to A$ is an isomorphism. $\square$

*Theorem P5.7.4.* *Step 1: the linear system.* Let $H_0$ be very ample on $X$. By Theorem P4.2.7 there is $n$ with $H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X(nH_0)) = 0$, and $H = nH_0$ is very ample by Lemma P5.5.1, applied with the globally generated sheaf $\mathcal{O}((n-1)H_0)$. Put $a = H \cdot E$, which is positive by Lemma P5.5.20, and $M = H + aE$. For $1 \leq i \leq a$ there is the exact sequence
$$0 \to \mathcal{O}_X(H + (i - 1)E) \to \mathcal{O}_X(H + iE) \to \mathcal{O}_E(H + iE) \to 0,$$
and $\mathcal{O}_E(H + iE)$ has degree $a - i \geq 0$ on $E \cong \mathbb{P}^1$, so $H^1(\mathcal{O}_E(H + iE)) = 0$ by Theorem P4.2.1. By induction on $i$, $H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X(H + iE)) = 0$ for $0 \leq i \leq a$. For $i = a$ the sequence shows that $H^0(X, M) \to H^0(E, \mathcal{O}_E(M))$ is surjective, and $\mathcal{O}_E(M)$ has degree $a - a = 0$, so it is $\mathcal{O}_E$ and its sections are the constants. So there is $s_0 \in H^0(M)$ that vanishes at no point of $E$.

Let $e \in H^0(\mathcal{O}_X(E))$ be the section with divisor $E$. For $s \in H^0(\mathcal{O}_X(H))$, the section $s e^a$ of $M$ has divisor $Z(s) + aE$. If $x \notin E$, choose $s$ with $s(x) \neq 0$; then $(s e^a)(x) \neq 0$. With $s_0$ this shows that $|M|$ is base point free. Let $\varphi \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^N$ be the morphism of $|M|$ (Theorem P3.10.9). The restrictions of the sections of $M$ to $E$ are constant multiples of one nowhere vanishing section, so $\varphi$ is constant on $E$, with value a point $P$. For $x \notin E$ and $s$ with $s(x) \neq 0$, the hyperplane of $s e^a$ contains $P$, since $s e^a$ vanishes on $E$, and does not contain $\varphi(x)$, by part 1 of Theorem P3.10.9. So $\varphi(x) \neq P$. For $x \neq x'$ in $X \setminus E$, the very ample $H$ gives $s$ with $s(x) = 0 \neq s(x')$ (Theorem P3.10.12), and the hyperplane of $s e^a$ contains $\varphi(x)$ but not $\varphi(x')$. So the closed points of $X$ over a closed point $z \neq P$ of $\mathbb{P}^N$ are at most one, and those over $P$ are the points of $E$.

*Step 2: the contraction.* The morphism $\varphi$ is projective: it is the composite of the graph $X \to X \times \mathbb{P}^N$, a closed immersion, and the projection, and $X$ is projective. Let $X \xrightarrow{\psi} Y \xrightarrow{g} \mathbb{P}^N$ be its Stein factorization (Theorem P4.6.15), with $g$ finite and $\psi$ a contraction, so $\psi$ is projective with $\psi_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_Y$ and surjective by Theorem P4.6.7. The scheme $Y$ is irreducible, as the image of $X$, and reduced, since $\mathcal{O}_Y(W) = \mathcal{O}_X(\psi^{-1}(W))$ is reduced for every open $W$. So $Y$ is integral, and of finite type over $k$ since $g$ is finite.

The fibre $\varphi^{-1}(P)$ is $E$ as a set: it is closed, and by part 3 of Proposition P3.7.3 a closed subset of $X$ is the closure of its closed points. It is connected, so by Theorem P4.6.15, $g^{-1}(P)$ is a single point $y_0$ with $\psi^{-1}(y_0) = E$, and $y_0$ is closed because $g^{-1}(P)$ is a finite closed set. Let $y \neq y_0$ be a closed point of $Y$. Then $z = g(y)$ is a closed point different from $P$, and $\psi^{-1}(y) \subseteq \varphi^{-1}(z)$ is a closed subset with at most one closed point, hence finite. By Proposition P4.6.9 there is an open neighbourhood $W_y$ of $y$ over which $\psi$ is finite. A finite morphism is affine, so for every affine open $W \subseteq W_y$, $\psi^{-1}(W) = \operatorname{Spec} \mathcal{O}_X(\psi^{-1}(W)) = \operatorname{Spec} \mathcal{O}_Y(W) = W$, and $\psi$ is an isomorphism over $W_y$. The union of the $W_y$ is an open subset of $Y \setminus \{y_0\}$ containing all its closed points, so it is $Y \setminus \{y_0\}$ by part 3 of Proposition P3.7.3. So $\psi$ restricts to an isomorphism $X \setminus E \to Y \setminus \{y_0\}$, and $Y$ is a surface.

*Step 3: $Y$ is projective.* The morphism $g$ is finite, hence proper, and $\mathbb{P}^N$ is proper over $k$, so $Y$ is proper over $k$. Let $\mathcal{L} = g^* \mathcal{O}(1)$ and $\mathcal{F}$ coherent on $Y$. By the projection formula (Proposition P4.4.10), $g_*(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^n) \cong g_* \mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{O}(n)$, which is coherent and, by Theorem P3.6.23, generated by its global sections for $n \geq n_0$. On an affine open $W \subseteq \mathbb{P}^N$, $g^{-1}(W)$ is affine and these global sections, which are the global sections of $\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^n$, generate the module $(\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^n)(g^{-1}(W))$ over $\mathcal{O}(W)$, hence over $\mathcal{O}_Y(g^{-1}(W))$. So $\mathcal{F} \otimes \mathcal{L}^n$ is globally generated for $n \geq n_0$, $\mathcal{L}$ is ample (Definition P3.6.22), and $Y$ is projective by Theorem P3.6.25.

*Step 4: the formal neighbourhood of $y_0$.* Let $\mathcal{J} = \mathcal{I}_{y_0} \mathcal{O}_X$. The closed subscheme $X_n = X \times_Y \operatorname{Spec} \mathcal{O}_{Y, y_0}/\mathfrak{m}_{y_0}^n$ of Theorem P4.6.1 is the subscheme of $X$ defined by $\mathcal{J}^n$, because $\mathcal{O}_Y / \mathcal{I}_{y_0}^n$ is the skyscraper sheaf $\mathcal{O}_{Y, y_0}/\mathfrak{m}_{y_0}^n$ at $y_0$. Its support is $\psi^{-1}(y_0) = E$. Functions vanishing at $y_0$ pull back to functions vanishing on the reduced curve $E$, so $\mathcal{J} \subseteq \mathcal{I}_E$, and $\mathcal{I}_E$ is the radical of $\mathcal{J}$. Covering $X$ by finitely many affine open sets, on which $\mathcal{I}_E$ is finitely generated, there is $r$ with $\mathcal{I}_E^r \subseteq \mathcal{J}$. Then $\mathcal{I}_E^{rn} \subseteq \mathcal{J}^n \subseteq \mathcal{I}_E^n$, and the induced maps of inverse systems
$$H^0(\mathcal{O}_X / \mathcal{I}_E^{rn}) \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_X / \mathcal{J}^n) \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_X / \mathcal{I}_E^n)$$
compose to the restriction maps, so they induce inverse isomorphisms of the limits. With Corollary P4.6.3 and $\psi_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_Y$,
$$\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Y, y_0} \cong \varprojlim_n H^0(X_n, \mathcal{O}_{X_n}) \cong \varprojlim_n H^0(nE, \mathcal{O}_{nE}) = A \cong k[[U, V]],$$
the last isomorphism by Lemma P5.7.3, and all maps are induced by pulling back functions along $\psi$ and restricting. So $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Y, y_0}$ is regular of dimension $2$, and $\mathcal{O}_{Y, y_0}$ is regular by Proposition P1.9.5. By part 2 of Theorem P3.7.24, $Y$ is smooth at $y_0$, and it is smooth elsewhere since $Y \setminus \{y_0\} \cong X \setminus E$. This proves part 1.

*Step 5: the blow-up.* The maximal ideal of $A \cong k[[U, V]]$ is $(u, v)$, the kernel of $A \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_E) = k$, and $(u, v)/(u, v)^2$ maps isomorphically onto $H^0(\mathcal{I}_E / \mathcal{I}_E^2)$ by the case $n = 2$ of Lemma P5.7.3. Under $\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Y, y_0} \cong A$ the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_{y_0} \widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{Y, y_0}$ (Theorem P1.6.18) goes to $(u, v)$, so the images of the elements of $\mathfrak{m}_{y_0}$ span $(u, v)/(u, v)^2$. Choose $s_1, s_2 \in \mathfrak{m}_{y_0}$, regular on an affine neighbourhood $W$ of $y_0$, such that the restrictions of $\psi^* s_1$ and $\psi^* s_2$ to $2E$ are sections of $\mathcal{I}_E / \mathcal{I}_E^2 \cong \mathcal{O}_E(1)$ forming a basis $\bar u, \bar v$. Two independent sections of $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^1}(1)$ have no common zero. Let $q \in E$ and let $t$ generate $\mathcal{I}_{E, q}$. Then $\psi^* s_1 = b_1 t$ and $\psi^* s_2 = b_2 t$ with $b_i \in \mathcal{O}_{X, q}$, and the images of $\bar u, \bar v$ in the fibre of $\mathcal{I}_E/\mathcal{I}_E^2$ at $q$ are $b_1(q) \bar t$, $b_2(q) \bar t$. So one of $b_1, b_2$ is a unit and $\mathcal{I}_{E, q} = (\psi^* s_1, \psi^* s_2) \subseteq \mathcal{J}_q \subseteq \mathcal{I}_{E, q}$. At $q \notin E$, $\psi(q) \neq y_0$, so some function vanishing at $y_0$ does not vanish at $\psi(q)$, and $\mathcal{J}_q = \mathcal{O}_{X, q} = \mathcal{I}_{E, q}$. So $\mathcal{J} = \mathcal{I}_E$, which is invertible.

Let $\pi \colon \tilde Y = \operatorname{Bl}_{y_0} Y \to Y$, with exceptional curve $\tilde E$. By Theorem P3.11.9 there is a unique $h \colon X \to \tilde Y$ with $\pi \circ h = \psi$, and $h^{-1}(\tilde E) = \psi^{-1}(y_0)$ as subschemes, that is, $h^{-1}(\tilde E) = E$. Over $Y \setminus \{y_0\}$ both $\psi$ and $\pi$ are isomorphisms, so $h$ maps $X \setminus E$ isomorphically onto $\tilde Y \setminus \tilde E$. The image $h(X)$ is closed, as $X$ is proper, and contains the dense open set $\tilde Y \setminus \tilde E$, so $h$ is surjective, and $h(E) = \tilde E$. So $h|_E \colon E \to \tilde E$ is a surjective morphism of integral curves, and its fibres are finite. All fibres of $h$ are then finite. The morphism $h$ is projective, by the graph argument of Step 2, birational, and $\tilde Y$ is smooth, hence normal (Corollary P1.9.26), by Theorem P5.6.2. By Corollary P4.6.10, $h$ is an isomorphism, and it carries $E$ onto $\tilde E$. $\square$

*Corollary P5.7.5.* (1) The fibres of $\psi$ are $E$ and single points, so $f$ maps every fibre to a point, and Lemma P5.7.2 applies to $\psi$, which is projective with $\psi_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_Y$. If $\psi'$ is a second contraction of $E$, it is projective with $\psi'_* \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_{Y'}$ by Theorem P5.6.2, and its fibres are $E$ and single points. Lemma P5.7.2 gives $u \colon Y \to Y'$ with $\psi' = u \circ \psi$ and $u' \colon Y' \to Y$ with $\psi = u' \circ \psi'$. Then $(u' \circ u) \circ \psi = \psi$, so $u' \circ u = \mathrm{id}_Y$ by the uniqueness in Lemma P5.7.2, and likewise $u \circ u' = \mathrm{id}_{Y'}$.

(2) and (3). By Theorem P5.7.4, $\psi$ is the blow-up of $y_0$ up to an isomorphism that matches $E$ with the exceptional curve. So Theorem P5.6.3 applies. It gives the orthogonal decompositions of $\operatorname{Pic}$ and $\operatorname{Num}$, the equality $\psi^* D \cdot \psi^* D' = D \cdot D'$, the formula $K_X = \psi^* K_Y + E$, $K_X^2 = K_Y^2 - 1$, and $h^i(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^i(\mathcal{O}_Y)$. For $D = \psi^* D_Y + cE$, $D \cdot E = -c$, so $D \cdot E = 0$ if and only if $D \in \psi^* \operatorname{Pic}(Y)$.

(4) A blow-up of a point contains its exceptional curve, a $(-1)$-curve by Theorem P5.6.2. Conversely a $(-1)$-curve is contractible by Theorem P5.7.4. An integral curve with $C^2 < 0$ and $K \cdot C < 0$ is a $(-1)$-curve by part 2 of Corollary P5.5.15, and a $(-1)$-curve has $C^2 = K \cdot C = -1$. $\square$

### Exercises

1. (A) Let $X = S_r$ be the plane blown up at $r$ distinct points as in Example P5.6.8, and $C$ a smooth conic through exactly five of them. Show that the strict transform of $C$ is contractible, and compute $K^2$ and the Picard group of the surface obtained by contracting it.
2. (A) In Theorem P5.7.4, let $D$ be a divisor on $X$ with $D \cdot E = 0$. Show that $D \sim \psi^* \psi_* D$, where $\psi_* D$ is the closure of the image of $D|_{X \setminus E}$, and that $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ is trivial on a neighbourhood of $E$ if $D$ is effective and disjoint from $E$.
3. (B) Let $E_1, E_2$ be disjoint $(-1)$-curves on $X$, and $\psi_1 \colon X \to Y_1$ the contraction of $E_1$. Show that $\psi_1(E_2)$ is a $(-1)$-curve on $Y_1$, and that contracting $E_1$ and then $\psi_1(E_2)$, or $E_2$ and then the image of $E_1$, gives the same surface up to a unique isomorphism under $X$.
4. (B) Let $E_1, E_2$ be distinct $(-1)$-curves on $X$ with $m = E_1 \cdot E_2$, and $\psi_1$ the contraction of $E_1$. Show that $\psi_1(E_2)$ is an integral curve with multiplicity $m$ at the image point of $E_1$, that $\psi_1^* \psi_1(E_2) = E_2 + m E_1$, and that $\psi_1(E_2)^2 = m^2 - 1$. Check this on $S_2$ with $E_1$ and $L = \ell - E_1 - E_2$.
5. (B) Let $E \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ be an integral curve on $X$ with $E^2 = -n$, $n \geq 1$. Show that $\mathcal{I}_E^j / \mathcal{I}_E^{j+1} \cong \mathcal{O}_E(nj)$, that the restriction maps $H^0(\mathcal{O}_{(m+1)E}) \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_{mE})$ are surjective, and that $h^0(\mathcal{O}_{mE}) = \sum_{j = 0}^{m-1} (nj + 1)$. Compare with $\dim_k \mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{m}^m$ for a regular local ring of dimension $2$, and with Example P5.7.8 for $n = 2$.
6. (C) Let $E_1, \dots, E_r$ be pairwise disjoint $(-1)$-curves on $X$, $H$ very ample with $H^1(H) = 0$, $a_i = H \cdot E_i$ and $M = H + \sum_i a_i E_i$. Adapt the proof of Theorem P5.7.4 to show that $|M|$ is base point free and that the Stein factorization of its morphism is a morphism $X \to Y$ onto a smooth projective surface that is the blow-up of $r$ distinct points, with exceptional curves $E_1, \dots, E_r$.

### Lean

Mathlib has neither blow-ups of surfaces nor the intersection pairing, so neither Castelnuovo's criterion nor the notion of a $(-1)$-curve can be stated there yet. It has the algebraic ingredients of Lemma P5.7.3: multivariate power series `MvPowerSeries` and the adic completion `AdicCompletion` of a module along an ideal. The theorem on formal functions is not formalized. The handbook adds no `MinimalModel` node here.

### Notes and history

Castelnuovo proved the criterion in 1901 in the language of linear systems, and Enriques used it to build the classification of surfaces on the contraction of exceptional curves of the first kind, as $(-1)$-curves were then called. The proof above is Hartshorne's, Theorem V.5.7, with the normality of the image replaced by the Stein factorization, so that the formal function computation of Lemma P5.7.3 is applied directly to the contraction. Beauville, Theorem II.17, gives the same argument over $\mathbb{C}$. The literature state is theorem. In the minimal model program, Theorem P5.7.4 is the surface case of the contraction theorem for a $K$-negative extremal ray: by Corollary P5.7.5 the class of a $(-1)$-curve satisfies $K \cdot E < 0$, and its contraction lowers the Picard number by one while keeping the surface smooth. Artin's and Grauert's criteria in Chapter 8 contract curves of higher negativity, such as the $(-2)$-curve of Example P5.7.8, to singular points, and Chapter 12 recovers Theorem P5.7.4 from the cone theorem for surfaces.
