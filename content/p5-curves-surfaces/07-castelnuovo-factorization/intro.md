# Castelnuovo's criterion and factorization

Chapter 6 computed what the blow-up of a point does to a smooth projective surface: it adds a smooth rational curve $E$ with $E^2 = -1$ and $K \cdot E = -1$, and it changes the Picard group, the canonical class and nothing else in a controlled way. This chapter proves the converse and its consequences. Every curve with these two numbers is the exceptional curve of a blow-up, and every birational morphism of smooth projective surfaces is a composite of blow-ups of points. So the operations of Chapter 6 generate all birational geometry of smooth surfaces.

Section 7.1 proves Castelnuovo's contractibility criterion. Given a $(-1)$-curve $E$ on $X$, the linear system $|H + aE|$, with $H$ very ample and $a = H \cdot E$, is trivial on $E$ and separates the points of $X \setminus E$, so it contracts $E$ to a point. The Stein factorization makes the image normal, and the theorem on formal functions computes the completed local ring of the image point as $\varprojlim_n H^0(\mathcal{O}_{nE}) \cong k[[u, v]]$, so the point is smooth and $X$ is its blow-up. The contraction is universal among morphisms that collapse $E$. Section 7.2 removes the indeterminacy of a rational map from a surface by blowing up base points, which ends because the self-intersection of the moving part drops at each step. It then proves that a birational morphism of smooth projective surfaces factors through the blow-up of every point where its inverse is undefined, so that it is a composite of exactly $\rho(X) - \rho(Y)$ blow-ups, and Zariski's theorem that every birational map of smooth projective surfaces is a composite of blow-ups and blow-downs. The quadric blown up at a point, the plane blown up at two points and the standard Cremona transformation are worked out.

In the minimal model program, the contraction of Section 7.1 is the first divisorial contraction: $E$ spans a $K_X$-negative extremal ray, and the contraction it defines is the blow-down. The termination of the run in dimension $2$ is the drop of the Picard number by one at each step, and the factorization theorem says that the program on a smooth surface never leaves the category of smooth surfaces. Chapters 11 and 12 take these results as their starting point.

The chapter follows Hartshorne, Section V.5, Beauville, Chapter II, and the second chapter of Reid's *Chapters on algebraic surfaces*.
