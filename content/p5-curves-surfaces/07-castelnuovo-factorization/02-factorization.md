## Elimination of indeterminacy and factorization

### Statement

A *composite of point blow-ups* $Z \to X$ is a morphism $Z = X_r \to X_{r-1} \to \dots \to X_0 = X$ in which each $X_{i+1} \to X_i$ is the blow-up of a closed point of $X_i$; for $r = 0$ it is an isomorphism. By Theorem P5.6.2 every $X_i$ is a smooth projective surface, and the composite is a projective birational morphism. An integral curve $C \subseteq X$ is *contracted* by a morphism $f$ if $f(C)$ is a point. For a smooth projective surface $X$, $\rho(X)$ denotes the rank of $\operatorname{Num}(X)$ (Definition P5.5.7). It is finite by the theorem of the base of Néron and Severi, which the handbook does not prove; the results below use the finiteness only in parts 2 and 3 of Corollary P5.7.14, and for the surfaces in the examples $\rho$ is computed directly from Theorem P5.6.3.

**Theorem P5.7.9 (elimination of indeterminacy).** Let $S$ be a smooth projective surface, $X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n_k$ a closed subscheme and $\varphi \colon S \dashrightarrow X$ a rational map. Then there is a composite of point blow-ups $\pi \colon S_r \to S$ such that $\varphi \circ \pi$ is a morphism $S_r \to X$. If $\varphi = \varphi_V$ for a linear system $V \subseteq \Gamma(S, \mathcal{O}_S(D))$ without fixed component, $\pi$ can be chosen with $r \leq D^2$, and the centre of each blow-up is a base point of the transformed linear system.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Lemma P5.7.10 (where the inverse is defined).**

1. Let $f \colon X \to Y$ be a projective birational morphism of integral schemes, separated and of finite type over $k$, with $Y$ normal, and let $y \in Y$. Then the following are equivalent: the rational map $f^{-1}$ is defined at $y$; the fibre $X_y$ is finite; $f$ is an isomorphism over an open neighbourhood of $y$.
2. Let $g \colon X \dashrightarrow Z$ be a birational map of smooth projective surfaces that is not defined at the closed point $x \in X$. Then there is an integral curve $\Gamma \subseteq Z$ such that $g^{-1}$ is defined at the generic point of $\Gamma$ and $g^{-1}(z) = x$ for all $z$ in a dense open subset of $\Gamma$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Proposition P5.7.11 (factoring through a blow-up).** Let $f \colon X \to Y$ be a birational morphism of smooth projective surfaces, and $p \in Y$ a closed point at which $f^{-1}$ is not defined. Let $\pi \colon \tilde Y \to Y$ be the blow-up of $p$. Then there is a morphism $f_1 \colon X \to \tilde Y$ with $f = \pi \circ f_1$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P5.7.12 (factorization of birational morphisms).** Let $f \colon X \to Y$ be a birational morphism of smooth projective surfaces, and let $n$ be the number of integral curves contracted by $f$, which is finite.

1. There is a composite of $n$ point blow-ups $Y_n \to Y_{n-1} \to \dots \to Y_0 = Y$ and an isomorphism $X \cong Y_n$ over $Y$. The curves contracted by $f$ are the strict transforms in $X$ of the exceptional curves of the $n$ blow-ups.
2. Let $\varepsilon_i \in \operatorname{Pic}(X)$ be the pullback of the exceptional curve of $Y_i \to Y_{i-1}$. Then $\operatorname{Pic}(X) = f^* \operatorname{Pic}(Y) \oplus \mathbb{Z} \varepsilon_1 \oplus \dots \oplus \mathbb{Z} \varepsilon_n$, an orthogonal sum with $f^* D \cdot f^* D' = D \cdot D'$ and $\varepsilon_i \cdot \varepsilon_j = -\delta_{ij}$, and the same holds for $\operatorname{Num}$. So $\rho(X) = \rho(Y) + n$.
3. $K_X = f^* K_Y + \varepsilon_1 + \dots + \varepsilon_n$, $K_X^2 = K_Y^2 - n$, and $h^i(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^i(\mathcal{O}_Y)$ for every $i$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P5.7.13 (Zariski's factorization of birational maps).** Let $T \colon X \dashrightarrow Y$ be a birational map of smooth projective surfaces. Then there are a smooth projective surface $Z$ and composites of point blow-ups $g \colon Z \to X$ and $h \colon Z \to Y$ with $h = T \circ g$. So $T$ is a composite of finitely many blow-ups of points followed by finitely many contractions of $(-1)$-curves.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Corollary P5.7.14 (consequences of factorization).**

1. A birational morphism $f \colon X \to Y$ of smooth projective surfaces that is not an isomorphism contracts a $(-1)$-curve of $X$: the exceptional curve of the last blow-up in Theorem P5.7.12.
2. If $X$ and $Y$ are birational smooth projective surfaces, then $h^i(\mathcal{O}_X) = h^i(\mathcal{O}_Y)$ for every $i$, and $K_X^2 + \rho(X) = K_Y^2 + \rho(Y)$.
3. Every smooth projective surface $X$ has a birational morphism onto a smooth projective surface without $(-1)$-curves, which is a composite of at most $\rho(X) - 1$ contractions of $(-1)$-curves.
4. Let $f \colon X \to Y$ be a birational morphism of smooth projective surfaces whose contracted curves $C_1, \dots, C_n$ are pairwise disjoint $(-1)$-curves. Then the points $p_i = f(C_i)$ are distinct, and $f$ is the blow-up of $p_1, \dots, p_n$, one after another, with $C_i$ the exceptional curve over $p_i$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

### Proof in words

A rational map from a smooth surface is given by a linear system without fixed component, and it fails to be a morphism only at the finitely many base points. Blowing up a base point of multiplicity $m$ replaces $D$ by $\pi^* D - mE$, which again has no fixed component, and lowers the self-intersection by $m^2 \geq 1$. Since two general members of a linear system without fixed component meet properly, the self-intersection is never negative, so the process stops.

For a birational morphism $f$, the point is that if $f^{-1}$ is undefined at $p$, then $f$ already factors through the blow-up of $p$. Otherwise $\pi^{-1} \circ f$ would be undefined at some $x$, and the inverse map would blow $x$ up to a curve, necessarily the exceptional curve $\tilde E$ over $p$. So the local ring at $x$ is dominated by the valuation ring of $\tilde E$. Two local parameters at $p$ have order $1$ along $\tilde E$, and their pullbacks at $x$ have a common factor that vanishes along the curve of $f^{-1}(p)$ through $x$. Hence the quotients by that factor have order $0$, one of them is a unit, and $\mathfrak{m}_p \mathcal{O}_{X, x}$ is principal; the universal property of the blow-up then defines $\pi^{-1} \circ f$ at $x$ after all. Each factorization step uses up one contracted curve, so $f$ is a composite of as many blow-ups as it contracts curves. Zariski's theorem combines this with the elimination of indeterminacy.

### Examples

**Example P5.7.15 (a pencil of conics with infinitely near base points).** Let $V = \langle x^2, yz \rangle \subseteq \Gamma(\mathbb{P}^2, \mathcal{O}(2))$, the pencil of conics $x^2 = \lambda yz$, and $\varphi_V = [x^2 : yz] \colon \mathbb{P}^2 \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^1$. The two generators have no common component, so $V$ has no fixed component, and $D^2 = 4$. The base points are the common zeros $p = [0 : 0 : 1]$ and $r = [0 : 1 : 0]$. Near $p$, on $z = 1$, the system is spanned by $x^2$ and $y$, of multiplicities $2$ and $1$, so $m = 1$. In the chart $y = xt$ of the blow-up, with $E_1 = V(x)$, the transformed sections are $x^2/x = x$ and $xt/x = t$, with the common zero $x = t = 0$; in the chart $x = ys$, with $E_1 = V(y)$, they are $y s^2$ and $1$, with no common zero. So there is exactly one base point $p_1 \in E_1$, the tangent direction $y = 0$ shared by all the conics. Blowing up $p_1$ with $m = 1$, the sections $x, t$ become $1, u$ in the chart $t = xu$ and $v, 1$ in the chart $x = tv$, so no base point remains over $p$. The point $r$ is symmetric under $y \leftrightarrow z$. So four blow-ups resolve $\varphi_V$, the bound $r \leq D^2 = 4$ of Theorem P5.7.9 is attained, and the final system has class $2\ell - \varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_3 - \varepsilon_4$ with self-intersection $4 - 4 = 0$, as it must for a base point free pencil whose morphism has one-dimensional image. A single base point of multiplicity $1$ with no infinitely near base point, as for the projection from a point, needs one blow-up (Example P5.6.7).

**Example P5.7.16 (the standard Cremona transformation).** Let $\tau = [yz : xz : xy] \colon \mathbb{P}^2 \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^2$ (Example P3.10.31), given by $V = \langle yz, xz, xy \rangle \subseteq \Gamma(\mathcal{O}(2))$, without fixed component. It is birational, since $\tau \circ \tau = [x^2 yz : x y^2 z : x y z^2] = [x : y : z]$ on the open set $xyz \neq 0$. The base points are $p_x = [1 : 0 : 0]$, $p_y = [0 : 1 : 0]$, $p_z = [0 : 0 : 1]$. Near $p_x$, on $x = 1$, the system is spanned by $yz, z, y$, with $m = 1$. In the chart $z = ys$ the transformed sections are $ys, s, 1$, and in the chart $y = zt$ they are $zt, 1, t$, so there is no base point on $E_x$; on $E_x = \operatorname{Proj} k[Y, Z]$ they restrict to $0, Z, Y$. The same holds at $p_y$ and $p_z$. So the blow-up $S_3$ of the three points resolves $\tau$ to a birational morphism $\tilde\tau \colon S_3 \to \mathbb{P}^2$ with
$$\tilde\tau^* \ell = 2\ell - E_x - E_y - E_z, \qquad (\tilde\tau^* \ell)^2 = 4 - 3 = 1.$$

An integral curve $C$ is contracted by $\tilde\tau$ if and only if $C \cdot \tilde\tau^* \ell = 0$: if $\tilde\tau(C)$ is a point, a line missing it pulls back to a member of $|\tilde\tau^* \ell|$ disjoint from $C$, and if $\tilde\tau(C)$ is a curve, a line meeting it in a finite nonempty set pulls back to a member meeting $C$ in a finite nonempty set, so $C \cdot \tilde\tau^* \ell > 0$ by Proposition P5.5.5. Now $E_x \cdot \tilde\tau^* \ell = 1$. Any other $C$ is the strict transform of a plane curve of degree $d \geq 1$ with multiplicities $m_x, m_y, m_z$, of class $d \ell - m_x E_x - m_y E_y - m_z E_z$ (Proposition P5.6.4), and $C \cdot \tilde\tau^* \ell = 2d - m_x - m_y - m_z$. Let $L_x$, $L_y$, $L_z$ be the strict transforms of the lines $V(x)$, $V(y)$, $V(z)$, so $L_x = \ell - E_y - E_z$, and $L_x \cdot \tilde\tau^* \ell = 2 - 2 = 0$. If $C$ is none of them, $C \cdot L_x = d - m_y - m_z \geq 0$, and similarly for $L_y$, $L_z$; adding, $3d \geq 2(m_x + m_y + m_z)$, and with $m_x + m_y + m_z = 2d$ this gives $3d \geq 4d$, which is false. So $\tilde\tau$ contracts exactly $L_x, L_y, L_z$, which are pairwise disjoint $(-1)$-curves by Example P5.6.8: $(\ell - E_y - E_z) \cdot (\ell - E_x - E_z) = 1 - 1 = 0$. On $V(x)$, $\tau = [yz : 0 : 0] = p_x$, so $\tilde\tau(L_x) = p_x$. By part 4 of Corollary P5.7.14, $\tilde\tau$ is itself the blow-up of $p_x, p_y, p_z$, with exceptional curves $L_x, L_y, L_z$:
$$\mathbb{P}^2 \xleftarrow{\ \pi\ } S_3 \xrightarrow{\ \tilde\tau\ } \mathbb{P}^2, \qquad \tilde\tau^* \ell = 2\ell - E_x - E_y - E_z, \quad \text{exceptional curves } \ell - E_y - E_z, \ \ell - E_x - E_z, \ \ell - E_x - E_y.$$
This is the second basis $\ell', E'_x, E'_y, E'_z$ of $\operatorname{Pic}(S_3)$ in Exercise 4 of Section 6.1, now realized by a second blow-down. In the factorization of Theorem P5.7.13, $\tau$ is three blow-ups followed by three blow-downs.

**Example P5.7.17 (the quadric blown up at a point is the plane blown up at two points).** Let $\varphi \colon \tilde Q \to \mathbb{P}^2$ be the birational morphism of Example P5.6.9, induced by the projection of $Q = V(xw - yz)$ from $p$, with $\varphi^* \ell = F_1 + F_2 - E$. As in Example P5.7.16, an integral curve $C$ is contracted by $\varphi$ if and only if $C \cdot (F_1 + F_2 - E) = 0$. Here $E \cdot (F_1 + F_2 - E) = 1$. Any other $C$ is the strict transform of an integral curve of class $aF_1 + bF_2$ on $Q$ (Example P5.5.9), $a, b \geq 0$ not both $0$, of multiplicity $m$ at $p$, so $C = aF_1 + bF_2 - mE$ by Proposition P5.6.4 and $C \cdot \varphi^* \ell = a + b - m$. The strict transforms $\tilde L_i$ of the lines through $p$ have class $F_i - E$ and $\tilde L_i \cdot \varphi^* \ell = 1 - 1 = 0$. If $C$ is neither, then $C \cdot \tilde L_1 = b - m \geq 0$ and $C \cdot \tilde L_2 = a - m \geq 0$, so a contracted $C$ would have $a + b = m \leq (a + b)/2$, which is false. So $\varphi$ contracts exactly $\tilde L_1$ and $\tilde L_2$, which are disjoint $(-1)$-curves (Example P5.6.9) with images $[0 : 0 : 1]$ and $[0 : 1 : 0]$. By part 4 of Corollary P5.7.14,
$$\operatorname{Bl}_p (\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1) \cong \tilde Q \cong \operatorname{Bl}_{[0:0:1], [0:1:0]} \mathbb{P}^2,$$
which completes Example P5.6.9. The counts agree with Theorem P5.7.12: $\rho(\tilde Q) = 3 = \rho(\mathbb{P}^2) + 2$ and $K_{\tilde Q}^2 = 8 - 1 = 7 = 9 - 2$. The curve $E$ maps onto the line $V(x)$ through both points, so on $\operatorname{Bl}_{q_1, q_2} \mathbb{P}^2$ it is the curve $\ell - E_1 - E_2$ of Example P5.7.7, and the isometry $F_1 + F_2 - E \mapsto \ell$, $F_2 - E \mapsto E_1$, $F_1 - E \mapsto E_2$ (up to the order of the two points) solves Exercise 6 of Section 6.1 geometrically.

**Example P5.7.18 (the $(-1)$-curves of $S_2$ and its two minimal models).** Let $S_2$ be the plane blown up at two distinct points, as in Example P5.7.7. A $(-1)$-curve other than $E_1, E_2$ is the strict transform of an integral plane curve of degree $d \geq 1$, of class $d\ell - m_1 E_1 - m_2 E_2$ with $m_i \geq 0$, and $C^2 = K \cdot C = -1$ reads
$$m_1^2 + m_2^2 = d^2 + 1, \qquad m_1 + m_2 = 3d - 1.$$
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality $(m_1 + m_2)^2 \leq 2(m_1^2 + m_2^2)$, so $(3d - 1)^2 \leq 2d^2 + 2$, that is $7d^2 - 6d - 1 = (7d + 1)(d - 1) \leq 0$, and $d = 1$, $m_1 = m_2 = 1$. So the $(-1)$-curves of $S_2$ are exactly $E_1$, $E_2$ and $L = \ell - E_1 - E_2$. Contracting $E_1$ gives $\operatorname{Bl}_{p_2} \mathbb{P}^2 \cong F_1$, contracting $L$ gives $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ (Example P5.7.7). The plane has no $(-1)$-curve, as every curve has $C^2 = d^2 > 0$. The quadric has none, as an integral curve of class $aF_1 + bF_2$ has $C^2 = 2ab \neq -1$ (Example P5.5.9). On $F_1 = \operatorname{Bl}_p \mathbb{P}^2$ a $(-1)$-curve other than $E$ would have class $d\ell - mE$ with $m = 3d - 1$ and $d^2 - (3d - 1)^2 = -1$, that is $2d(4d - 3) = 0$, which has no solution $d \geq 1$; so $E$ is its only $(-1)$-curve (compare Exercise 1 of Section 6.1). So every sequence of contractions of $(-1)$-curves starting from $S_2$ ends at $\mathbb{P}^2$ or at $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$: the surface without $(-1)$-curves in part 3 of Corollary P5.7.14 is not unique. Part 2 of the same corollary is visible here: $K^2 + \rho$ is $7 + 3$ for $S_2$, $9 + 1$ for $\mathbb{P}^2$ and $8 + 2$ for $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ (Example P5.5.17), all equal to $10$.

### Proof

*Theorem P5.7.9.* Let $(U, \varphi_U)$ be a representative of $\varphi$ and $\Lambda \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n$ the smallest linear subspace containing $\varphi_U(U)$. In coordinates $\Lambda \cong \mathbb{P}^m$, and $\varphi$ is a rational map $S \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^m$ whose image lies in no hyperplane. If $\psi \colon S_r \to \mathbb{P}^m$ is a morphism that represents $\varphi \circ \pi$, then $\psi^{-1}(X \cap \Lambda)$ is closed and contains a dense open subset of the integral scheme $S_r$, so it is all of $S_r$, and $\psi$ factors through $X$ because $S_r$ is reduced. So it is enough to treat $X = \mathbb{P}^m$. By part 5 of Theorem P3.10.29 there are a divisor $D$ and $V \subseteq \Gamma(S, \mathcal{O}_S(D))$ without fixed component with $\varphi = \varphi_V$, and it suffices to prove the second assertion. As $S$ is regular, $D$ is Cartier (Corollary P3.9.11).

First, $D^2 \geq 0$ for every linear system $V \neq 0$ without fixed component on a smooth projective surface. Choose $g \in V \setminus 0$ and let $P_1, \dots, P_t$ be the prime divisors in $Z(g)$. The sections $h \in V$ with $\operatorname{mult}_{P_j} Z(h) \geq 1$ form a linear subspace $V_j$, and $V_j \neq V$ because $V$ has no fixed component. Since $k$ is infinite, a vector space over $k$ is not a finite union of proper subspaces, so there is $h \in V$ outside every $V_j$. Then $Z(g)$ and $Z(h)$ have no common component, and $D^2 = Z(g) \cdot Z(h) \geq 0$ by Proposition P5.5.5.

Now argue by induction on $D^2$. By part 4 of Theorem P3.10.29 the base locus of $V$ has codimension at least $2$, so it is a finite set of closed points, and $\varphi_V$ is a morphism if it is empty, by Theorem P3.10.9. Otherwise let $p$ be a base point, $\pi \colon S_1 \to S$ its blow-up with exceptional curve $E$, and $m = \min_{g \in V \setminus 0} m_p(Z(g))$, which is at least $1$. By part 2 of Corollary P5.6.5, pullback identifies $V$ with a subspace $V_1 \subseteq \Gamma(S_1, \mathcal{O}(D_1))$, $D_1 = \pi^* D - mE$, and the member of $|V_1|$ corresponding to $g$ is $\widetilde{Z(g)} + (m_p(Z(g)) - m)E$. The coefficient of $E$ is $0$ for some $g$, by the choice of $m$. Every other prime divisor of $S_1$ is the strict transform $\tilde P$ of a prime divisor $P$ of $S$, and its coefficient is $\operatorname{mult}_P Z(g)$ by part 2 of Proposition P5.6.4, which is $0$ for some $g$. So $V_1$ has no fixed component. The elements of $V_1$ are the same rational functions as those of $V$, in $K(S_1) = K(S)$, so by part 4 of Theorem P3.10.29, $\varphi_{V_1} = \varphi_V \circ \pi$: both are given by $[g_0 : \dots : g_m]$ on the dense open set where $\pi$ is an isomorphism and $V$ is base point free. By part 2 of Theorem P5.6.3, $D_1^2 = D^2 - m^2 \leq D^2 - 1$, and $S_1$ is a smooth projective surface by Theorem P5.6.2. By induction there is a composite of at most $D_1^2$ point blow-ups $S_r \to S_1$, each centred at a base point, after which $\varphi_{V_1}$ becomes a morphism, and $r \leq D_1^2 + 1 \leq D^2$. $\square$

*Lemma P5.7.10.* (1) If $f$ is an isomorphism over an open $W \ni y$, its inverse represents $f^{-1}$ at $y$. If $X_y$ is finite, then $f$ is an isomorphism over a neighbourhood of $y$ by Corollary P4.6.10, which applies because $f$ is projective and birational and $Y$ is normal; conversely the fibre over a point of such a neighbourhood is one point. Suppose $f^{-1}$ is represented by $\iota \colon W \to X$ with $y \in W$. The morphisms $f \circ \iota$ and the inclusion $W \to Y$ agree on a dense open subset of $W$, as $f \circ f^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$, and $W$ is reduced and $Y$ separated, so $f \circ \iota$ is the inclusion. Likewise $\iota \circ f$ and the inclusion $f^{-1}(W) \to X$ agree on a dense open subset of the reduced scheme $f^{-1}(W)$, hence everywhere. So $f^{-1}(W) \to W$ is an isomorphism with inverse $\iota$.

(2) Embed $Z \subseteq \mathbb{P}^N$. By Theorem P5.7.9 there is a composite of point blow-ups $\sigma \colon X' \to X$ such that $g' = g \circ \sigma$ is a morphism $X' \to Z$. Both $\sigma$ and $g'$ are projective, since $X'$ is projective, and birational. Let $T \subseteq X$ be the finite set of images of the centres of the blow-ups, so that $\sigma$ is an isomorphism over $X \setminus T$, and $U = (X \setminus T) \cup \{x\}$, which is open. The fibre $F = \sigma^{-1}(x)$ is nonempty and connected by Theorem P4.6.8, and $\sigma_* \mathcal{O}_{X'} = \mathcal{O}_X$. Suppose $g'(F)$ is a point. Then every fibre of $\sigma^{-1}(U) \to U$ is mapped to a point by $g'$, and Lemma P5.7.2 gives $h \colon U \to Z$ with $g' = h \circ \sigma$ on $\sigma^{-1}(U)$. On $X \setminus T$, $h = g' \circ \sigma^{-1}$ represents $g$, so $g$ is defined at $x$, a contradiction.

So $g'(F)$ is not a point. Since $g$ is not defined at $x$, $\sigma$ is not an isomorphism over $x$, so $F$ is not finite by part 1, and $F$ is a closed subset of $X'$ of dimension $1$, a finite union of integral curves $F_1, \dots, F_s$. Each $g'(F_j)$ is closed and irreducible, hence a point or an integral curve. If all of them were points, $g'(F)$ would be a finite connected set, hence a point. So some $\Gamma = g'(F_j)$ is an integral curve. The rational map $g'^{-1} \colon Z \dashrightarrow X'$ is defined at the generic point $\eta$ of $\Gamma$ by part 2 of Theorem P3.10.29, so by part 1 there is an open $V \ni \eta$ over which $g'$ is an isomorphism. For $z \in V \cap \Gamma$, a dense open subset of $\Gamma$, the fibre $g'^{-1}(z)$ is one point, and it lies in $F_j$ because $g'(F_j) = \Gamma$. So $g^{-1} = \sigma \circ g'^{-1}$ is defined on $V$ and maps every $z \in V \cap \Gamma$ to $\sigma(F_j) = x$. $\square$

*Proposition P5.7.11.* Let $g = \pi^{-1} \circ f \colon X \dashrightarrow \tilde Y$, a birational map, and let $\tilde E$ be the exceptional curve of $\pi$. If $g$ is defined everywhere, its representative $f_1$ on $X$ satisfies $\pi \circ f_1 = f$, as both agree on a dense open subset of the reduced scheme $X$ and $Y$ is separated. Suppose instead that $g$ is not defined on the nonempty closed set $X \setminus \operatorname{dom}(g)$ (part 1 of Theorem P3.10.29). It contains a closed point $x$ by part 3 of Proposition P3.7.3.

By part 2 of Lemma P5.7.10 there is an integral curve $\Gamma \subseteq \tilde Y$ such that $g^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ \pi$ is represented by a morphism $\iota \colon W \to X$ on an open set $W$ containing the generic point $\eta$ of $\Gamma$, with $\iota(z) = x$ for all $z$ in a dense open subset $\Gamma^\circ$ of $\Gamma$. As before, $f \circ \iota = \pi|_W$, so $\pi(z) = f(x)$ for $z \in \Gamma^\circ$. So $\pi$ contracts $\Gamma$, which forces $\Gamma = \tilde E$, since $\pi$ is an isomorphism off $\tilde E$, and $f(x) = p$.

Identify the function fields of $X$, $Y$ and $\tilde Y$ by $f$ and $\pi$, and let $\operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}$ be the valuation of the discrete valuation ring $\mathcal{O}_{\tilde Y, \eta}$. If $a \in \mathcal{O}_{X, x}$ is regular on an open $U_a \ni x$, then $\iota^* a$ is regular on the open set $\iota^{-1}(U_a)$, which contains $\Gamma^\circ$ and hence $\eta$, so $\operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(a) \geq 0$. If also $a(x) = 0$, then $\iota^* a$ vanishes on $\Gamma^\circ$, a dense subset of $\tilde E$, so $\operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(a) \geq 1$. Thus
$$\operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(a) \geq 1 \quad \text{for every } a \in \mathfrak{m}_x \setminus 0. \tag{$*$}$$

Let $s, t$ be local parameters at $p$. By part 1 of Proposition P5.6.4, $\operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(\pi^* s) = \operatorname{mult}_p(s) = 1$, and likewise for $t$. The pullbacks $f^* s, f^* t \in \mathfrak{m}_x$ are nonzero, as $f$ is dominant, and $\mathcal{O}_{X, x}$ is a unique factorization domain by Corollary P1.9.26. Write $f^* s = d \, a$ and $f^* t = d \, b$ with $d$ a greatest common divisor and $a, b$ coprime. By part 1 of Lemma P5.7.10 the fibre $f^{-1}(p)$ is not finite, so by Corollary P4.6.10 it has positive dimension at $x$, and $x$ lies on an integral curve $C \subseteq f^{-1}(p)$. A local equation $c$ of $C$ at $x$ is a prime element dividing $f^* s$ and $f^* t$, which vanish on $C$. So $c \mid d$, $d \in \mathfrak{m}_x$, and $\operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(d) \geq 1$ by $(*)$. As $f^* s$ and $\pi^* s$ are the same element of the function field,
$$1 = \operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(f^* s) = \operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(d) + \operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(a) \geq 1 + \operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(a),$$
so $\operatorname{ord}_{\tilde E}(a) \leq 0$, and $a \notin \mathfrak{m}_x$ by $(*)$: $a$ is a unit. Then $\mathfrak{m}_p \mathcal{O}_{X, x} = (f^* s, f^* t) = d(a, b) = (d)$, a principal ideal generated by a nonzerodivisor. The coherent ideal $\mathcal{I}_p \mathcal{O}_X$ is therefore generated by $d$ on an open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$, and it is invertible there. Theorem P3.11.9 gives a morphism $U \to \tilde Y$ lifting $f|_U$, which agrees with $\pi^{-1} \circ f$ where $\pi$ is an isomorphism. So $g$ is defined at $x$, a contradiction. $\square$

*Theorem P5.7.12.* Let $U \subseteq Y$ be the set of points with finite fibre. By Corollary P4.6.10, which applies because $f$ is projective and birational and $Y$ is normal, $U$ is open, $f$ is an isomorphism over $U$, and the fibres over $Y \setminus U$ are connected of positive dimension at every point. The exceptional locus $f^{-1}(Y \setminus U)$ is a proper closed subset of $X$, so it is a finite union of integral curves and closed points. Every integral curve contracted by $f$ lies over a point of $Y \setminus U$, so it is one of these components, and every point of $Y \setminus U$ is the image of such a curve. So the number $N(f)$ of contracted curves is finite and $Y \setminus U$ is a finite set of closed points.

Induction on $N(f)$. If $N(f) = 0$, then $U = Y$ and $f$ is an isomorphism. If $N(f) > 0$, let $p \in Y \setminus U$. By part 1 of Lemma P5.7.10, $f^{-1}$ is not defined at $p$, and Proposition P5.7.11 gives $f = \pi \circ f_1$, with $\pi \colon \tilde Y \to Y$ the blow-up of $p$ and $f_1 \colon X \to \tilde Y$ a birational morphism of smooth projective surfaces. A curve contracted by $f_1$ is contracted by $f$. A curve $C$ contracted by $f$ but not by $f_1$ is mapped onto an integral curve contracted by $\pi$, that is, onto $\tilde E$. There is such a curve, because $f_1$ is surjective, being proper and dominant, and $f_1^{-1}(\tilde E)$ is a proper closed subset. There is only one: by the first paragraph applied to $f_1$, $f_1$ is an isomorphism over a neighbourhood of the generic point $\eta$ of $\tilde E$, so the fibre over $\eta$ is a single point, while the generic point of every curve mapping onto $\tilde E$ maps to $\eta$. This curve is the strict transform of $\tilde E$, the closure of $f_1^{-1}(\tilde E \cap U_1)$ for the open set $U_1$ of finite fibres of $f_1$. So $N(f_1) = N(f) - 1$, and by induction $f_1$ is a composite of $N(f) - 1$ point blow-ups of $\tilde Y$ whose exceptional curves have the remaining contracted curves as strict transforms. This proves part 1 with $n = N(f)$.

Parts 2 and 3 follow by applying Theorem P5.6.3 to each of the $n$ blow-ups. At the $i$th step $\operatorname{Pic}(Y_i) = \operatorname{Pic}(Y_{i-1}) \oplus \mathbb{Z} E_i$, orthogonal, with pullback an isometry, $E_i^2 = -1$, and $K_{Y_i} = (\text{pullback of } K_{Y_{i-1}}) + E_i$; pulling back to $X$ preserves orthogonality and intersection numbers, so the decomposition, the formula for $K_X$, and $K_X^2 = K_Y^2 + \sum_i \varepsilon_i^2 = K_Y^2 - n$ follow. The same induction applies to $\operatorname{Num}$ by part 4 of Theorem P5.6.3, and the $h^i(\mathcal{O})$ are unchanged at each step by part 3. $\square$

*Theorem P5.7.13.* Embed $Y$ in a projective space. By Theorem P5.7.9 there is a composite of point blow-ups $g \colon Z \to X$ such that $h = T \circ g$ is a morphism. It is a birational morphism of smooth projective surfaces, so a composite of point blow-ups by Theorem P5.7.12. Then $T = h \circ g^{-1}$ as rational maps. Read from $X$, this is first the sequence of blow-ups whose composite is $g$, from $X$ up to $Z$, and then the sequence of blow-downs whose composite is $h$, from $Z$ down to $Y$; each blow-down is the contraction of the exceptional curve of a blow-up, a $(-1)$-curve by Theorem P5.6.2. $\square$

*Corollary P5.7.14.* (1) By Theorem P5.7.12, $X \cong Y_n$ with $n \geq 1$, and the exceptional curve of $Y_n \to Y_{n-1}$ is a $(-1)$-curve on $X$ by Theorem P5.6.2, contracted by $f$.

(2) By Theorem P5.7.13 it is enough to compare $X$ with a blow-up of a point of $X$, and then the claim is parts 3 and 4 of Theorem P5.6.3: $h^i(\mathcal{O})$ does not change, $K^2$ drops by $1$ and $\rho$ rises by $1$.

(3) If $X$ contains a $(-1)$-curve, contract it by Theorem P5.7.4, to $X_1$ with $\rho(X_1) = \rho(X) - 1$ by part 2 of Corollary P5.7.5, and repeat. Every smooth projective surface has $\rho \geq 1$: for a very ample $H$, a hyperplane section $C \in |H|$ is a curve with $H \cdot C > 0$ by Lemma P5.5.20, so $H \not\equiv 0$. So the process stops after at most $\rho(X) - 1$ contractions, at a surface without $(-1)$-curves, and the composite of the contractions is a birational morphism.

(4) Induction on $n$, the case $n = 0$ being part 1 of Theorem P5.7.12. Let $\psi_1 \colon X \to X_1$ be the contraction of $C_1$ (Theorem P5.7.4), with $y_1 = \psi_1(C_1)$. By part 1 of Corollary P5.7.5, $f = f' \circ \psi_1$ for a morphism $f' \colon X_1 \to Y$, birational because $f$ and $\psi_1$ are. Since $C_2, \dots, C_n$ are disjoint from $C_1$, $\psi_1$ is an isomorphism on a neighbourhood of each of them, so $C'_i = \psi_1(C_i)$ are pairwise disjoint integral curves isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^1$, not passing through $y_1$. By part 1 of Theorem P5.6.3, $\psi_1^* C'_i = C_i + c E$ with $c = -C_i \cdot C_1 = 0$, so $C_i'^2 = C_i^2 = -1$ by part 2. An integral curve $B \subseteq X_1$ contracted by $f'$ has a strict transform contracted by $f$ and different from $C_1$, so $B$ is one of the $C'_i$; conversely each $C'_i$ is contracted by $f'$. By induction $f'$ is the blow-up of the distinct points $p_2, \dots, p_n$, $p_i = f'(C'_i) = f(C_i)$, with exceptional curves $C'_i$. The point $y_1$ lies on none of the $C'_i$, so $f'$ is an isomorphism near $y_1$, $p_1 = f'(y_1)$ is different from $p_2, \dots, p_n$, and $X = \operatorname{Bl}_{y_1} X_1$ is the blow-up of $p_1$ after the others. Since the centres lie over distinct points of $Y$, the blow-ups can be performed in any order. $\square$

3. (B) On the Hirzebruch surface $F_2$, with $C_-^2 = -2$ as in Example P5.7.8, show by the genus formula that $K \cdot C_- = 0$ and $K \cdot F = -2$, and that every integral curve $C \neq C_-$ has class $aC_- + bF$ with $a \geq 0$ and $b \geq 2a$. Deduce that $K \cdot C$ is even for every curve, that $F_2$ has no $(-1)$-curve, and, using part 1 of Corollary P5.7.14, that no birational morphism from $F_2$ onto a smooth projective surface contracts $C_-$.

(C) Let $T \colon \mathbb{P}^2 \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^2$ be a birational map given by a linear system $V \subseteq \Gamma(\mathcal{O}(d))$ without fixed component, and let $m_1, \dots, m_r$ be the multiplicities $m$ of the successive blow-ups in the proof of Theorem P5.7.9. ### Notes and history

Elimination of indeterminacy by blowing up base points goes back to Noether's treatment of plane Cremona transformations, and Noether's equations of Exercise 6 are the first constraint on such maps. Zariski proved the factorization of birational maps of smooth surfaces, Theorem P5.7.13, in the 1940s as part of his algebraic foundations of the theory of surfaces. The proofs above follow Hartshorne, Proposition V.5.3, Corollary V.5.4 and Theorem V.5.5, and Beauville, Chapter II, whose proof of elimination by the drop of $D^2$ is the one given here. The literature state is theorem. In higher dimension a birational map of smooth projective varieties over a field of characteristic $0$ factors into blow-ups and blow-downs along smooth centres, but with the two kinds of steps interleaved: this is the weak factorization theorem of Abramovich, Karu, Matsuki and Włodarczyk. The strong form, all blow-ups first, is open in dimension $3$ and higher. In the minimal model program, part 3 of Corollary P5.7.14 is the run of the program for smooth surfaces, and Example P5.7.18 shows that its output need not be unique when $K$ is not nef; Chapter 11 proves that it is unique when $X$ is not ruled.
