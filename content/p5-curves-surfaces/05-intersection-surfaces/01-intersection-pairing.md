## The intersection pairing

### Statement

In this chapter $k$ is algebraically closed and $S$ is a smooth projective surface over $k$. A *curve on $S$* is an effective divisor; by Corollary P1.9.26 the local rings of $S$ are factorial, so Weil and Cartier divisors agree and $\operatorname{Pic}(S) = \operatorname{Cl}(S)$ (Corollary P3.9.11). An effective divisor $C$ is identified with the closed subscheme it defines, with ideal sheaf $\mathcal{O}_S(-C)$, and $\mathcal{O}_C(D)$ denotes $\mathcal{O}_S(D)|_C$. For an integral curve $C$ with normalization $\nu$, and $\mathcal{L}$ invertible on $C$, $\deg \mathcal{L} = \deg \nu^* \mathcal{L}$, as in Theorem P5.3.10. The first two results produce the curves that the intersection pairing is computed on.

**Lemma P5.5.1 (twisting by a very ample sheaf).** Let $X$ be projective over $k$, $\mathcal{H}$ very ample and $\mathcal{G}$ invertible and generated by global sections. Then $\mathcal{G} \otimes \mathcal{H}$ is very ample. For every invertible $\mathcal{L}$ there is $n_0$ such that $\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n$ is very ample for all $n \geq n_0$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Lemma P5.5.2 (Bertini's theorem for surfaces).** Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{P}^N$ be a smooth projective surface, embedded as a closed subscheme. There is a nonempty open subset $U$ of the dual projective space $(\mathbb{P}^N)^\vee$ of hyperplanes such that for every $H \in U$, $S \cap H$ is an effective Cartier divisor on $S$ in the linear system $|\mathcal{O}_S(1)|$ that is smooth over $k$ of pure dimension $1$. If in addition $H^1(S, \mathcal{O}_S(-1)) = 0$, then $S \cap H$ is a smooth integral curve for every $H \in U$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Corollary P5.5.3 (smooth curves generate the Picard group).** Let $\mathcal{H}$ be very ample on $S$ and $\mathcal{L}$ invertible. For $n$ large, the linear systems $|\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n|$ and $|\mathcal{H}^n|$ contain smooth integral curves $A$ and $B$, and then $\mathcal{L} \cong \mathcal{O}_S(A - B)$. In particular every divisor on $S$ is linearly equivalent to a difference of two smooth integral curves.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Definition P5.5.4 (the intersection pairing).** For invertible sheaves $\mathcal{L}$ and $\mathcal{M}$ on $S$, the *intersection number* is
$$(\mathcal{L} \cdot \mathcal{M}) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) - \chi(\mathcal{L}^{-1}) - \chi(\mathcal{M}^{-1}) + \chi(\mathcal{L}^{-1} \otimes \mathcal{M}^{-1}).$$
For divisors $D$ and $E$, $D \cdot E = (\mathcal{O}_S(D) \cdot \mathcal{O}_S(E))$, and $D^2 = D \cdot D$ is the *self-intersection* of $D$. The number depends only on the classes of $D$ and $E$ in $\operatorname{Pic}(S)$.

**Proposition P5.5.5 (local intersection multiplicities).** Let $C$ and $D$ be curves on $S$ with no common component. Then $C \cap D$ is a finite set, and
$$C \cdot D = \sum_{P \in C \cap D} (C \cdot D)_P, \qquad (C \cdot D)_P = \dim_k \mathcal{O}_{S,P} / (f_P, g_P),$$
where $f_P$ and $g_P$ are local equations of $C$ and $D$ at $P$. Each $(C \cdot D)_P$ is a positive integer, and $(C \cdot D)_P = 1$ if and only if $f_P$ and $g_P$ generate the maximal ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{S,P}$, that is, $C$ and $D$ are smooth at $P$ with distinct tangent lines. In particular $C \cdot D \geq 0$.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Theorem P5.5.6 (the intersection pairing).** The intersection number is a symmetric bilinear form
$$\operatorname{Pic}(S) \times \operatorname{Pic}(S) \to \mathbb{Z}.$$
For every invertible $\mathcal{L}$ and every integral curve $C$ on $S$,
$$(\mathcal{L} \cdot \mathcal{O}_S(C)) = \deg (\mathcal{L}|_C).$$
It is the only bilinear form on $\operatorname{Pic}(S)$ with this property.

Lean: not in Lean as stated.

**Definition P5.5.7 (numerical equivalence).** A divisor $D$ is *numerically trivial*, written $D \equiv 0$, if $D \cdot E = 0$ for every divisor $E$. Divisors $D$ and $D'$ are *numerically equivalent* if $D - D' \equiv 0$. The quotient $\operatorname{Num}(S)$ of $\operatorname{Pic}(S)$ by the numerically trivial classes is a torsion-free abelian group on which the intersection pairing is nondegenerate.

### Proof in words

The Euler characteristic is additive in exact sequences. So a definition in terms of $\chi$ turns the exact sequence $0 \to \mathcal{O}(-C) \to \mathcal{O}_S \to \mathcal{O}_C \to 0$ into a formula for a restriction to $C$, and the Koszul complex of two local equations into a count of the points of $C \cap D$. Bilinearity comes from a single observation. The defect $s(\mathcal{L}_1, \mathcal{L}_2, \mathcal{M}) = (\mathcal{L}_1 \otimes \mathcal{L}_2 \cdot \mathcal{M}) - (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{M}) - (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdot \mathcal{M})$ is symmetric in its three arguments, and it vanishes when $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{O}(C)$ for an integral curve $C$, because the degree on $C$ is additive. Bertini's theorem writes every divisor as a difference of integral curves, and that finishes the proof. Bertini itself is a dimension count: the hyperplanes tangent to $S$ at some point form a family of dimension at most $N - 1$, one less than the dimension of the space of all hyperplanes.

### Examples

**Example P5.5.8 (Bézout's theorem).** On $S = \mathbb{P}^2$, $\operatorname{Pic}(S) = \mathbb{Z} \ell$ for the class $\ell$ of a line (Corollary P3.9.11). Two distinct lines meet transversally in one point, so $\ell^2 = 1$ by Proposition P5.5.5, and a curve $C$ of degree $d$ is linearly equivalent to $d \ell$. So for curves $C$ and $D$ of degrees $d$ and $e$ with no common component,
$$\sum_{P \in C \cap D} \dim_k \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2, P} / (f_P, g_P) = de.$$
The same computation from the definition: $\chi(\mathcal{O}(n)) = (n+1)(n+2)/2$ by Theorem P4.2.1, and
$$(\mathcal{O}(d) \cdot \mathcal{O}(e)) = 1 - \tbinom{2 - d}{2} - \tbinom{2 - e}{2} + \tbinom{2 - d - e}{2} = de,$$
with $\binom{m}{2} = m(m-1)/2$ for every integer $m$. This recovers Corollary P4.3.11.

**Example P5.5.9 (the quadric surface).** Let $S = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ with projections $p_1$, $p_2$, and let $F_1 = \{x\} \times \mathbb{P}^1$ and $F_2 = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \{y\}$, so that $\mathcal{O}(F_1) = p_1^* \mathcal{O}(1)$ and $\mathcal{O}(F_2) = p_2^* \mathcal{O}(1)$. Every divisor is linearly equivalent to $a F_1 + b F_2$ (Hartshorne, Example II.6.6.1), and the curve of a bihomogeneous form of bidegree $(a, b)$ has this class. Two fibres $\{x\} \times \mathbb{P}^1$ and $\{x'\} \times \mathbb{P}^1$ with $x \neq x'$ are disjoint, and $F_1$ and $F_2$ meet transversally at $(x, y)$. So $F_1^2 = F_2^2 = 0$ and $F_1 \cdot F_2 = 1$, and
$$(a F_1 + b F_2) \cdot (c F_1 + d F_2) = ad + bc.$$
The diagonal $\Delta$ of $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ has bidegree $(1, 1)$, so $\Delta^2 = 2$. In the Segre embedding as a quadric in $\mathbb{P}^3$ (Theorem P3.10.20), a plane section has class $F_1 + F_2$ and self-intersection $2$, the degree of the quadric.

**Example P5.5.10 (multiplicities of tangency).** In the affine chart $z \neq 0$ of $\mathbb{P}^2$, let $D$ be the line $y = 0$. The conic $C \colon y = x^2$ meets $D$ only at the origin, and
$$(C \cdot D)_0 = \dim_k k[x, y]_{(x, y)} / (y - x^2, y) = \dim_k k[x]_{(x)} / (x^2) = 2,$$
which is all of $C \cdot D = 2 \cdot 1$. The cuspidal cubic $y^2 z = x^3$ meets $D$ at the origin with multiplicity $\dim_k k[x]_{(x)} / (x^3) = 3$, which is all of $3 \cdot 1$. Its tangent line at the cusp, $y = 0$, meets it with multiplicity $3$, while a general line $y = cx$ through the cusp meets it with multiplicity $\dim_k k[x]_{(x)} / (c^2 x^2 - x^3) = 2$. In general $(C \cdot D)_P \geq m_P(C) \, m_P(D)$, with equality if and only if the tangent cones have no common line (Exercise 4).

**Example P5.5.11 (self-intersection and the normal bundle).** Let $C$ be an integral curve on $S$. By Theorem P5.5.6, $C^2 = \deg \mathcal{O}_C(C)$, the degree of the normal bundle $\mathcal{O}_C(C) = (\mathcal{I}_C / \mathcal{I}_C^2)^\vee$. For a line $L \subseteq \mathbb{P}^2$, $\mathcal{O}_L(L) = \mathcal{O}_L(1)$ has degree $1$. For the diagonal $\Delta \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$, the normal bundle is the tangent bundle $T_{\mathbb{P}^1} = \mathcal{O}(2)$, of degree $2 = \Delta^2$. The self-intersection is defined without moving $C$. If $C' \in |C|$ is a curve distinct from $C$, then $C'$ does not contain $C$, since $C' - C$ would be an effective divisor linearly equivalent to $0$, hence $0$. So $C$ and $C'$ have no common component, and $C^2 = C \cdot C' \geq 0$ by Proposition P5.5.5. A curve with $C^2 < 0$ is therefore the only member of its linear system.

### Proof

*Lemma P5.5.1.* By Theorem P3.6.21, the global sections of $\mathcal{G}$ give a morphism $\varphi \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^a$ with $\varphi^* \mathcal{O}(1) \cong \mathcal{G}$, and those of $\mathcal{H}$ give a closed immersion $\psi \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^b$ with $\psi^* \mathcal{O}(1) \cong \mathcal{H}$. The morphism $(\varphi, \psi) \colon X \to \mathbb{P}^a \times \mathbb{P}^b$ is the composite of the graph $X \to \mathbb{P}^a \times X$ of $\varphi$, a closed immersion because $\mathbb{P}^a$ is separated, and the closed immersion $\operatorname{id} \times \psi$. Composing with the Segre embedding $\sigma$ of Theorem P3.10.20 gives a closed immersion $X \to \mathbb{P}^{ab + a + b}$ with $(\varphi, \psi)^* \sigma^* \mathcal{O}(1) \cong \mathcal{G} \otimes \mathcal{H}$. So $\mathcal{G} \otimes \mathcal{H}$ is very ample.

For the second statement, view $\mathcal{L}$ as a coherent sheaf on $\mathbb{P}^b$ through $\psi$. By Theorem P3.6.23 there is $m$ such that $\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n$ is generated by global sections for all $n \geq m$. Then $\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^{n} = (\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^{n-1}) \otimes \mathcal{H}$ is very ample for $n \geq m + 1$ by the first part. $\square$

*Lemma P5.5.2.* Let $F_1, \dots, F_m$ generate the homogeneous ideal of $S$ in $k[X_0, \dots, X_N]$. For a point $x \in S$, fix a vector $\hat{x} \in k^{N+1}$ representing it, and let $J(x)$ be the $m \times (N + 1)$ matrix $(\partial F_j / \partial X_i (\hat{x}))$, with row space $R_x \subseteq k^{N+1}$.

*Step 1: $\dim R_x = N - 2$.* Suppose $x_0 \neq 0$ and $\hat{x} = (1, y)$. On the chart $X_0 \neq 0$ the ideal of $S$ is generated by $f_j(y) = F_j(1, y)$, with $\partial f_j / \partial y_i (y) = \partial F_j / \partial X_i (1, y)$ for $i \geq 1$. Since $S$ is smooth of dimension $2$ at $x$, the Jacobian criterion (Theorem P1.9.12) says that the columns $i \geq 1$ of $J(x)$ have rank $N - 2$. By Euler's identity $\sum_i X_i \, \partial F_j / \partial X_i = (\deg F_j) F_j$, which holds in every characteristic, $\sum_i \hat{x}_i \, \partial F_j / \partial X_i (\hat{x}) = 0$. So the column $i = 0$ is a combination of the others and $\operatorname{rank} J(x) = N - 2$.

*Step 2: the criterion.* Let $H = V_+(h)$ with $h = \sum_i a_i X_i$ and $a = (a_0, \dots, a_N) \neq 0$. If $a \in R_x$, then $h(\hat{x}) = a \cdot \hat{x} = 0$ by Euler's identity, so $x \in H$. If $H \supseteq S$, then $h$ lies in the ideal of $S$ in degree $1$, $h = \sum_j c_j F_j$ with $c_j$ homogeneous, and $a = \nabla h(\hat{x}) = \sum_j c_j(\hat{x}) \nabla F_j(\hat{x}) \in R_x$ for every $x \in S$. If $H \not\supseteq S$, then $h$ is a nonzerodivisor on the integral scheme $S$ locally, so $S \cap H$ is an effective Cartier divisor in $|\mathcal{O}_S(1)|$. It is nonempty: otherwise $S$ would lie in the affine space $\mathbb{P}^N \setminus H$, the affine coordinates would be global functions on $S$, constant by Example P3.4.27, and $S$ would be a point. By Krull's principal ideal theorem every irreducible component of $S \cap H$ has dimension $1$. Its ideal on the chart is generated by the $f_j$ and the dehomogenization of $h$, so by the Jacobian criterion again, with Step 1, $S \cap H$ is smooth at $x \in S \cap H$ if and only if $\operatorname{rank} \binom{J(x)}{a} = N - 1$, that is, $a \notin R_x$.

*Step 3: the dimension count.* Let
$$B = \{ (x, H) \in S \times (\mathbb{P}^N)^\vee : a \in R_x \}.$$
The condition $a \in R_x$ says that the $(m + 1) \times (N + 1)$ matrix with rows $J(x)$ and $a$ has rank at most $N - 2$, that is, all its minors of size $N - 1$ vanish. These minors are bihomogeneous polynomials in $(\hat{x}, a)$, so $B$ is closed. The fibre of $B \to S$ over $x$ is the projective space $\mathbb{P}(R_x) \cong \mathbb{P}^{N - 3}$. Let $B'$ be an irreducible component of $B$, with the reduced structure, and $Z \subseteq S$ the closure of its image. Each fibre of $B' \to Z$ over a closed point lies in a fibre of $B \to S$, so has dimension at most $N - 3$, and by Theorem P3.7.6, $\dim B' - \dim Z \leq N - 3$. So $\dim B \leq 2 + N - 3 = N - 1$. The projection $S \times (\mathbb{P}^N)^\vee \to (\mathbb{P}^N)^\vee$ is closed, because $S$ is projective, so the image $\pi(B)$ is a closed subset of dimension at most $N - 1$. Let $U$ be its complement, nonempty. By Step 2, for $H \in U$, $H \not\supseteq S$ and $S \cap H$ is smooth of pure dimension $1$.

*Step 4: connectedness.* Let $H \in U$ and $C = S \cap H$. The sequence $0 \to \mathcal{O}_S(-1) \to \mathcal{O}_S \to \mathcal{O}_C \to 0$ gives an exact sequence $H^0(\mathcal{O}_S) \to H^0(\mathcal{O}_C) \to H^1(\mathcal{O}_S(-1))$, and $H^0(\mathcal{O}_S) = k$ because $S$ is integral and projective (Example P3.4.27). If $H^1(\mathcal{O}_S(-1)) = 0$, then $h^0(\mathcal{O}_C) = 1$, so $C$ is connected: a disconnected $C$ has $H^0(\mathcal{O}_C) \supseteq k \times k$. The local rings of $C$ are regular, hence domains (Corollary P1.9.3). Two irreducible components of $C$ through a point would give two minimal primes of its local ring, so the components of $C$ are disjoint, and connectedness leaves one. So $C$ is integral. $\square$

*Corollary P5.5.3.* By Lemma P5.5.1, $\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n$ and $\mathcal{H}^n$ are very ample for $n$ large. By Corollary P4.7.21 and Theorem P4.2.7,
$$H^1(\mathcal{L}^{-1} \otimes \mathcal{H}^{-n}) \cong H^1(\omega_S \otimes \mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n)' = 0, \qquad H^1(\mathcal{H}^{-n}) \cong H^1(\omega_S \otimes \mathcal{H}^n)' = 0$$
for $n$ large. Embed $S$ by the complete linear system of $\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n$, so that $\mathcal{O}_S(1) = \mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n$. Lemma P5.5.2 gives a smooth integral curve $A \in |\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n|$, and in the same way a smooth integral $B \in |\mathcal{H}^n|$. Then $\mathcal{O}_S(A - B) \cong \mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{H}^n \otimes \mathcal{H}^{-n} = \mathcal{L}$. $\square$

*Proposition P5.5.5.* A common point of $C$ and $D$ lies on an irreducible component of each. The components are distinct curves, and two distinct integral curves on a surface meet in a closed subset of dimension $0$, so $C \cap D$ is finite. At $P \in C \cap D$, the ring $\mathcal{O}_{S,P}$ is factorial (Corollary P1.9.26), and $f_P$ and $g_P$ have no common prime factor because $C$ and $D$ have no common component through $P$. So $g_P$ is a nonzerodivisor modulo $f_P$: if $g_P h \in (f_P)$, each prime factor of $f_P$ divides $h$, so $f_P$ divides $h$. Thus $f_P, g_P$ is a regular sequence, and the Koszul complex (Lemma P1.9.20) gives the exact sequence of sheaves
$$0 \to \mathcal{O}_S(-C - D) \xrightarrow{(g, -f)} \mathcal{O}_S(-C) \oplus \mathcal{O}_S(-D) \xrightarrow{(f, g)} \mathcal{O}_S \to \mathcal{O}_{C \cap D} \to 0,$$
where $C \cap D$ is the closed subscheme with ideal $\mathcal{O}_S(-C) + \mathcal{O}_S(-D)$. Exactness is local: away from $C \cap D$ one of the local equations is a unit and the sequence is split exact, and at points of $C \cap D$ it is the Koszul complex of $f_P, g_P$. By additivity of $\chi$ (Proposition P4.3.2),
$$\chi(\mathcal{O}_{C \cap D}) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) - \chi(\mathcal{O}_S(-C)) - \chi(\mathcal{O}_S(-D)) + \chi(\mathcal{O}_S(-C - D)) = C \cdot D.$$
The sheaf $\mathcal{O}_{C \cap D}$ is supported on a finite set, so it has no higher cohomology, and $\chi(\mathcal{O}_{C \cap D}) = h^0 = \sum_P \dim_k \mathcal{O}_{S,P} / (f_P, g_P)$. Each term is positive because $f_P, g_P$ lie in the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_P$. It equals $1$ if and only if $(f_P, g_P) = \mathfrak{m}_P$. That holds if and only if the images of $f_P$ and $g_P$ in the plane $\mathfrak{m}_P / \mathfrak{m}_P^2$ are linearly independent. By Proposition P1.9.4 this says that $C$ and $D$ are smooth at $P$, with tangent lines, the kernels of these images on the tangent plane, that are distinct. $\square$

*Theorem P5.5.6.* Symmetry is clear from the definition. Let $C$ be an integral curve and $\mathcal{L}$ invertible. Tensoring $0 \to \mathcal{O}_S(-C) \to \mathcal{O}_S \to \mathcal{O}_C \to 0$ with $\mathcal{O}_S$ and with $\mathcal{L}^{-1}$ gives
$$\chi(\mathcal{O}_C) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_S) - \chi(\mathcal{O}_S(-C)), \qquad \chi(\mathcal{L}^{-1}|_C) = \chi(\mathcal{L}^{-1}) - \chi(\mathcal{L}^{-1}(-C)),$$
so that
$$(\mathcal{L} \cdot \mathcal{O}_S(C)) = \chi(\mathcal{O}_C) - \chi(\mathcal{L}^{-1}|_C).$$
By Theorem P5.3.10 on the integral curve $C$, $\chi(\mathcal{O}_C) = 1 - p_a(C)$ and $\chi(\mathcal{L}^{-1}|_C) = -\deg(\mathcal{L}|_C) + 1 - p_a(C)$. So $(\mathcal{L} \cdot \mathcal{O}_S(C)) = \deg(\mathcal{L}|_C)$.

For bilinearity, put $s(\mathcal{L}_1, \mathcal{L}_2, \mathcal{M}) = (\mathcal{L}_1 \otimes \mathcal{L}_2 \cdot \mathcal{M}) - (\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{M}) - (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdot \mathcal{M})$. Expanding the definition,
$$s = -\chi(\mathcal{O}_S) + \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{-1}) + \chi(\mathcal{L}_2^{-1}) + \chi(\mathcal{M}^{-1}) - \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{-1} \mathcal{L}_2^{-1}) - \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{-1} \mathcal{M}^{-1}) - \chi(\mathcal{L}_2^{-1} \mathcal{M}^{-1}) + \chi(\mathcal{L}_1^{-1} \mathcal{L}_2^{-1} \mathcal{M}^{-1}),$$
writing products for tensor products. This is symmetric in $\mathcal{L}_1, \mathcal{L}_2, \mathcal{M}$. If $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{O}_S(C)$ with $C$ integral, then $s = \deg(\mathcal{L}_1 \mathcal{L}_2|_C) - \deg(\mathcal{L}_1|_C) - \deg(\mathcal{L}_2|_C) = 0$, because $\nu^*$ commutes with tensor products and degree on a smooth curve is additive. By symmetry $s(\mathcal{N}, \mathcal{O}_S(C), \mathcal{M}) = 0$ for all $\mathcal{N}$, $\mathcal{M}$ and integral $C$, that is,
$$(\mathcal{N}(C) \cdot \mathcal{M}) = (\mathcal{N} \cdot \mathcal{M}) + (C \cdot \mathcal{M}). \tag{$*$}$$
Now let $\mathcal{L}_1, \mathcal{L}_2, \mathcal{M}$ be arbitrary. By Corollary P5.5.3, $\mathcal{L}_1 \cong \mathcal{O}_S(A - B)$ with $A$ and $B$ integral. Apply $(*)$ with $\mathcal{N} = \mathcal{L}_2(-B)$ and the curves $A$ and $B$:
$$(\mathcal{L}_1 \mathcal{L}_2 \cdot \mathcal{M}) = (\mathcal{N} \cdot \mathcal{M}) + (A \cdot \mathcal{M}), \qquad (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdot \mathcal{M}) = (\mathcal{N} \cdot \mathcal{M}) + (B \cdot \mathcal{M}).$$
So $(\mathcal{L}_1 \mathcal{L}_2 \cdot \mathcal{M}) - (\mathcal{L}_2 \cdot \mathcal{M}) = (A \cdot \mathcal{M}) - (B \cdot \mathcal{M})$. Taking $\mathcal{L}_2 = \mathcal{O}_S$ identifies the right side with $(\mathcal{L}_1 \cdot \mathcal{M})$, because $(\mathcal{O}_S \cdot \mathcal{M}) = 0$ from the definition. So $s = 0$ always, and the pairing is additive in the first variable, hence bilinear by symmetry.

Uniqueness: a bilinear form is determined by its values on generators, and the classes $\mathcal{O}_S(C)$ of integral curves generate $\operatorname{Pic}(S)$ by Corollary P5.5.3. If $(\mathcal{L} \cdot \mathcal{O}_S(C)) = \deg(\mathcal{L}|_C)$ is prescribed, the form is determined. $\square$

*Definition P5.5.7.* The numerically trivial classes form a subgroup by bilinearity, and the pairing descends to $\operatorname{Num}(S)$, where by construction only $0$ pairs to zero with everything. If $nD \equiv 0$ with $n \neq 0$, then $n (D \cdot E) = 0$ for all $E$, so $D \equiv 0$, and $\operatorname{Num}(S)$ is torsion-free. $\square$

### Exercises

1. (A) Compute $\chi(\mathcal{O}(a, b))$ on $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ by the Künneth formula, and check $(\mathcal{O}(a, b) \cdot \mathcal{O}(c, d)) = ad + bc$ directly from Definition P5.5.4.
2. (A) Show that $(\mathcal{O}_S \cdot \mathcal{M}) = 0$ and $(\mathcal{L}^{-1} \cdot \mathcal{M}) = -(\mathcal{L} \cdot \mathcal{M})$ from the definition and Theorem P5.5.6, and that $D^2 = (-D)^2$.
3. (B) Let $C, D \subseteq \mathbb{P}^2$ be the curves $y^2 z = x^3$ and $y^2 z = x^3 + x^2 z$. Find $C \cap D$ and all local multiplicities, and check that they add up to $9$.
4. (B) Let $P \in S$ and $C$, $D$ curves through $P$ with no common component, of multiplicities $m$ and $n$ at $P$. Show that $(C \cdot D)_P \geq mn$, with equality if and only if the tangent cones of $C$ and $D$ at $P$ have no common line. Use the filtration of $\mathcal{O}_{S,P}$ by powers of $\mathfrak{m}_P$ and the case of $k[x, y]$ first.
5. (B) Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{P}^3$ be a smooth surface of degree $d$ and $H$ a plane section. Show that $H^2 = d$. If $L \subseteq S$ is a line, show that $H \cdot L = 1$.
6. (C) Let $f \colon S' \to S$ be a finite surjective morphism of smooth projective surfaces of degree $n$. Show that $f^* D \cdot f^* E = n \, (D \cdot E)$. First take $D$ and $E$ to be smooth curves in general position, as in Corollary P5.5.3, and use the generic freeness of $f_* \mathcal{O}_{S'}$.

### Lean

Mathlib has neither the intersection pairing on a surface nor Bertini's theorem. It has the length of a module (`Module.length`), which is the local intersection multiplicity $\dim_k \mathcal{O}_{S,P} / (f, g)$ over an algebraically closed field. The handbook adds no `MinimalModel` node here.

### Notes and history

Intersection multiplicities of plane curves go back to Newton and Bézout. Weil gave the first general algebraic definition in *Foundations of algebraic geometry* (1946), and Serre's Tor formula of 1958 is the definition used in higher dimension. The definition through Euler characteristics is due to Mumford and appears in Beauville's book; Hartshorne instead proves the existence of the pairing from Bertini's theorem, as in the uniqueness part of Theorem P5.5.6. The literature state is theorem. Bertini proved the theorem that bears his name over $\mathbb{C}$ in 1882. The algebraic proof above, by a dimension count of tangent hyperplanes, works in every characteristic. The statement for general members of a linear system with base points needs characteristic $0$, and Zariski gave counterexamples in characteristic $p$. In the minimal model program, Bertini's theorem in this form produces the general hyperplane sections along which adjunction and induction on dimension proceed, and the intersection pairing becomes the pairing between $N^1(X)$ and $N_1(X)$ of Volume 1.
